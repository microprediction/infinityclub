What the evidence says about learning maths

A running list of studies on what helps children learn mathematics. The bar for inclusion is a research design that can actually tell cause from coincidence: a randomised trial, a meta-analysis that pools many trials, or a natural experiment with a credible comparison group. Opinion pieces, case studies and surveys of teachers are not here, however sensible they sound. Null results are included when the study was good, because knowing what does not work matters as much.

How to read an entry

  • The tag says the design. RCT means children, classes or schools were randomly assigned; Experiment means the same within a classroom study, often with each student doing both conditions. Synthesis is a meta-analysis or an evidence review that pools studies and reports an overall estimate. Quasi-experiment means no lottery, but a comparison that is nearly as good, such as a cut-off rule. Correlational means the study shows an association only, and is here because it is large and often cited.
  • Effect sizes are in standard deviations unless stated. As a rough guide, 0.1 is small but real at scale, 0.2 to 0.4 is a solid classroom effect, 0.8 is large and rare outside the laboratory. On US national tests, a year of maths learning is about 0.4 SD in grade 6 and 0.3 SD in grades 7 and 8 (Hill, Bloom, Black & Lipsey, 2008), so an intervention of 0.2 SD is several months of progress.
  • Lab versus classroom. Effects found in a one-hour laboratory session with undergraduates are routinely smaller when tried over a term with schoolchildren. Entries say which setting they come from.
  • Links go to the paper via its DOI. Most are paywalled, but the abstract is always free, and many have a free preprint a search away.

How to practise: retrieval, spacing, interleaving

The best-replicated results in all of learning science. Testing yourself beats rereading, spreading practice out beats cramming, and mixing problem types beats doing twenty of the same kind in a row. All three have been shown in real maths classrooms, not just the lab.

Large classroom RCT of interleaved maths practice (grade 7)

Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., Hartwig, M. K., & Cheung, C.-N. (2020). A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice. Journal of Educational Psychology, 112(1), 40–52. doi:10.1037/edu0000367

RCT Preregistered cluster RCT (classes randomised). Who: 787 students analysed (856 enrolled), 54 classes, 15 teachers, 5 middle schools, grade 7, one Florida school district, USA.

Finding. Four months of mostly interleaved (mixed problem types) or mostly blocked assignments, then an interleaved review for all. On an unannounced test one month later the interleaved group scored 61% versus 38%, d = 0.83. Positive for every one of the 15 teachers (ds 0.23 to 1.48).

One district; researcher-written test; one-month delay; teachers knew their condition.

Interleaved practice over three months (grade 7)

Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., & Stershic, S. (2015). Interleaved practice improves mathematics learning. Journal of Educational Psychology, 107(3), 900–908. doi:10.1037/edu0000001

Experiment Classroom experiment, within-subjects (each student learned some skills by interleaved and some by blocked practice). Who: n = 126, grade 7, Hillsborough County Public Schools, Florida, USA.

Finding. Same problems, different arrangement, over three months, then a review and an unannounced test 1 or 30 days later. Interleaved practice beat blocked practice on both tests: d = 0.42 at one day and d = 0.79 at 30 days.

Single school; researcher-designed materials and test; the benefit grew with delay, so short-delay studies will understate it.

Interleaving helps even for dissimilar problem types (grade 7)

Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., & Burgess, K. (2014). The benefit of interleaved mathematics practice is not limited to superficially similar kinds of problems. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 21(5), 1323–1330. doi:10.3758/s13423-014-0588-3

Experiment Classroom experiment (interleaved versus blocked practice). Who: n = 140, grade 7, USA (school setting).

Finding. Nine weeks of blocked or interleaved practice, then an unannounced test two weeks later. Interleaved 72% versus blocked 38%, d = 1.05. Earlier studies used near-identical problem types; here the types looked different from one another, and the benefit still appeared.

Two-week delay only; researcher-written test; same research group as the other interleaving studies.

Spaced practice in Year 7 maths lessons, and overconfidence after massing

Emeny, W. G., Hartwig, M. K., & Rohrer, D. (2021). Spaced mathematics practice improves test scores and reduces overconfidence. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 35(4), 1082–1089. doi:10.1002/acp.3814

Experiment Two within-subjects classroom experiments (each student massed one topic and spaced the other). Who: Experiment 1 n = 44, Experiment 2 n = 55; Year 7 (ages 11–12); one large school in the United Kingdom.

Finding. Twelve practice problems in one session or spread over three weekly sessions; test one month later. Spacing raised scores: d = 0.61 (95% CI 0.28–0.92) and d = 0.39 (0.11–0.66). Students' predicted scores were roughly accurate after spaced practice but badly overconfident after massed practice.

Small samples, one school, two non-curriculum topics (Venn diagrams, permutations).

Spaced practice in grade 3 and grade 7 classrooms

Barzagar Nazari, K., & Ebersbach, M. (2019). Distributing mathematical practice of third and seventh graders: Applicability of the spacing effect in the classroom. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 33(2), 288–298. doi:10.1002/acp.3485

Experiment Classroom experiment, massed versus distributed practice, Bayesian analysis. Who: N = 213, grades 3 and 7, school classrooms (authors based in Germany).

Finding. After a curriculum lesson, three practice sets either all in one day or one per day over three days. Tests one and six weeks later: grade 7 did better after distributed practice at both delays; grade 3 only at one week.

Effect sizes not given in the abstract; the benefit for younger children was less durable.

Spacing versus overlearning for a maths procedure (college)

Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2006). The effects of overlearning and distributed practise on the retention of mathematics knowledge. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 20, 1209–1224. doi:10.1002/acp.1266

RCT Two randomised lab experiments. Who: n = 216 college students (across both experiments), USA.

Finding. Students learned a maths procedure and were tested one or four weeks later. Splitting ten practice problems across two sessions instead of one roughly doubled scores on the four-week test. Doing extra problems in the same session ("overlearning") had a negligible effect on retention.

Lab study with undergraduates and a single procedure; exact scores not given in the abstract in the record.

Shuffled versus blocked practice problems (college)

Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. Instructional Science, 35(6), 481–498. doi:10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8

RCT Two randomised lab experiments. Who: Experiment 1 n = 66, Experiment 2 n = 18; undergraduates, University of South Florida, USA.

Finding. Experiment 1 (permutations): spaced practice 74% versus massed 49% on a test one week later. Experiment 2 (volumes of four solids): mixed practice 63% versus blocked 20%, d = 1.34, even though the blocked group did better during practice (89% versus 60%). Practice that feels easier produced worse test results.

Experiment 2 had only 18 students; one-week delay; lab setting.

Spaced retrieval practice in a precalculus course

Hopkins, R. F., Lyle, K. B., Hieb, J. L., & Ralston, P. A. S. (2016). Spaced retrieval practice increases college students' short- and long-term retention of mathematics knowledge. Educational Psychology Review, 28(4), 853–873. doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9349-8

Experiment Classroom experiment; spaced versus massed quiz questions manipulated both within and between students. Who: Engineering students in a precalculus course (n not given in the abstract), USA; a subset followed into calculus the next semester.

Finding. Content quizzed at spaced intervals through the semester was retained better than content quizzed only soon after the lesson. Students given some spaced retrieval practice did better on the precalculus final and on exams in the following calculus course than those whose practice was all massed.

Effect sizes not given in the abstract. Follow-up (Lyle et al., 2020, same journal, 32(1), 277–295): across-semester retention improved only by spacing, not by extra practice.

Retrieval practice versus worked examples for learning a maths procedure

Yeo, D. J., & Fazio, L. K. (2019). The optimal learning strategy depends on learning goals and processes: Retrieval practice versus worked examples. Journal of Educational Psychology, 111(1), 73–90. doi:10.1037/edu0000268

RCT Three randomised lab experiments. Who: n not given in the abstract; students learning a novel maths procedure, USA.

Finding. For a new maths procedure, repeatedly studying worked examples beat repeated testing on an immediate test. After one week, testing matched studying when practice problems varied, and beat it when they were identical. For memorising the text of an example, testing won.

A boundary condition on the testing effect; the advantage of retrieval depends on delay and material. Lab study.

Null result: no spacing effect for a maths procedure (university)

Ebersbach, M., & Barzagar Nazari, K. (2020). No robust effect of distributed practice on the short- and long-term retention of mathematical procedures. Frontiers in Psychology, 11, 811. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00811

RCT Randomised experiment, 3 (gap between practice sessions: 0, 1 or 11 days) x 2 (test after 1 or 5 weeks), between subjects. Who: n = 235 university students (mean age about 24), Germany.

Finding. Students learned permutation problems. No main effect of spacing and no interaction with retention interval; scores fell between one and five weeks regardless of schedule.

Conflicts with the same authors' school result and with Rohrer and Taylor (2006) on the same topic; authors suggest spacing matters less for procedures than for facts.

Meta-analysis of interleaving: smaller effect for maths than for pictures

Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. Psychological Bulletin, 145(11), 1029–1052. doi:10.1037/bul0000209

Synthesis Multilevel meta-analysis of 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples. Who: Mixed lab and classroom samples; all ages.

Finding. Overall interleaving effect g = 0.42 versus blocked practice. For mathematical tasks the effect was small, g = 0.34; for paintings g = 0.67; for expository texts no reliable effect; for word lists blocking was better (g = -0.39). Interleaving helped most when categories were similar to each other and material was complex.

Many included studies are lab-based with short delays; the maths estimate pools varied tasks.

Meta-analysis of classroom quizzing, with a maths estimate

Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin, 147(4), 399–435. doi:10.1037/bul0000309

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 222 independent classroom studies. Who: 48,478 students, elementary school to university, many countries.

Finding. Classroom quizzing raised achievement by g = 0.499 overall. For mathematics/statistics (27 effects) g = 0.433, 95% CI 0.193–0.674. Subject area did not significantly moderate the effect (p = .69). Effects were larger with corrective feedback and repeated quizzes.

Classroom studies vary in rigour; the maths estimate has a wide interval; control conditions differ across studies.

Review rating practice testing and spacing as high utility

Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. doi:10.1177/1529100612453266

Synthesis Narrative evidence review (no quantitative pooling) of ten learning techniques. Who: Learners of all ages, lab and classroom studies.

Finding. Practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility. Interleaved practice, elaborative interrogation and self-explanation were rated moderate. Summarising, highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery for text and rereading were rated low.

Expert ratings, not effect sizes; the interleaving rating predates most of the maths classroom RCTs above.

IES practice guide on spacing, quizzing and worked examples

Pashler, H., Bain, P. M., Bottge, B. A., Graesser, A., Koedinger, K., McDaniel, M., & Metcalfe, J. (2007). Organizing instruction and study to improve student learning. IES practice guide. Washington, DC: National Center for Education Research, Institute of Education Sciences, U.S. Department of Education. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1

Synthesis Government evidence synthesis with graded recommendations. Who: K–12 and college learners; not maths-specific.

Finding. Evidence ratings: "space learning over time" moderate; "interleave worked example solutions with problem-solving exercises" moderate; "use quizzes to re-expose students to key content" strong; "ask deep explanatory questions" strong; quizzes to identify gaps, and pre-questions, minimal.

Written in 2007, before the classroom interleaving trials; panel judgement, not pooled effect sizes.

How to teach: worked examples, explicit instruction, discovery

For a novice, studying a worked example beats solving the same problem unaided, and explicit teaching beats unguided discovery. Guided discovery, where the struggle comes before the explanation, can work. Manipulatives help, most when the teacher guides their use closely and fades them out.

Explicit instruction and heuristics for students with maths learning disabilities

Gersten, R., Chard, D. J., Jayanthi, M., Baker, S. K., Morphy, P., & Flojo, J. (2009). Mathematics instruction for students with learning disabilities: A meta-analysis of instructional components. Review of Educational Research, 79(3), 1202–1242. doi:10.3102/0034654309334431

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 42 intervention studies (RCTs and high-quality quasi-experiments). Who: School-age students with learning disabilities; mostly US; total n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Most instructional components had significant positive mean effects, ranging from 0.21 to 1.56. Explicit, step-by-step teacher modelling (g = 1.22, 11 studies) and teaching heuristic problem-solving strategies (g = 1.56, 4 studies) were the two components that mattered most in the regression.

Population is students with LD; component-level effect sizes confirmed only from a secondary summary (Bryant, 2009), the abstract gives the 0.21–1.56 range.

Which maths programmes work: curricula vs software vs teaching methods (K–12)

Slavin, R. E., & Lake, C. (2008). Effective programs in elementary mathematics: A best-evidence synthesis. Review of Educational Research, 78(3), 427–515. Slavin, R. E., Lake, C., & Groff, C. (2009). Effective programs in middle and high school mathematics: A best-evidence synthesis. Review of Educational Research, 79(2), 839–911. doi:10.3102/0034654308317473 · doi:10.3102/0034654308330968

RCT Best-evidence syntheses; 87 studies (36 randomised) for elementary, 100 studies (26 randomised) for grades 6–12; minimum 12-week duration, independent outcome measures. Who: K–12 students, mainly US.

Finding. Elementary: median ES +0.10 for textbooks/curricula (13 studies), +0.19 for computer-assisted instruction (38), +0.33 for instructional-process programmes such as cooperative learning and classroom management (36). Secondary (report version): weighted mean ES +0.03 curricula, +0.10 CAI, +0.18 instructional process, +0.42 for cooperative learning (9 studies).

Many small or matched (non-random) studies; figures above are from the authors' full report versions, which may differ slightly from the journal text.

Concrete manipulatives vs symbols only

Carbonneau, K. J., Marley, S. C., & Selig, J. P. (2013). A meta-analysis of the efficacy of teaching mathematics with concrete manipulatives. Journal of Educational Psychology, 105(2), 380–400. doi:10.1037/a0031084

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 55 studies comparing manipulative-based instruction with abstract-symbol-only instruction. Who: N = 7,237 students, kindergarten to college.

Finding. Aggregated mean effect d = 0.37 (95% CI 0.30–0.44) in favour of manipulatives. Larger for retention (k = 53) than for problem solving, transfer or justification (small effects). Effects were larger with high instructional guidance (d = 0.46 vs 0.29), for fractions (0.69) than arithmetic (0.27) or algebra (0.21), and in shorter interventions.

Heterogeneous; within-subject and published studies gave much larger effects than randomised ones; manipulatives work best when the teacher guides their use, not as free play.

Direct Instruction programmes, 50 years of studies

Stockard, J., Wood, T. W., Coughlin, C., & Rasplica Khoury, C. (2018). The effectiveness of Direct Instruction curricula: A meta-analysis of a half century of research. Review of Educational Research, 88(4), 479–507. doi:10.3102/0034654317751919

Synthesis Meta-analysis (mixed models) of 328 studies, 413 designs, 3,999 effects, 1966–2016. Who: Mostly US school students; maths subset 70 studies, 685 effects.

Finding. Overall effect 0.54 (95% CI 0.49–0.59); for mathematics 0.55 (0.46–0.65), against comparison instruction. Effects were larger with more years of exposure and 60+ minutes daily, and declined little after the programme ended.

Covers scripted DI curricula (e.g. Connecting Math Concepts), not "explicit teaching" generally; many older quasi-experimental studies; authors associated with the DI research community.

IES practice guide: helping primary pupils who struggle with maths

Fuchs, L. S., Bucka, N., Clarke, B., Dougherty, B., Jordan, N. C., Karp, K. S., & Woodward, J. (2021). Assisting students struggling with mathematics: Intervention in the elementary grades (WWC 2021006). Institute of Education Sciences, What Works Clearinghouse. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/26

Synthesis Government evidence synthesis (WWC practice guide) with graded recommendations. Who: Elementary (K–6) students needing intervention; US studies.

Finding. Six recommendations, all rated strong evidence: systematic (explicit, sequenced) instruction; precise mathematical language; concrete and semi-concrete representations; number lines; targeted word-problem instruction; regular timed fluency activities.

Aimed at intervention for struggling pupils, not whole-class teaching of advanced children; effect sizes are in the underlying study tables, not in the summary.

Worked examples with self-explanation in real algebra classes (AlgebraByExample)

Booth, J. L., Oyer, M. H., Paré-Blagoev, E. J., Elliot, A. J., Barbieri, C., Augustine, A., & Koedinger, K. R. (2015). Learning algebra by example in real-world classrooms. Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness, 8(4), 530–551. See also Booth, J. L., Lange, K. E., Koedinger, K. R., & Newton, K. J. (2013). Using example problems to improve student learning in algebra. Learning and Instruction, 25, 24–34. doi:10.1080/19345747.2015.1055636

RCT Two RCTs (students randomised within classrooms); 2013 paper adds two smaller in-vivo experiments. Who: Exp. 1 n = 56 Algebra I students, 3 classrooms; Exp. 2 n = 425 students, 28 classes, 5 US districts.

Finding. Replacing some practice problems with correct and incorrect worked examples to explain raised posttest scores in Exp. 1 (ηp² = .10, p = .03). In Exp. 2 there was no overall main effect, but a significant condition × prior-knowledge interaction: lower-prior-knowledge students gained more. The 2013 study (n = 134) found higher conceptual scores (63% vs 58%) with fewer practice problems.

Small effects; the larger trial's benefit was confined to weaker students.

Comparing two solution methods side by side

Rittle-Johnson, B., & Star, J. R. (2007). Does comparing solution methods facilitate conceptual and procedural knowledge? An experimental study on learning to solve equations. Journal of Educational Psychology, 99(3), 561–574. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.561

RCT Randomised experiment, classroom-based. Who: n = 70 seventh-graders; country not given in the abstract (US authors).

Finding. Students who studied worked examples of two different methods side by side and compared them made greater gains in procedural knowledge and flexibility than students who studied the same examples one at a time; conceptual gains were comparable.

Short intervention, single topic (linear equations); effect sizes not given in the abstract in the record; later studies by the same group found comparison only helps students who already know one method.

Worked examples instead of problem solving (the original algebra experiments)

Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra. Cognition and Instruction, 2(1), 59–89. Replicated in class by Carroll, W. M. (1994). Using worked examples as an instructional support in the algebra classroom. Journal of Educational Psychology, 86(3), 360–367. doi:10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3 · doi:10.1037/0022-0663.86.3.360

Experiment Five lab experiments (Sweller & Cooper); two classroom experiments (Carroll). Who: Year 9, Year 11 and university maths students; 74 US high-school students including a remedial class (Carroll).

Finding. Studying worked examples took far less time than solving equivalent problems, and students then solved similar problems faster with fewer errors. Carroll: worked-example students outperformed controls on posttests after fewer practice problems, with fewer errors and less teacher help.

Gains were specific to problems with the same structure; small samples; effect sizes not given in the abstract.

Concreteness fading: start concrete, end abstract

Fyfe, E. R., McNeil, N. M., & Borjas, S. (2015). Benefits of "concreteness fading" for children's mathematics understanding. Learning and Instruction, 35, 104–120. Background review: Fyfe, E. R., McNeil, N. M., Son, J. Y., & Goldstone, R. L. (2014). Concreteness fading in mathematics and science instruction: A systematic review. Educational Psychology Review, 26(1), 9–25. doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2014.10.004

Synthesis Randomised experiments (2015); narrative systematic review without pooling (2014). Who: Elementary-school children learning mathematical equivalence (e.g. 3 + 4 + 5 = 3 + __); n not given in the abstracts fetched.

Finding. Children taught with a progression from puppets and objects, to pictures, to symbols solved more transfer problems than children given the same materials in reverse order or concrete-only/abstract-only teaching.

Full abstract and effect sizes could not be retrieved (publisher blocked); study details confirmed from the first author's later open-access paper. Short lab-style sessions.

Unassisted discovery learning does not help; guided discovery does

Alfieri, L., Brooks, P. J., Aldrich, N. J., & Tenenbaum, H. R. (2011). Does discovery-based instruction enhance learning? Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(1), 1–18. doi:10.1037/a0021017

Synthesis Two meta-analyses, 164 studies in total. Who: Learners of all ages across subjects (maths, science, problem solving); total n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d = −0.38 for discovery). But "enhanced" discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or prompts to explain beat other kinds of instruction (d = 0.30).

Not maths-specific; wide variety of tasks and outcome measures; "discovery" defined broadly.

Productive failure: trying problems before being taught

Sinha, T., & Kapur, M. (2021). When problem solving followed by instruction works: Evidence for productive failure. Review of Educational Research, 91(5), 761–798. doi:10.3102/00346543211019105

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 53 studies, 166 comparisons. Who: School and university learners, largely maths and science topics; total n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Problem solving before instruction beat instruction before problem solving, g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20–0.51); g = 0.37–0.58 when implemented faithfully to Productive Failure design. The effect reversed for grades 2–5 and for domain-general skills, where teach-first was better.

Second author is the originator of Productive Failure; the publication-bias-adjusted estimate (g = 0.87) runs the unusual direction; few young-child studies.

Prompting students to explain to themselves

Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 30(3), 703–725. doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 64 studies, 69 effect sizes. Who: Learners across ages and subjects, including many maths tasks; total n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Asking learners to explain material or worked steps to themselves improved learning, random-effects g = 0.55 versus no prompts; the effect held across 20 moderators.

Not maths-specific; mostly short lab or computer-based studies.

Mastery learning

Kulik, C.-L. C., Kulik, J. A., & Bangert-Drowns, R. L. (1990). Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 60(2), 265–299. doi:10.3102/00346543060002265

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations. Who: Upper-elementary, high-school and college students, various subjects; total n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Mastery programmes (learn a unit, test, re-teach until a criterion is met) raised exam performance, with larger gains for weaker students; they also improved attitudes but increased time on task, and self-paced versions lowered college completion rates.

Overall mean effect size is not given (often cited as about 0.5 SD, unverified here); Slavin's 1987 best-evidence review found much smaller effects on standardised tests.

IES practice guide: problem solving in grades 4–8

Woodward, J., Beckmann, S., Driscoll, M., Franke, M., Herzig, P., Jitendra, A., Koedinger, K. R., & Ogbuehi, P. (2012). Improving mathematical problem solving in grades 4 through 8. Institute of Education Sciences, What Works Clearinghouse. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/16

Synthesis Government evidence synthesis (WWC practice guide) with graded recommendations. Who: Students in grades 4–8; US studies.

Finding. Strong evidence: teach students to monitor and reflect on their problem solving; teach the use of visual representations. Moderate evidence: expose students to multiple strategies; help them articulate concepts and notation. Minimal evidence: preparing problems for whole-class use.

Guide ratings, not pooled effect sizes; revised 2018; publication number not given in the abstract.

Tutoring, software and curricula

High-dosage tutoring is the most reliable large effect in education economics. Software helps when it adapts to the child and is actually used. Choice of textbook matters less than how daily practice is run.

Tutoring works, and about as well for maths as for reading (meta-analysis)

Nickow, A., Oreopoulos, P., & Quan, V. (2024). The promise of tutoring for PreK–12 learning: A systematic review and meta-analysis of the experimental evidence. American Educational Research Journal, 61(1), 74–107. doi:10.3102/00028312231208687

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 89 randomised controlled trials of one-to-one or small-group tutoring. Who: PreK to grade 12, mostly United States.

Finding. Pooled effect 0.288 SD versus no tutoring; maths 0.268 SD, literacy 0.290 SD. Largest effects with teacher or paraprofessional tutors, in earlier grades, at three or more sessions a week, during school. The 2020 working paper (NBER w27476, 96 studies) reported 0.37 SD overall, 0.38 SD for maths; teacher tutors 0.50, paraprofessionals 0.40, volunteers 0.21.

Only about a quarter of studies were maths; no sign of publication bias; the journal estimate is lower than the working paper's.

High-dosage maths tutoring for teenagers (Chicago, two RCTs)

Guryan, J., Ludwig, J., Bhatt, M. P., Cook, P. J., Davis, J. M. V., Dodge, K., Farkas, G., Fryer, R. G., Jr., Mayer, S., Pollack, H., Steinberg, L., & Stoddard, G. (2023). Not too late: Improving academic outcomes among adolescents. American Economic Review, 113(3), 738–765. doi:10.1257/aer.20210434

RCT Two large school-based RCTs of Saga Education's daily, in-school, two-to-one maths tutoring by paraprofessional tutors. Who: n = 2,633 (first trial) and 2,710 (replication); grades 9–10; Chicago public high schools.

Finding. Maths test scores rose 0.18 to 0.40 SD (0.16 SD first trial, 0.37 SD replication) relative to controls; maths and other grades also improved, with effects persisting. Cost about $3,500–$4,300 per student per year. The 106-boy pilot (Cook et al., 2014, NBER w19862) found 0.65 SD on maths tests.

One provider and city; the two trials gave different point estimates.

What helps low-income pupils most: tutoring, feedback, cooperative learning (meta-analysis)

Dietrichson, J., Bøg, M., Filges, T., & Klint Jørgensen, A.-M. (2017). Academic interventions for elementary and middle school students with low socioeconomic status: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 87(2), 243–282. doi:10.3102/0034654316687036

Synthesis Systematic review and meta-analysis of 101 studies (2000–2014), mostly RCTs, with standardised maths and reading tests as outcomes. Who: Elementary and middle-school pupils of low socioeconomic status in OECD/EU countries.

Finding. Pooled effects by intervention component: tutoring 0.36 SD, feedback and progress monitoring 0.32 SD, cooperative learning 0.22 SD, each versus business-as-usual controls.

Large unexplained variation in effects within each component; maths and reading pooled together in the headline figures.

EEF Toolkit: one-to-one tuition

Education Endowment Foundation (2021, review last updated). One to one tuition. Teaching and Learning Toolkit. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/one-to-one-tuition

Synthesis Government-funded evidence synthesis (meta-analysis of 123 studies meeting inclusion criteria). Who: School-age pupils, mainly UK and US studies.

Finding. "+5 months" additional progress over a year; rated "high impact for moderate cost based on moderate evidence" (moderate evidence security padlocks). Primary +6 months, secondary +4. The entry says effects in mathematics appear substantially lower (+2 months) than in literacy (+6 months). Short regular sessions (about 30 minutes, 3–5 times a week, up to ten weeks) linked to lessons are recommended.

Months-progress is a translation of effect sizes; many included studies were not independently evaluated.

EEF Toolkit: small-group tuition

Education Endowment Foundation (2021, review last updated July 2021). Small group tuition. Teaching and Learning Toolkit. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/small-group-tuition

Synthesis Government-funded evidence synthesis (meta-analysis of 62 studies). Small-group tuition means one adult working with two to five pupils. Who: School-age pupils, mainly UK and US studies.

Finding. "+4 months" additional progress over a year; summary rating "moderate impact for low cost based on moderate evidence". Studies in mathematics show a slightly smaller impact (+3 months) than reading (+4 months). Impact is larger when targeted at diagnosed needs and when staff are trained.

Evidence security rated down because a large share of studies were not independently evaluated; months-progress is a translation of effect sizes.

Remedial tutoring (Balsakhi) and maths software in Indian primary schools

Banerjee, A. V., Cole, S., Duflo, E., & Linden, L. (2007). Remedying education: Evidence from two randomized experiments in India. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 122(3), 1235–1264. doi:10.1162/qjec.122.3.1235

RCT Two school-level RCTs: a remedial tutor (balsakhi) for lagging pupils, and computer-assisted maths practice. Who: 122 primary schools in Vadodara and 77 in Mumbai; grades 2–4; 2001–2003.

Finding. Balsakhi raised average test scores by 0.14 SD (year one) and 0.28 SD (year two), largest in maths, about 0.6 SD among children actually tutored. Computer-assisted learning raised grade-4 maths by 0.35 SD and 0.47 SD. Gains faded but partly persisted a year after the programmes ended.

Low-resource setting; tutors were community women with two weeks' training, so transfer to rich countries is uncertain.

Personalised adaptive maths software after school (Mindspark, Delhi)

Muralidharan, K., Singh, A., & Ganimian, A. J. (2019). Disrupting education? Experimental evidence on technology-aided instruction in India. American Economic Review, 109(4), 1426–1460. doi:10.1257/aer.20171112

RCT Lottery-based RCT of vouchers for a personalised, computer-adaptive after-school programme. Who: n = 619 students, mostly grades 6–9, Delhi, 2015–16.

Finding. After 4.5 months, lottery winners scored 0.37 SD higher in maths and 0.23 SD higher in Hindi than losers. Instrumental-variable estimates imply about 0.6 SD in maths for 90 days of attendance. Gains were similar in absolute terms for all pupils, so the weakest gained most relative to their starting point.

Small sample, one city, short duration; the software targeted each child's actual level rather than the grade curriculum, which may be the active ingredient.

A research-based preschool maths curriculum (Building Blocks)

Clements, D. H., Sarama, J., Spitler, M. E., Lange, A. A., & Wolfe, C. B. (2011). Mathematics learned by young children in an intervention based on learning trajectories: A large-scale cluster randomized trial. Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 42(2), 127–166. doi:10.5951/jresematheduc.42.2.0127

RCT Cluster RCT; 42 schools randomly assigned, with teacher professional development built on learning trajectories. Who: n = 1,375 preschoolers in 106 classrooms in low-resource US communities.

Finding. Building Blocks children learned more mathematics than controls, g = 0.72. The earlier 36-classroom RCT (Clements & Sarama, 2008, AERJ, 45(2), 443–494) found effect sizes of 0.47 versus a comparison curriculum and 1.07 versus business as usual.

Outcome measure developed by the same team; What Works Clearinghouse rated the 2011 study as meeting standards without reservations.

One hour of a linear number board game (preschool)

Ramani, G. B., & Siegler, R. S. (2008). Promoting broad and stable improvements in low-income children's numerical knowledge through playing number board games. Child Development, 79(2), 375–394. doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.01131.x

RCT RCT; children randomly assigned to a 1–10 linear number board game or an identical colour game. Who: n = 136 Head Start preschoolers (mean age 5.4), 10 centres, Pittsburgh, USA.

Finding. About one hour of play (four short sessions) improved magnitude comparison, number-line estimation, counting and numeral identification versus the colour game; gains held nine weeks later. Effect sizes not given in the abstract. A follow-up (Siegler & Ramani, 2009, J. Educational Psychology, 101(3), 545ff.; n = 88) found a linear board worked but a circular one did not.

Small lab-style studies, short delays, researcher-designed measures.

Which early-elementary maths curriculum? (IES four-curriculum RCT)

Agodini, R., Harris, B., Thomas, M., Murphy, R., & Gallagher, L. (2010). Achievement effects of four early elementary school math curricula: Findings for first and second graders (NCEE 2011-4001). Institute of Education Sciences, US Department of Education. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20114001/

RCT RCT; 110 schools in 12 districts randomly assigned to Investigations, Math Expressions, Saxon Math or Scott Foresman–Addison Wesley (SFAW). Who: 4,716 first-graders (109 schools) and 3,344 second-graders (71 schools), USA.

Finding. Grade 1: Math Expressions beat Investigations and SFAW by 0.11 SD (50th to 54th percentile). Grade 2: Math Expressions and Saxon beat SFAW by 0.12 and 0.17 SD. Other pairwise differences were not significant.

After adjusting for multiple comparisons only the Saxon–SFAW grade-2 difference (0.17 SD) survived; participating schools were volunteers, not a national sample.

Cognitive Tutor Algebra I at scale

Pane, J. F., Griffin, B. A., McCaffrey, D. F., & Karam, R. (2014). Effectiveness of Cognitive Tutor Algebra I at scale. Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, 36(2), 127–144. doi:10.3102/0162373713507480

RCT Matched-pair school-level RCT over two years; blended mastery-learning software plus textbook versus existing algebra course. Who: Middle and high schools in seven US states; WWC counts 5,738 high-school students (grades 9–12) in the year-2 analysis.

Finding. No effect in year one. In year two, high schools using the programme scored about 0.2 SD higher on an algebra proficiency exam (median student up roughly eight percentile points); the middle-school estimate was similar but not significant.

The What Works Clearinghouse re-analysis found no statistically significant effects, so the result is fragile; first-year implementation problems were common.

Online maths homework with instant feedback (ASSISTments, Maine)

Roschelle, J., Feng, M., Murphy, R. F., & Mason, C. A. (2016). Online mathematics homework increases student achievement. AERA Open, 2(4), article 2332858416673968. doi:10.1177/2332858416673968

RCT School-level RCT; teachers assigned homework through ASSISTments (hints and immediate feedback for pupils, reports for teachers) plus training, versus usual homework. Who: n = 2,850 seventh-graders in 43 schools in Maine, USA, where every seventh-grader has a laptop.

Finding. Treatment pupils scored higher on the end-of-year standardised maths test, g = 0.18 (50th to 58th percentile), with the largest gains for pupils with low prior achievement.

One state with unusual laptop provision; the effect size appears in a companion SRI report rather than the article abstract.

"Singapore Math" in US classrooms (Math in Focus)

Jaciw, A. P., Hegseth, W. M., Lin, L., Toby, M., Newman, D., Ma, B., & Zacamy, J. (2016). Assessing impacts of Math in Focus, a "Singapore Math" program. Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness, 9(4), 473–502. doi:10.1080/19345747.2016.1164777

RCT Cluster RCT; grade-level teams randomly assigned to Math in Focus or business-as-usual curriculum. Who: 12 schools, 22 grade-level teams, grades 3–5, USA; student n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Effects on mathematics achievement of 0.11 to 0.15 SD in favour of Math in Focus after one year, with no significant differences by prior achievement, minority status or grade.

Small number of clusters; one school year; modest effects relative to the programme's reputation.

196 education field experiments pooled: tutoring stands out

Fryer, R. G., Jr. (2017). The production of human capital in developed countries: Evidence from 196 randomized field experiments. In Handbook of Economic Field Experiments (pp. 95–322). Elsevier. doi:10.1016/bs.hefe.2016.08.006

Synthesis Review with fixed- and random-effects meta-analysis of 196 RCTs, grouped by intervention type. Who: Children and adolescents in developed countries, mostly USA.

Finding. High-dosage tutoring had the largest school-based meta-coefficient: 0.309 SD (SE 0.106) for maths and 0.229 SD for reading; 54% of tutoring estimates were significantly positive and none significantly negative. Low-dosage tutoring, teacher incentives, general professional development and vouchers averaged near zero in maths (0.015, 0.022, 0.019 and 0.024 SD).

Only six maths tutoring studies behind the headline figure; heterogeneous outcomes pooled across very different programmes.

Anxiety, mindset and the home

Maths anxiety is real, measurable and partly contagious from anxious parents and teachers. Mindset interventions are far smaller than their press. Short interventions aimed at parents, including a bedtime maths app, have moved children's achievement.

Maths story problems at home (Bedtime Math app), grade 1

Berkowitz, T., Schaeffer, M. W., Maloney, E. A., Peterson, L., Gregor, C., Levine, S. C., & Beilock, S. L. (2015). Math at home adds up to achievement in school. Science, 350(6257), 196–198. doi:10.1126/science.aac7427

RCT Randomised field experiment (families assigned to a maths-story app or a matched reading app). Who: n = 587 first-graders and their parents, Chicago-area schools, USA; one school year.

Finding. The maths-app group gained more on a standardised maths test than reading-app controls; children who used the app often were about three months ahead by year end (figure from the university release). Gains were largest for children of maths-anxious parents.

Effect size in SD units not given in the abstract; the dose-response comparison is not randomised (use was self-selected); the follow-up below found no overall main effect by grade 3.

Bedtime Math follow-up to grade 3

Schaeffer, M. W., Rozek, C. S., Berkowitz, T., Levine, S. C., & Beilock, S. L. (2018). Disassociating the relation between parents' math anxiety and children's math achievement: Long-term effects of a math app intervention. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 147(12), 1782–1790. doi:10.1037/xge0000490

RCT Longitudinal follow-up of the 2015 RCT. Who: The same cohort (587 first-graders, about 40 Chicago-area classrooms) followed to the end of grade 3, USA.

Finding. No overall main effect of the maths app on maths achievement by grade 3. Children of highly maths-anxious parents in the reading-app group learned less maths each year than peers; in the maths-app group this gap was absent, even though app use had fallen to roughly monthly.

A moderation result, not an average gain; the authors state there is no main effect, and the paper has been criticised for its framing. Sample retained at follow-up not given in the abstract.

Telling parents why maths matters (utility-value), high school

Harackiewicz, J. M., Rozek, C. S., Hulleman, C. S., & Hyde, J. S. (2012). Helping parents to motivate adolescents in mathematics and science: An experimental test of a utility-value intervention. Psychological Science, 23(8), 899–906. doi:10.1177/0956797611435530

RCT RCT (families randomised; two mailed brochures and a website for parents). Who: n = 188 families (181 with transcripts) from the Wisconsin Study of Families and Work; teenagers in grades 10–12, USA.

Finding. Students whose parents received the materials took 8.31 semesters of maths and science in the last two years of high school versus 7.50 for controls, about 0.8 of a semester more.

Outcome is course-taking, not test scores; sample mostly well-educated two-parent families (parents averaged 15.4 years of schooling).

Utility-value parent intervention, five-year follow-up

Rozek, C. S., Svoboda, R. C., Harackiewicz, J. M., Hulleman, C. S., & Hyde, J. S. (2017). Utility-value intervention with parents increases students' STEM preparation and career pursuit. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 114(5), 909–914. doi:10.1073/pnas.1607386114

RCT Follow-up of the 2012 RCT. Who: Same Wisconsin families; outcomes measured through the end of high school and five years after the intervention, USA.

Finding. The intervention raised ACT mathematics and science scores by 12 percentile points. Higher STEM preparation in turn predicted more STEM career interest and more college STEM courses five years on.

Downstream career effects are indirect (mediation), not a direct randomised contrast; single moderate-sized sample; number analysed at follow-up not given in the abstract.

National growth-mindset trial, grade 9

Yeager, D. S., Hanselman, P., Walton, G. M., Murray, J. S., Crosnoe, R., Muller, C., ... Dweck, C. S. (2019). A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement. Nature, 573(7774), 364–369. doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y

RCT Pre-registered RCT, students randomised within schools; two online sessions under one hour in total. Who: n = 12,490 ninth-graders in 65 US public high schools, a nationally representative sample.

Finding. Among the pre-registered lower-achieving half (n = 6,320), core GPA rose by 0.10 grade points (95% CI 0.04–0.16; standardised effect 0.11). Across all students, enrolment in advanced maths (algebra II or higher) in grade 10 rose from 33% to 36% (+3 percentage points). No GPA effect for higher achievers.

Effects small and concentrated where peer norms supported effort; several authors disseminate mindset programmes (disclosed); see the two meta-analyses below.

Changing Mindsets (EEF trial), Year 6 England: null

Foliano, F., Rolfe, H., Buzzeo, J., Runge, J., & Wilkinson, D. (2019). Changing Mindsets: Effectiveness trial. Evaluation report. London: Education Endowment Foundation (evaluated by NIESR). https://niesr.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Changing-Mindsets_0-4.pdf

RCT Two-arm cluster RCT (schools randomised); eight weekly teacher-led growth-mindset lessons plus teacher training. Who: 101 schools, 5,018 Year 6 pupils (age 10–11), England.

Finding. No effect on Key Stage 2 maths: effect size −0.01 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.01), 0 months' progress, n = 4,454. Reading and GPS also zero. No effect for pupils eligible for free school meals.

EEF gave the result a high security rating. Most control-school teachers already knew growth-mindset theory, which may dilute the contrast.

Growth-mindset interventions: systematic review and meta-analysis

Macnamara, B. N., & Burgoyne, A. P. (2023). Do growth mindset interventions impact students' academic achievement? A systematic review and meta-analysis with recommendations for best practices. Psychological Bulletin, 149(3–4), 133–173. doi:10.1037/bul0000352

Synthesis Systematic review with three meta-analyses, plus study-quality coding. Who: 63 studies, N = 97,672 students, all ages; mostly USA.

Finding. Overall d = 0.05 (95% CI 0.02–0.09), not significant after publication-bias correction. Studies showing the intervention actually changed mindsets: d = 0.04 (CI −0.01 to 0.10). Highest-quality six studies (N = 13,571): d = 0.02 (CI −0.06 to 0.10). Authors with a financial interest reported larger effects.

Outcomes are general achievement, not maths-specific; the authors' conclusions are contested by mindset researchers (published replies exist).

Mindset and achievement: two meta-analyses

Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses. Psychological Science, 29(4), 549–571. doi:10.1177/0956797617739704

Synthesis Meta-analysis of correlations (k = 273) and of intervention studies (k = 43). Who: N = 365,915 (correlational) and N = 57,155 (interventions); children, adolescents and adults.

Finding. Mindset correlated weakly with achievement, r = .10 (95% CI .08–.13). Interventions: d = 0.08 (95% CI 0.02–0.14). Low-SES students (7 effect sizes) d = 0.34 (CI 0.07–0.62); middle/upper-class students d = 0.03, not significant.

Outcomes not maths-specific; subgroup estimates rest on few studies; large heterogeneity.

Ten minutes of writing about worries before a test

Ramirez, G., & Beilock, S. L. (2011). Writing about testing worries boosts exam performance in the classroom. Science, 331(6014), 211–213. doi:10.1126/science.1199427

RCT Two lab experiments and two randomised classroom experiments. Who: Lab: college students doing maths under pressure (n = 20 and 47). Classroom: 106 ninth-graders sitting a biology final at a Midwestern US high school over two years.

Finding. Test-anxious students who wrote about their worries for 10 minutes averaged B+ on the final versus B− for anxious students who wrote about a biology topic (about 6% higher), matching low-anxiety peers. Low-anxiety students were unaffected.

The classroom outcome was biology, not maths; maths was tested only in the lab with small samples; a 2014 erratum was published.

Expressive writing and maths anxiety (lab)

Park, D., Ramirez, G., & Beilock, S. L. (2014). The role of expressive writing in math anxiety. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 20(2), 103–111. doi:10.1037/xap0000013

RCT Lab experiment, random assignment to writing or sitting quietly. Who: N = 80 university students varying in maths anxiety, USA.

Finding. In the control group, high maths-anxious students did significantly worse on maths problems than low-anxious students; after seven minutes of writing about their feelings this gap was significantly reduced. Word problems showed no such pattern.

Adults in a lab, single session, immediate test; effect size not given in the abstract.

Stereotype threat and girls' maths: meta-analysis

Flore, P. C., & Wicherts, J. M. (2015). Does stereotype threat influence performance of girls in stereotyped domains? A meta-analysis. Journal of School Psychology, 53(1), 25–44. doi:10.1016/j.jsp.2014.10.002

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 47 effect sizes from (quasi-)experiments. Who: Girls under 18 taking maths, science or spatial tests; multiple countries.

Finding. Mean effect of a threat manipulation on girls' scores d = −0.22, significantly below zero. No moderator (test difficulty, presence of boys, national gender equality, control type) was significant.

Several indicators of publication bias; the authors warn the literature may be seriously distorted and call for a large preregistered test (next entry).

Stereotype threat preregistered replication: null

Flore, P. C., Mulder, J., & Wicherts, J. M. (2018). The influence of gender stereotype threat on mathematics test scores of Dutch high school students: A registered report. Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology, 3(2), 140–174. doi:10.1080/23743603.2018.1559647

RCT Preregistered registered report; students randomised within class to a threat or nullified-stereotype instruction before a maths test. Who: N = 2,064 second-year secondary students (age 13–14, about US grade 8), average-to-high tracks, Netherlands.

Finding. No stereotype threat effect: threat coefficient −0.06 points (SE 0.16) on a 20-item test; threat × gender interaction 0.16 (SE 0.31), not significant. Bayes factor 28 in favour of no effect over the threat hypothesis. Boys outscored girls regardless of condition.

One country and one manipulation; Dutch students may not hold the stereotype.

Maths anxiety and achievement: meta-analysis (correlational)

Barroso, C., Ganley, C. M., McGraw, A. L., Geer, E. A., Hart, S. A., & Daucourt, M. C. (2021). A meta-analysis of the relation between math anxiety and math achievement. Psychological Bulletin, 147(2), 134–168. doi:10.1037/bul0000307

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 747 effect sizes (1992–2018). Who: Children through adults, many countries.

Finding. Correlation r = −.28 between maths anxiety and maths achievement. Present from childhood to adulthood; smaller in grades 3–5 and at university, smaller for exam grades, larger for certain anxiety scales.

Correlational only; direction of causation unresolved. Published studies report stronger correlations than unpublished ones.

Maths-anxious parents and homework help (correlational)

Maloney, E. A., Ramirez, G., Gunderson, E. A., Levine, S. C., & Beilock, S. L. (2015). Intergenerational effects of parents' math anxiety on children's math achievement and anxiety. Psychological Science, 26(9), 1480–1488. doi:10.1177/0956797615592630

Correlational Longitudinal field study across one school year; not an experiment. Who: n = 438 first- and second-graders (90 classrooms, 29 schools, three Midwestern US states) and their primary caregivers.

Finding. Parents' maths anxiety predicted lower maths growth and higher child maths anxiety only when parents reported helping often with maths homework (interaction p = .033). No such relation for reading.

Correlational; help frequency self-reported; families who returned surveys were higher-SES than the full sample; effect in grade-equivalent units only shown graphically.

Homework, feedback, grouping and time

Feedback helps on average, but more than a third of measured feedback effects in the classic meta-analysis were negative. Homework correlates with achievement in secondary school and barely at all in primary. Acceleration works for the children who are ready for it.

Small classes in kindergarten to grade 3 (Project STAR)

Krueger, A. B. (1999). Experimental estimates of education production functions. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 114(2), 497–532. doi:10.1162/003355399556052

RCT Randomised controlled trial (students and teachers randomly assigned to small, regular, or regular-with-aide classes), with adjustment for attrition and class switching. Who: n = about 11,600 students, kindergarten to grade 3, Tennessee, USA, from 1985–86.

Finding. Standardised test performance (Stanford Achievement Test, reading and maths) rose by about 4 percentile points in the first year in a small class (13–17 pupils) versus a regular class (22–25), and the gap grew by about 1 percentile point per year after that. Teacher aides had little effect. Gains were larger for minority and free-lunch pupils.

The abstract pools reading and maths; maths-only estimates were not verified in a fetched record. Single US state, 1980s.

Class size in Israeli primary schools (Maimonides' rule)

Angrist, J. D., & Lavy, V. (1999). Using Maimonides' rule to estimate the effect of class size on scholastic achievement. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 114(2), 533–575. doi:10.1162/003355399556061

Quasi-experiment Regression discontinuity / instrumental variables, using the rule that splits a grade into two classes once enrolment passes 40. Who: Population of Israeli public-school classes; grade 4 and 5 pupils tested in 1991, grade 3 in 1992. Unit of analysis is the class; class counts not given in the abstract.

Finding. Smaller classes raised grade 5 reading and maths scores. With enrolment controls, the IV estimate for grade 5 maths was about −0.23 points per extra pupil (reading −0.26 to −0.28), so a class 10 pupils smaller scores roughly 2–3 points higher. Smaller effect for grade 4 reading, none for grade 3.

Grade 3 null may reflect different testing that year. Later re-analyses (not fetched here) have debated score manipulation near the cutoff.

Mandatory summer school and grade retention in Chicago

Jacob, B. A., & Lefgren, L. (2004). Remedial education and student achievement: A regression-discontinuity analysis. The Review of Economics and Statistics, 86(1), 226–244. doi:10.1162/003465304323023778

Quasi-experiment Regression discontinuity around promotion test cut-offs under Chicago's 1996 accountability policy. Who: Chicago Public Schools grade 3 and grade 6 cohorts 1997–1999 (base sample of about 347,000 students across pre- and post-policy cohorts); over 30,000 third graders and 21,000 sixth graders per year attended summer school.

Finding. For grade 3, summer school plus retention raised maths and reading achievement by roughly 20% of a year's learning; effects faded by 25–40% by the second year but stayed significant. For grade 6, no net benefit, and retention had little effect on maths.

Low-income urban district; effects are the combined programme, not summer school alone; test-based selection means the estimates apply to pupils near the cut-off.

Ability grouping and acceleration, 100 years of meta-analyses

Steenbergen-Hu, S., Makel, M. C., & Olszewski-Kubilius, P. (2016). What one hundred years of research says about the effects of ability grouping and acceleration on K–12 students' academic achievement: Findings of two second-order meta-analyses. Review of Educational Research, 86(4), 849–899. doi:10.3102/0034654316675417

Synthesis Two second-order meta-analyses (13 ability-grouping meta-analyses; several acceleration meta-analyses). Who: K–12 students, mostly US studies spanning about a century.

Finding. Within-class grouping g = 0.19–0.30, cross-grade subject grouping g = 0.26, special grouping for the gifted g = 0.37; between-class grouping (streaming/tracking) g = 0.04–0.06, with no difference for high-, medium- or low-ability students. Accelerated students outperformed same-age peers (g = 0.70) and matched older non-accelerated peers (g = 0.09); overall acceleration g = 0.42.

Second-order synthesis inherits the mostly non-randomised designs of the underlying studies; not maths-specific.

Weekly instruction time and PISA maths scores

Lavy, V. (2015). Do differences in schools' instruction time explain international achievement gaps? Evidence from developed and developing countries. The Economic Journal, 125(588), F397–F424. doi:10.1111/ecoj.12233

Quasi-experiment Quasi-experimental; within-student comparison across subjects (maths, science, language) so pupil fixed effects absorb ability and family background. Who: PISA 2006, over 400,000 15-year-olds in more than 50 countries.

Finding. One extra hour per week of instruction in a subject raised that subject's score by about 0.15 SD of the within-student test-score distribution in OECD and Eastern European samples, but only about 0.025 SD in developing countries. Returns were higher where schools faced accountability or had more autonomy.

Effect sizes quoted from the NBER working paper version; instruction time is self-reported by pupils.

Homework and maths/science achievement, 30-year meta-analysis

Fan, H., Xu, J., Cai, Z., He, J., & Fan, X. (2017). Homework and students' achievement in math and science: A 30-year meta-analysis, 1986–2015. Educational Research Review, 20, 35–54. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2016.11.003

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 28 studies (41 independent correlations), correlational designs including PISA/TIMSS samples. Who: About 312,800 students, mainly primary to upper secondary, mostly US (31 of 41 correlations).

Finding. Small positive association overall, r = 0.23; maths r = 0.20, science r = 0.26. Stronger in elementary (r = 0.32) and high school (r = 0.30) than middle school (r = 0.15). US r = 0.27 versus r = 0.07 in Europe and Asia. Time spent on homework correlated only r = 0.19.

Correlational, so no causal claim; effects heterogeneous and several moderator estimates rest on few studies.

Basic-fact fluency interventions

Codding, R. S., Burns, M. K., & Lukito, G. (2011). Meta-analysis of mathematic basic-fact fluency interventions: A component analysis. Learning Disabilities Research & Practice, 26(1), 36–47. doi:10.1111/j.1540-5826.2010.00323.x

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 17 single-case design studies, with component analysis. Who: 55 participants, school-age students (many with maths difficulties); setting details not given in the abstract.

Finding. Drill-and-practice combined with modelling produced the largest effect sizes for basic-fact fluency. Interventions with more than three components beat those with fewer, and using more than one treatment agent (e.g. peer plus teacher) was more effective than one.

Single-case designs and a very small pooled sample; numeric effect sizes not given in the abstract.

Calculators in K–12 maths classes

Ellington, A. J. (2003). A meta-analysis of the effects of calculators on students' achievement and attitude levels in precollege mathematics classes. Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 34(5), 433–463. doi:10.2307/30034795

Synthesis Meta-analysis of calculator-use studies in K–12 classrooms. Who: K–12 students, mostly US; number of studies not given in the abstract (the author's 2000 dissertation on which it builds pooled 53 studies, 307 effect sizes).

Finding. Calculator use during instruction did not harm students' operational (paper-and-pencil) skills, and problem-solving skills and attitudes to maths improved; benefits were largest when calculators were allowed in both instruction and testing.

Mostly short studies from the 1980s–90s; findings for the published article taken from the record abstract and the underlying dissertation, not the full text.

Feedback interventions: positive on average, often harmful

Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. Psychological Bulletin, 119(2), 254–284. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes. Who: 23,663 observations across lab and field studies of task performance (not maths-specific).

Finding. Feedback improved performance on average, d = 0.41, but more than one third of effects were negative. Effectiveness fell as feedback drew attention away from the task and toward the self (e.g. praise or person-focused comments).

Broad performance literature, not classroom maths; the theory part is labelled preliminary by the authors.

Educational feedback meta-analysis (2020)

Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). The power of feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research. Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 3087. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 435 studies (k = 994 effect sizes), random-effects model. Who: N > 61,000 students; median publication year 1985.

Finding. Overall d = 0.48 on learning (0.55 before removing outliers), with 17% of effects negative and high heterogeneity (I² = 83%). Larger for cognitive outcomes (d = 0.51) than motivational (d = 0.33). High-information feedback (task, process and self-regulation combined) d = 0.99; corrective feedback d = 0.46; reinforcement/punishment d = 0.24.

Egger's test indicated publication bias in journal articles but not dissertations; not maths-specific.

Teachers' mathematical knowledge and pupil gains (correlational)

Hill, H. C., Rowan, B., & Ball, D. L. (2005). Effects of teachers' mathematical knowledge for teaching on student achievement. American Educational Research Journal, 42(2), 371–406. doi:10.3102/00028312042002371

Correlational Correlational; linear mixed models of one-year gain scores, students nested in teachers in schools. Who: 1,190 first graders (334 teachers) and 1,773 third graders (365 teachers) in 115 mostly low-income US schools.

Finding. Teachers' score on a 30-item "mathematical knowledge for teaching" test predicted pupils' maths gains in both grades after controlling for student and teacher covariates; press coverage of the study put it at roughly one third to one half of a month's extra growth per SD of teacher knowledge.

Observational, so teacher knowledge may proxy for other teacher qualities; the month-equivalent figure comes from an Education Week report, not the article's abstract.

Summer loss is larger in maths (classic meta-analysis)

Cooper, H., Nye, B., Charlton, K., Lindsay, J., & Greathouse, S. (1996). The effects of summer vacation on achievement test scores: A narrative and meta-analytic review. Review of Educational Research, 66(3), 227–268. doi:10.3102/00346543066003227

Synthesis Narrative review of 39 studies with meta-analysis of the 13 most recent. Who: US school pupils across grades; pooled sample size not given in the abstract.

Finding. Scores fell over summer by about one month on a grade-equivalent scale, or one tenth of a standard deviation relative to spring scores. The loss was larger for maths than reading, and largest for maths computation and spelling. Losses grew with grade level; no moderation by gender or race.

Older studies on grade-equivalent scales; later work (Atteberry & McEachin below; von Hippel) questions how much of the classic pattern is a measurement artefact.

Summer learning loss at scale (NWEA MAP)

Atteberry, A., & McEachin, A. (2021). School's out: The role of summers in understanding achievement disparities. American Educational Research Journal, 58(2), 239–282. doi:10.3102/0002831220937285

Correlational Descriptive longitudinal growth modelling of a large test-score panel (not causal). Who: About 18 million US students, grades 1–8, roughly 200 million maths and reading MAP scores, 7,500 districts, all 50 states, 2008–2016.

Finding. On average pupils lost 25–34% of each school-year maths gain over the following summer (17–28% in ELA). Variation was large: some pupils kept learning, others lost almost all of the year's gain; 52% lost ground in ELA in five consecutive summers. Race and income explained only about 4% of the variance in summer loss.

Published online 2020; test taken on different dates, so summer is partly imputed; no intervention tested.

Early maths skills predict later achievement (correlational)

Duncan, G. J., Dowsett, C. J., Claessens, A., Magnuson, K., Huston, A. C., Klebanov, P., Pagani, L. S., Feinstein, L., Engel, M., Brooks-Gunn, J., Sexton, H., Duckworth, K., & Japel, C. (2007). School readiness and later achievement. Developmental Psychology, 43(6), 1428–1446. doi:10.1037/0012-1649.43.6.1428

Synthesis Correlational; coordinated regressions in six longitudinal datasets (US, UK, Canada) pooled by meta-analysis of 228 standardised coefficients. Who: Children followed from school entry (about age 5) into later primary and secondary school; pooled n not given in the abstract.

Finding. School-entry maths was the strongest predictor of later reading and maths achievement (average standardised coefficient 0.33), ahead of early reading (0.13) and attention (0.07), after controlling for prior cognitive skills and family background. Socio-emotional measures did not predict achievement.

Prediction, not causation; Watts et al. (2014, Educational Researcher) replicate with β = 0.24 for 54-month maths to age-15 maths in the NICHD SECCYD (n = 828).

Claims the evidence does not support

Each of these was tested properly and did not hold up, or held up much more weakly than commonly claimed.

Claim: "Chess lessons make children better at maths"

Sala, G., & Gobet, F. (2016). Do the benefits of chess instruction transfer to academic and cognitive skills? A meta-analysis. Educational Research Review, 18, 46–57. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2016.02.002

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 24 studies (40 effect sizes). Who: 2,788 young people in chess conditions, 2,433 controls; countries not given in the abstract.

Finding. Moderate overall effect (g = 0.338), larger for mathematics (g = 0.382) than reading (g = 0.248), rising with hours of instruction. But no study used an "ideal design" with pre/post tests, full randomisation and an active control group, so placebo effects cannot be ruled out.

The positive pooled effect rests on weak designs; treat as unproven, not as support.

Claim: "Working-memory training (brain games) improves maths"

Melby-Lervåg, M., Redick, T. S., & Hulme, C. (2016). Working memory training does not improve performance on measures of intelligence or other measures of "far transfer": Evidence from a meta-analytic review. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 512–534. doi:10.1177/1745691616635612

Synthesis Meta-analysis of 87 publications, 145 experimental comparisons (pretest–posttest with control group). Who: Ages and pooled n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Training improved the trained tasks, but for far transfer (arithmetic, reading, verbal and nonverbal ability) there was "no convincing evidence of any reliable improvements" against treated controls, and working-memory gains did not predict far-transfer gains.

Sala & Gobet (2017, https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000265): far transfer g = 0.12 in children aged 3–16, shrinking with design quality. Simons et al. (2016, https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100616661983): same verdict on commercial brain-training.

Claim: "Music lessons make children smarter and better at maths"

Sala, G., & Gobet, F. (2020). Cognitive and academic benefits of music training with children: A multilevel meta-analysis. Memory & Cognition, 48(8), 1429–1441. doi:10.3758/s13421-020-01060-2

Synthesis Multilevel meta-analysis (254 effect sizes). Who: 6,984 children; international.

Finding. Once study-design quality is controlled for, "the overall effect of music training programs is null." Significant effects appeared only in studies without random allocation or with non-active control groups, and did not vary by outcome, age or training length.

The authors' 2017 meta-analysis (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2016.11.005 found d = 0.16, shrinking with design quality. Transfer only; musical skill itself is not in question.

Claim: "Playing Mozart boosts reasoning (the Mozart effect)"

Pietschnig, J., Voracek, M., & Formann, A. K. (2010). Mozart effect–Shmozart effect: A meta-analysis. Intelligence, 38(3), 314–323. doi:10.1016/j.intell.2010.03.001

Synthesis Meta-analysis of nearly 40 studies. Who: Over 3,000 participants, published and unpublished studies.

Finding. Mozart's K.448 versus silence gave a small effect on spatial tasks (d = 0.37); any other music gave the same (d = 0.38); Mozart versus other music was negligible (d = 0.15). Effects were larger in the originating lab and shrank with publication-bias correction. "Little evidence left for a specific, performance-enhancing Mozart effect."

Short-term spatial-task performance, not maths learning.

Claim: "10,000 hours of practice is what separates experts from the rest"

Macnamara, B. N., Hambrick, D. Z., & Oswald, F. L. (2014). Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis. Psychological Science, 25(8), 1608–1618. doi:10.1177/0956797614535810

Synthesis Meta-analysis across all major domains in which deliberate practice has been measured; study count not given in the abstract. Who: Pooled n not given in the abstract.

Finding. Deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education and under 1% for professions. "Deliberate practice is important, but not as important as has been argued."

Correlational data on accumulated practice; the education figure is for academic attainment, not maths specifically.

Claim: "Teach to each child's learning style (visual, auditory...)"

Rogowsky, B. A., Calhoun, B. M., & Tallal, P. (2020). Providing instruction based on students' learning style preferences does not improve learning. Frontiers in Psychology, 11, 164. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00164

RCT Randomised experiment using the Pashler et al. (2008) test: assess style, randomise to modality, same test for all. Who: n = 125 fifth graders (118 with complete data), rural Pennsylvania, USA.

Finding. No relationship between learning-style preference, instructional mode (listening vs reading) and comprehension, either immediately or at two-week retention. Children classed as visual learners did better in both modes.

Comprehension, not maths; one school. The same team's adult study (2015, J. Educational Psychology, 107(1), 64–78, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037478 also "failed to statistically support the meshing hypothesis".

Claim: "Learning styles are established science"

Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105–119. doi:10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x

Synthesis Commissioned evidence review setting out the required test (an aptitude-by-treatment crossover) and auditing the literature against it. Who: Review; no new participants.

Finding. People do report preferences, but "we found virtually no evidence for the interaction pattern" needed to justify matching instruction to style; the few properly designed studies contradicted it. Conclusion: no justification for learning-styles assessment in general practice.

Review rather than meta-analysis; subsequent RCTs (Rogowsky et al. 2015, 2020) tested and rejected the pattern.

Claim: "Handwritten notes beat typed notes"

Morehead, K., Dunlosky, J., & Rawson, K. A. (2019). How much mightier is the pen than the keyboard for note-taking? A replication and extension of Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014). Educational Psychology Review, 31(3), 753–780. doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09468-2 · doi:10.1177/0956797614524581

Synthesis Two randomised experiments: direct replication plus eWriter and no-notes conditions; small internal meta-analysis. Who: Students; n and setting not given in the abstract.

Finding. "Performance did not consistently differ between any groups", even against students who took no notes; pooled effects favouring longhand were small and non-significant. The original Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014, Psychological Science, 25(6), 1159–1168) found laptop note-takers did worse on conceptual questions in three studies.

Lecture comprehension, not maths; the popular finding did not replicate cleanly.

Claim: "Frequent quizzing makes children anxious"

Agarwal, P. K., D'Antonio, L., Roediger, H. L., McDermott, K. B., & McDaniel, M. A. (2014). Classroom-based programs of retrieval practice reduce middle school and high school students' test anxiety. Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 3(3), 131–139. doi:10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.07.002

Correlational Student survey following classroom retrieval-practice programmes (correlational, self-report). Who: n = 1,408 middle- and high-school students, USA.

Finding. In classes using regular low-stakes retrieval practice, 92% of students said it helped them learn and 72% said it made them less nervous for unit tests and exams.

Not an RCT and no objective anxiety measure; include only as evidence against the "quizzing causes anxiety" claim, not as proof of a reduction.

What this means for Infinity Club

  • Every sheet ends with answers so that a child checks their own work: retrieval with feedback, the same night.
  • The Start-here order mixes the tracks rather than finishing one topic before the next: interleaving and spacing by construction.
  • One worked example before the exercises, with the first exercise a near copy of it: the worked-example effect, then faded guidance.
  • The stretch problem is guided struggle, with the answer printed, not a test. Honest attempts matter more than getting it.
  • Parents are asked to sit nearby and not to say "I was never good at maths". The studies on parental maths anxiety are why.
