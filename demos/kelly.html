Kelly betting

Slide the bet fraction f. Twenty gamblers each start with 1 and bet the fraction f of whatever they have on a coin that comes up heads with probability p = 0.6, paying even money. The left graph shows their wealth over 1,000 rounds on a log scale, with the median gambler in bold. The right graph is the long-run growth rate for every f at once. Find the f that makes the median climb fastest, then push f past 0.5 and watch a favourable game ruin everyone. The same coin flips are reused as you slide, so only your bet size changes.

What's going on

Betting a fixed fraction f means your wealth is multiplied by 1 + f on a win and 1 − f on a loss. After many rounds the order of wins and losses does not matter, only how many of each: wealth = (1 + f)wins(1 − f)losses. Take logs and divide by the number of rounds, and the law of large numbers hands you the growth rate g(f) = p ln(1 + f) + (1 − p) ln(1 − f). Wealth grows like eg·t, so the sign of g decides everything: positive and you get rich, negative and you go broke, no matter how favourable the coin is.

Differentiate: g′(f) = p/(1 + f) − (1 − p)/(1 − f), which is zero at f* = 2p − 1. For p = 0.6 that is f* = 0.2, the Kelly fraction: bet your edge. Anything above 2f* has g below zero. Betting more than Kelly does not just add risk, it lowers your typical growth, because the logarithm punishes big losses more than it rewards big wins.

Why the median and not the mean? The expected wealth is largest when you bet everything every time, (1.2)1000 in the lucky case. But that case has probability 0.61000, and every other gambler is at zero. Expected value is dominated by a miracle nobody will live to see. The median gambler, and in fact almost every gambler, experiences the growth rate g(f) instead. The orange theory line on the left is eg(f)·t, and the bold median hugs it.
