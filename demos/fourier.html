Build a sound from sines

A note at 220 Hz is never just 220 Hz. Real instruments also vibrate at 440, 660, 880 and so on: whole-number multiples called harmonics. The mix of harmonics is what makes a violin sound different from a flute playing the same note. Set the amount of each harmonic, watch the wave they add up to, and press play.

Presets

What's going on

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that any repeating wave, however jagged, is a sum of smooth sine waves whose frequencies are whole-number multiples of the lowest one. Mathematicians were sceptical. He was right. The sliders above are the first eight terms of such a sum, and the picture is one period of the total.

The presets are recipes. A square wave uses only odd harmonics with amplitudes 1, 1/3, 1/5, 1/7, …. A sawtooth uses every harmonic, 1/n, with alternating signs. A triangle uses odd harmonics with amplitudes 1/n² and alternating signs, and because its harmonics fade so fast it sounds much softer than the square. With only eight terms the corners are still wobbly: that wobble is called the Gibbs phenomenon and it never quite goes away however many terms you add, it just gets narrower.

Notice what does not change the sound: flipping the sign of one slider, or shifting a harmonic in time, changes the picture a lot but changes the sound hardly at all. Your ear mostly hears how much of each harmonic is present, not how they line up. That is why the spectrum (the bars) is a better fingerprint of an instrument than the waveform.
