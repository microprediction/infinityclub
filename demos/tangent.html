Secant to tangent

Pick a curve and a point a on it. A second point sits at a + h. The line through the two points is a secant, and its slope is rise over run: (f(a + h) − f(a)) / h. Drag h toward zero. The second point slides into the first and the secant settles onto the tangent. The slope it settles on is the derivative f′(a).

What's going on

You cannot compute the slope of a curve at a single point directly, because a slope needs two points. Calculus gets round this by using two points and then asking what the slope approaches as the second point closes in. For f(x) = x² at a = 1, the secant slope is ((1 + h)² − 1) / h = (2h + h²) / h = 2 + h. As h shrinks, this is clearly heading for 2, and it never needs to arrive: the limit is 2, so f′(1) = 2.

Do the same algebra with a general a and you get 2a + h → 2a. That is the famous result: the derivative of x² is 2x. The table below the graph shows the numbers doing exactly this for whichever curve you pick. For x³ the slopes approach 3a². For sin x they approach cos a. For eˣ they approach eᵃ, the function's own value, which is what makes e special.

Notice h can be negative too: approach from the left and you should get the same answer. If a curve gave different answers from the two sides, it would have a corner there and no tangent. Pick |x| and set a = 0: the two sides disagree, so there is no tangent there.
