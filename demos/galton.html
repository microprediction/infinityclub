The Galton board

Each ball hits twelve pegs and bounces left or right at every one. Where it lands is just the number of rights. Drop a few and it looks random. Drop a thousand and the pile is a bell. The orange curve is the normal distribution with the same mean and spread, and it fits better the more balls you drop.

What's going on

Each peg is a coin flip. Landing in bin k means exactly k of the twelve flips went right, so the chance of bin k is the binomial number C(12, k) / 2¹² when the coin is fair: 1, 12, 66, 220, 495, 792, 924, 792, 495, 220, 66, 12, 1 out of 4096. Those are the numbers in row 12 of Pascal's triangle. The middle bin is 924 times more likely than the edge.

The mean position is 12p and the standard deviation is √(12 p (1 − p)), which for a fair coin is √3 ≈ 1.73. About 68% of the balls land within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two. Those are the numbers that make the normal curve famous, and here you can watch them appear out of nothing but left-or-right.

The deep reason is the central limit theorem: add up many small independent random nudges and the total is approximately normal, whatever the nudges look like individually. Heights, measurement errors and exam marks are bell-shaped for the same reason the balls are. Drag the bias slider to see what happens when the coin is unfair: the pile shifts and squashes but, with enough rows, it is still a bell.
