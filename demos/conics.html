Slicing a cone

Tilt the plane. Flat: a circle. Tilted: an ellipse. Parallel to the cone's side: a parabola. Steeper still: a hyperbola, cutting both cones. One shape, one knife, four curves.

Side view of the cone and the cutting plane

The curve, seen face on, with focus and directrix

What's going on

The cone here has sides at 60° to the horizontal. Call the plane's tilt θ. While θ is less than 60° the plane crosses every line on one cone and the cut closes up: an ellipse, and a circle when θ = 0. At exactly 60° the plane is parallel to one line of the cone, so it never crosses that line and the curve never closes: a parabola. Past 60° the plane is steeper than the cone's side and must cut the upper cone too, giving the two branches of a hyperbola.

One number tells the four apart: the eccentricity e. Every point on the curve is e times as far from a fixed point, the focus, as from a fixed line, the directrix. A circle has e = 0 (the directrix has gone off to infinity), an ellipse has 0 < e < 1, a parabola has e = 1 exactly, and a hyperbola has e > 1. For this cone, e = sin θ ÷ cos 30°, which is why e reaches 1 at θ = 60°.

The right-hand picture draws the curve from that focus-and-directrix rule alone. Change e continuously and the circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola slide into one another. For a hyperbola the picture zooms out, keeping the focus-side vertex pinned near the left edge, so that both branches fit; the readout and the corner label say by how much.

Predict first, then slide. Start at 57° and creep up toward 60°. Where does the far end of the ellipse go? Now start at 63° and creep down toward 60°. Where does the second branch go? Both answers are "off to infinity", from opposite sides: the far vertex leaves to the left, and the second branch arrives from the right. At 60° itself neither is anywhere, which is the parabola. The readout tracks the far vertex's distance from the focus, and you can watch it blow up on either side of 60°.

Planets travel on ellipses with the Sun at a focus. A ball thrown in the air follows a parabola. A comet passing once and leaving forever follows a hyperbola. Whispering galleries, satellite dishes and the shadow of a lampshade on a wall are all conics too.
