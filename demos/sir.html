An epidemic in a box

A town of 1,000 people. Everyone is Susceptible, Infected or Recovered. Each infected person passes the illness to R₀ others over the time they are infectious, but only to people who are still susceptible. Slide R₀, vaccinate some of the town in advance, and watch what happens to the curve.

What's going on

Each day, the number of new infections is proportional to how many infected people there are times how many susceptible people they can meet: new cases each day = (contact rate β) × S × I ÷ N. Each day a fraction γ of the infected recover, where γ = 1 ÷ (days infectious). The ratio β ÷ γ is R₀, the number of people one case infects in a town where everyone is susceptible.

At the start almost everyone is susceptible, so each case causes about R₀ more, and those cause R₀ more again: exponential growth. But every infection removes a susceptible person. Once the susceptible fraction drops below 1 ÷ R₀, each case causes fewer than one new case, and the epidemic turns over. That moment is the peak.

Turn the sentence around and you have the herd immunity threshold: when at least 1 − 1 ÷ R₀ of the town is immune, the illness cannot grow. For R₀ = 3 that is two thirds. The orange line marks it. Vaccination reaches it without anyone falling ill; an uncontrolled epidemic reaches it the hard way, and then overshoots, because the people infected around the peak go on to infect others.

Try R₀ = 3 with 0% vaccinated and note the final size. Then set vaccination to 60%, just under the threshold, and to 70%, just over it. The epidemic does not simply shrink in proportion. It collapses, and the few remaining cases protect the unvaccinated too. That is what "herd" means.
