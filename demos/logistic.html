The logistic map

One rule: replace x by r·x·(1 − x). Start anywhere between 0 and 1 and repeat. For small r the value settles down. Push r past 3 and it starts to flip between two values, then four, then eight, and then, past about 3.57, it never repeats at all. The left picture is a cobweb. Start at x₀ on the bottom axis and go up to the curve: that height is the next x. Go across to the diagonal line, which carries the height back to the bottom axis. Repeat: up, across, up, across. The first step is drawn in blue. The right picture is the same numbers in order.

What's going on

The rule x → r·x·(1 − x) was meant to model a population: next year's size is proportional to this year's (the r·x part) but shrinks as the habitat fills up (the 1 − x part). It is about the simplest nonlinear rule you can write down, and it contains a universe.

For r below 1 everything dies out. Between 1 and 3 the population settles at the fixed point x* = 1 − 1/r, where the curve crosses the diagonal. At r = 3 that point stops attracting and a 2-cycle takes over; at about 3.449 it becomes a 4-cycle, at 3.544 an 8-cycle, and the doublings pile up ever faster until about r = 3.5699, after which the sequence is chaotic: it never repeats, and two starting values that differ in the tenth decimal place end up nowhere near each other. Try x₀ = 0.2 and 0.2001 at r = 4 and compare the time series after thirty steps.

The bifurcation diagram plots, for each r across the bottom, every value the sequence keeps visiting after it has settled. The fork-shaped doublings are easy to see. So is a sudden window of calm at r ≈ 3.83, where a 3-cycle appears out of the chaos. The ratio of successive gaps between doublings approaches 4.669…, Feigenbaum's constant, and the astonishing fact is that this same number appears for almost any hump-shaped rule, not just this one.
