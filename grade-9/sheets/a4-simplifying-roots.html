Why \(\sqrt{50}\) is \(5\sqrt2\)

Pull the square factors out from under the root. Exact answers stay short, and \(\sqrt2\) turns up everywhere.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Pythagoras hands you lengths like \(\sqrt{50}\): the positive number whose square is 50. A calculator says 7.0710678…, which is rounded and hard to recognise. There is an exact form that is shorter.

A square of area 50 has side \(\sqrt{50}\). Cut it into a 5 by 5 grid. Each of the 25 small squares has area 2, so each has side \(\sqrt2\). Five of those sides make the big side: \(\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2\).

\(\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \times \sqrt b\) for positive \(a\) and \(b\).

Why? Square the right-hand side: \(\sqrt a \sqrt b \times \sqrt a \sqrt b = a \times b\). So \(\sqrt a \sqrt b\) is a positive number whose square is \(ab\), and that is exactly what \(\sqrt{ab}\) means.

To simplify a root, split off a square factor: \(\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = \sqrt{25} \times \sqrt2 = 5\sqrt2\).

Roots split over multiplying, not over adding: \(\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\), but \(\sqrt9 + \sqrt{16} = 7\). What you can do is collect like roots, just as \(3x + 5x = 8x\): \(3\sqrt2 + 5\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt2\).

Watch one: a right triangle with legs 4 and 8

By Pythagoras the longest side is \(\sqrt{4^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{80}\).

The biggest square that divides 80 is 16, since \(80 = 16 \times 5\). So \(\sqrt{80} = \sqrt{16} \times \sqrt5 = 4\sqrt5\).

Spotted only the 4? Then \(\sqrt{80} = 2\sqrt{20}\), and \(20 = 4 \times 5\) gives \(2 \times 2\sqrt5 = 4\sqrt5\) again. Keep going until no square factor is left under the root.

Check: \((4\sqrt5)^2 = 16 \times 5 = 80\). The short form also shows something the decimal hides: this side is exactly 4 times \(\sqrt5\), the longest side of the triangle with legs 1 and 2.

Your turn

  1. Simplify: (a) \(\sqrt{12}\); (b) \(\sqrt{45}\); (c) \(\sqrt{72}\); (d) \(\sqrt{200}\).
  2. Simplify: (a) \(\sqrt{18} + \sqrt8\); (b) \(\sqrt{75} - \sqrt{27}\); (c) \(\sqrt6 \times \sqrt{15}\).
  3. (a) A square has side 7. Find its diagonal exactly, in simplest form. (b) Another square has diagonal 10. Find its side exactly.
  4. No calculator. (a) Which is bigger, \(3\sqrt5\) or \(2\sqrt{11}\)? Hint: run the rule backwards and put everything under the root. (b) True or false: \(\sqrt2 + \sqrt8 = \sqrt{18}\). Give a reason.

Stretch: cubes instead of squares

\(\sqrt[3]{n}\), the cube root, is the number whose cube is \(n\); so \(\sqrt[3]{27} = 3\). The same rule holds: \(\sqrt[3]{ab} = \sqrt[3]{a} \times \sqrt[3]{b}\). Simplify \(\sqrt[3]{54}\). Then write \(\sqrt[3]{16} + \sqrt[3]{54}\) as a single cube root.

Answers

  1. (a) \(\sqrt{4 \times 3} = 2\sqrt3\). (b) \(\sqrt{9 \times 5} = 3\sqrt5\). (c) \(\sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt2\). (d) \(\sqrt{100 \times 2} = 10\sqrt2\).
  2. (a) \(3\sqrt2 + 2\sqrt2 = 5\sqrt2\). (b) \(5\sqrt3 - 3\sqrt3 = 2\sqrt3\). (c) \(\sqrt{90} = \sqrt{9 \times 10} = 3\sqrt{10}\).
  3. (a) \(\sqrt{7^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{98} = \sqrt{49 \times 2} = 7\sqrt2\). (b) If the side is \(s\), then \(s^2 + s^2 = 100\), so \(s^2 = 50\) and \(s = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2\), about 7.07.
  4. (a) \(3\sqrt5 = \sqrt9 \times \sqrt5 = \sqrt{45}\) and \(2\sqrt{11} = \sqrt{44}\). So \(3\sqrt5\) is bigger, but only just. (b) True: \(\sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2\), so the left side is \(3\sqrt2 = \sqrt{9 \times 2} = \sqrt{18}\). It is true because of like roots, not because of adding under the root: \(2 + 8\) is not 18.

Stretch. Look for cube factors: \(54 = 27 \times 2\), so \(\sqrt[3]{54} = 3\sqrt[3]{2}\). Likewise \(16 = 8 \times 2\) gives \(\sqrt[3]{16} = 2\sqrt[3]{2}\). The sum is \(5\sqrt[3]{2} = \sqrt[3]{125 \times 2} = \sqrt[3]{250}\).
