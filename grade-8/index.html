∞ Grade 8

For bright 13 and 14 year olds. The grade 8 course explained rather than drilled, ideas the course never reaches, and where all of it shows up in the world. Three tracks: Sharpen (the school course, with reasons), Around the corner (new mathematics), Connections (where it shows up). One sheet a night, ten to fifteen minutes, and a starred problem if you want more.

1Pick a sheetStart anywhere, or follow the first thirty nights. Sharpen sheets make the school course feel easy. Around the corner sheets are new mathematics.
2Print itEach sheet prints on three pages: two for you, and the answers on their own page at the end. Work on paper; the thinking goes better.
3Do the stretchEvery sheet ends with a starred problem that is harder than the rest. You are not expected to get it. You are expected to try.

Sharpen the grade 8 course, explained not drilled

Linear equations and lines, systems, exponents, units, roots, Pythagoras, functions, transformations, angles, scatter plots, percent change, inequalities, probability, expanding and factoring, volume, each with the why and problems a notch harder than school sets.

Sharpen 1

One, none, or infinitely many

Equations with x on both sides. Why 3x + 5 = 3x + 7 has no solution and 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2 has every solution, and how to tell before you finish.
Sharpen 2

Why slope is the same everywhere on a line

Rise over run between any two points on a line gives the same number. Similar triangles are why, and that is what makes y = mx + b work.
Sharpen 3

Reading a line at sight

Intercepts, parallel lines, horizontal and vertical lines, and writing the equation from two points without a formula sheet.
Sharpen 4

Two equations, two unknowns

Substitution, elimination, and the picture: two lines cross once, never, or everywhere.
Sharpen 5

Why a⁰ = 1 and a⁻³ = 1/a³

The exponent laws follow from counting factors, and the strange cases follow from the laws. Then (2³)⁴, 2⁻⁵, and 10⁶ ÷ 10⁹.
Sharpen 6

Multiply by 1

1 km / 1000 m equals 1, so multiplying by it changes nothing but the units. Chain these fractions to convert speeds, prices and areas, and use the units to catch a wrong formula.
Sharpen 7

Squeezing √2

It is between 1.4 and 1.5. Then 1.41 and 1.42. Trap a square root between decimals, then do it faster with the Babylonian method.
Sharpen 8

Pythagoras, distances, and the diagonal of a box

A rearrangement proof you can see, the distance between two points, and the longest pencil that fits in a box.
Sharpen 9

Linear or not?

Read the rate of change from a table, a graph, and a story. Constant change means a line; anything else does not.
Sharpen 10

Slide, flip, turn, scale

Coordinate rules for translations, reflections, rotations and dilations, which ones keep lengths, and what congruent and similar really mean.
Sharpen 11

Why a triangle's angles add to 180°

One parallel line proves it. Then the exterior angle, the angle sum of any polygon, and the angles of a regular 20-gon.
Sharpen 12

Scatter plots and the line of best fit

Plot two variables, draw the trend, read the slope as a rate, and learn what an outlier does to the line.
Sharpen 13

Up 20%, down 20%

Percent changes multiply. Why a 20% rise followed by a 20% fall is a loss, and the one multiplier that undoes any change.
Sharpen 14

Turning 0.272727… back into a fraction

Call it x, multiply by 100, subtract. Every repeating decimal is a fraction, and 0.999… is exactly 1.
Sharpen 15

Why the inequality flips

Multiply both sides by a negative and < becomes >. See why on a number line, then solve linear inequalities without ever being unsure.
Sharpen 16

Turning a story into an equation

Name the unknown, write what you know, solve. Two cyclists, a mixture, and why the average of two speeds is not the average speed.
Sharpen 17

"At least one": the complement trick

P(at least one six in four rolls) is 1 minus P(no sixes). Independence, the multiplication rule, and the gambler who lost to de Méré's dice.
Sharpen 18

Expand and factor

The area model for (a + b)(c + d), the difference of two squares, and why 51 × 49 = 2499 in your head.
Sharpen 19

A cone, a sphere and a cylinder: 1, 2, 3

Same radius, same height. Compute the three volumes and find the ratio Archimedes put on his tomb.

Around the corner new ideas

Euclid's algorithm, divisibility, proofs without words, Platonic solids, expected value, the birthday paradox, a secret agreed in public, fractals, magic squares, unsolved problems, logic, taxicab geometry, trapping π, constructions, strategy stealing, four dimensions, the perfect shuffle.

Corner 1

Euclid's algorithm

The fastest way to a common factor: divide, keep the remainder, repeat. Why it works, and gcd(1071, 462) in four lines.
Corner 2

The Chicken McNugget problem

Boxes of 6, 9 and 20. Which totals can you buy? The largest you cannot is 43, and there is a reason.
Corner 3

Why the digit-sum test works

A number is divisible by 9 when its digits add to a multiple of 9. The reason is that 10 is one more than 9. Then the test for 11.
Corner 4

Proofs without words

1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n², two triangles make a rectangle, and 1³ + 2³ + … + n³ is a perfect square. Each one is a picture.
Corner 5

The last digit of 7²⁰²⁴

Last digits of powers go round in cycles. Find the cycle, use the remainder, and answer in seconds.
Corner 6

Counting divisors, and perfect numbers

360 has 24 divisors, and you can count them without listing. Then 6, 28, 496, and a question open for 2,300 years.
Corner 7

Why there are exactly five Platonic solids

Each corner needs at least three faces and less than 360°. That rule leaves five shapes, and no more.
Corner 8

What a game is worth

Expected value: multiply each outcome by its chance and add. Fair prices, a lottery ticket, and a game that is worth infinity.
Corner 9

The birthday paradox

In a room of 23 people it is more likely than not that two share a birthday. Multiply the chances of no match and watch it fall.
Corner 10

Agreeing on a secret in public

Two people shout numbers across a room and end up with a shared secret nobody else can find. Diffie–Hellman with small numbers, by hand.
Corner 11

The snowflake with an infinite edge

The Koch snowflake: add bumps forever. The perimeter grows without bound while the area stays under 8/5 of the first triangle.
Corner 12

Egyptian fractions

The Egyptians wrote every fraction as a sum of different unit fractions. Fibonacci's greedy method always works. Why?
Corner 13

Magic squares

Why the centre of a 3 × 3 magic square must be 5, and a method that builds a magic square of any odd size.
Corner 14

Problems nobody has solved

Collatz, Goldbach, twin primes, odd perfect numbers. Each takes one minute to state. Test them, and see why they are hard.
Corner 15

Knights, knaves and truth tables

Knights always tell the truth, knaves always lie. Solve the island puzzles, then build the truth tables that solve them all.
Corner 16

The shoelace formula

The area of any polygon from its corner coordinates, by criss-cross multiplying. Why it works for a triangle, then for everything.
Corner 17

Taxicab geometry

Measure distance the way a taxi drives: along the grid. Circles become squares, and some lines have many midpoints.
Corner 18

Four weights that weigh anything to 40

Weights of 1, 3, 9 and 27 on a two-pan balance measure every whole number up to 40. The reason is a number system with digits −1, 0, 1.
Corner 19

Trapping π between polygons

A hexagon inside the circle and one outside give 3 < π < 3.46. Double the sides and the trap tightens. Archimedes got to 3.1408.
Corner 20

Ruler and compass

Bisect a segment and an angle, drop a perpendicular, copy an angle. Each construction is a proof in disguise: congruent triangles.
Corner 21

The mediant

Add tops and add bottoms: (a + c)/(b + d) always lands between a/b and c/d. Why, and the tree that lists every fraction once.
Corner 22

Strategy stealing

In Chomp the first player can always win, and we can prove it without knowing how. The strangest kind of proof.
Corner 23

The Josephus problem

Forty-one people in a circle, every second one leaves. Where do you stand to be last? The answer is a binary trick.
Corner 24

The shortest path bounces

To get from A to B via a wall, reflect B. Heron's problem, why light obeys it, and a pool shot you can compute.
Corner 25

A cube in four dimensions

A point, a segment, a square, a cube. Keep going: the 4-cube has 16 corners, 32 edges and 8 cubes for faces, and you can count them.
Corner 26

The perfect shuffle

Shuffle a 52-card deck perfectly eight times and it is back in order. Follow one card and the arithmetic shows why.
Corner 27

Half-planes and the best corner

Graph y > 2x − 1 as a shaded region. Add constraints, find the feasible region, and the best answer is always at a corner.

Connections barcodes · medicine · ecology · surveying · screens · roads · earthquakes · sport · elections · weather

The same mathematics, found in the wild.

Connections 1

Why a typo in your card number gets caught

ISBNs, barcodes and the Luhn check on credit cards: one extra digit that catches almost every slip.
Connections 2

When both groups improve and the total gets worse

Simpson's paradox at Berkeley, in hospitals and in sport. The numbers are right. The conclusion is not.
Connections 3

Counting fish you cannot see

Tag 50 fish, release them, catch 40, find 4 tagged. Estimate the lake. Proportions, and the same trick for counting words a writer knew.
Connections 4

How tall is that?

A tree, its shadow, and a metre stick. Similar triangles, a mirror on the ground, and a clinometer you can make from a protractor.
Connections 5

Sixteen million colours

Why #FF8800 is orange: hexadecimal, three bytes, and 256³ colours on every screen.
Connections 6

Why speed squared matters

Double your speed and braking distance quadruples. A nonlinear function that road signs get wrong, with the numbers from the Highway Code.
Connections 7

Earthquakes and decibels

Scales that go up by multiplying: one Richter unit is ten times the shaking, ten decibels is ten times the sound energy. Powers of ten in the news.
Connections 8

Will anyone run 100 m in 9 seconds?

Fit a line to world records and extrapolate. In 2004 a journal predicted women would outsprint men by 2156. What went wrong.
Connections 9

Winning with fewer votes

Draw the districts and you choose the winner. Packing, cracking, and a number that measures it: the efficiency gap.
Connections 10

What "70% chance of rain" means

How to judge a forecaster: calibration, the Brier score, and why always saying 50% is not safe.
