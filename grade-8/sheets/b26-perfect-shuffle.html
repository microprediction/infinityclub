The perfect shuffle

Shuffle a 52-card deck perfectly eight times and it is back in order. Follow one card and the arithmetic shows why.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Cut a deck of 52 cards in half, 26 and 26, and interleave the halves perfectly: one card from each in turn. If the top card stays on top, this is an out-shuffle. Card magicians practise it for years. Do it eight times and every card is back where it started.

To see why, number the positions 0 to 51 from the top and follow one card. The top half, positions 0 to 25, lands on the even positions 0, 2, 4, …, 50: the card at position \(p\) goes to \(2p\). The bottom half, positions 26 to 51, fills the odd positions: 26 goes to 1, 27 goes to 3, so \(p\) goes to \(2p - 51\).

After one out-shuffle, the card at position \(p\) moves to \(2p \bmod 51\): the remainder when \(2p\) is divided by 51. The bottom card, position 51, stays put.

"Mod" is clock arithmetic on a clock with 51 hours (grade 6). Each shuffle doubles the position, so after \(k\) shuffles the card sits at \(2^k p \bmod 51\). Every card is home when \(2^k\) leaves remainder 1 on division by 51. And \(2^8 = 256 = 5 \times 51 + 1\). Eight shuffles.

Watch one: the card at position 10

Double, and subtract 51 whenever the answer reaches 51:

\[10 \to 20 \to 40 \to 80 - 51 = 29 \to 58 - 51 = 7 \to 14 \to 28 \to 56 - 51 = 5 \to 10.\]

Eight arrows, and the card is back at 10. It visited eight different positions on the way, and so does almost every card: the trip takes exactly eight steps because 8 is the first power for which \(2^k\) has remainder 1. A smaller deck makes the pattern easy to test by hand. With 8 cards the rule is \(2p \bmod 7\), and \(1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 - 7 = 1\): three shuffles, because \(2^3 = 7 + 1\). Try it with eight playing cards.

Your turn

  1. Follow the card at position 3 through eight out-shuffles of a 52-card deck, writing each position. Check that it comes home.
  2. A deck of 10 cards is out-shuffled; the rule is \(p \to 2p \bmod 9\). How many shuffles bring it back? Explain the answer using a power of 2.
  3. Which positions in the 52-card deck are already home after a single out-shuffle? Which are home after two? (Solve \(2p \equiv p\) and \(4p \equiv p\) modulo 51; remember \(51 = 3 \times 17\).)
  4. (a) List \(2^k \bmod 51\) for \(k = 1, 2, \dots, 7\). Why does this prove that no number of out-shuffles below eight restores the deck? (b) A friend says that after four shuffles the deck is "half way back", so many cards must be home. Which cards actually are? Use \(16p \equiv p\).

Stretch: the in-shuffle

If the top card goes second, it is an in-shuffle. Check that its rule is \(p \to (2p + 1) \bmod 53\) by testing \(p = 0\) and \(p = 26\). Follow the top card for five in-shuffles. If the deck comes home after \(k\) in-shuffles, which number must divide \(2^k - 1\)? Show that \(k = 8\) fails. (It takes 52.)

Answers

  1. \(3 \to 6 \to 12 \to 24 \to 48 \to 96 - 51 = 45 \to 90 - 51 = 39 \to 78 - 51 = 27 \to 54 - 51 = 3\). Home after eight.
  2. Six. \(2^1, \dots, 2^6\) leave remainders 2, 4, 8, 7, 5, 1 on division by 9, and the first remainder 1 is at \(2^6 = 64 = 7 \times 9 + 1\). (Check with a card: \(1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 7 \to 5 \to 1\).)
  3. After one: \(2p \equiv p\) means \(p \equiv 0\), so only position 0, plus the bottom card 51 which never moves. After two: \(4p \equiv p\) means \(3p\) is a multiple of 51, so \(p\) is a multiple of 17: positions 0, 17 and 34 (and 51). Cards 17 and 34 simply swap places each shuffle.
  4. (a) \(2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 13, 26\). None is 1, so after \(k \lt 8\) shuffles the card at position 1 is at \(2^k \ne 1\): the deck is not restored. (b) \(16p \equiv p\) means \(15p\) is a multiple of 51, so \(5p\) is a multiple of 17, so \(p\) is: again only 0, 17, 34 and 51. Nobody else is home. There is no "half way".

Stretch. \(p = 0\) goes to 1 (the top card goes second) and \(p = 26\) goes to \(53 \bmod 53 = 0\) (the first card of the bottom half lands on top), as an in-shuffle should. Top card: \(1 \to 3 \to 7 \to 15 \to 31\). After \(k\) in-shuffles the top card is at \(2^k - 1 \bmod 53\), so coming home needs 53 to divide \(2^k - 1\). \(2^8 - 1 = 255 = 3 \times 5 \times 17\), not a multiple of 53. The first \(k\) that works is 52.
