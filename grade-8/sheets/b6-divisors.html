Counting divisors, and perfect numbers

360 has 24 divisors, and you can count them without listing. Then 6, 28, 496, and a question open for 2,300 years.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Factor first: \(72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2\). A divisor of 72 uses the same primes with exponents no bigger, so it looks like \(2^a \cdot 3^b\) with \(a\) from 0 to 3 and \(b\) from 0 to 2. Four choices for \(a\), three for \(b\), each pair giving a different divisor: \(4 \times 3 = 12\) divisors, shown below as a grid.

A third prime adds a third direction. \(360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5\), so the exponent of 5 is 0 or 1 and there are \(4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24\) divisors. Write \(d(n)\) for the number of divisors of \(n\), counting 1 and \(n\).

If \(n = p^a q^b r^c \cdots\), then \(d(n) = (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)\cdots\). Each exponent can be anything from 0 up to its maximum.

Now add the divisors instead. The divisors of 6 below 6 are 1, 2, 3, and \(1 + 2 + 3 = 6\). A number equal to the sum of its smaller divisors is perfect. Next come 28, 496 and 8128, all even. Euclid gave a recipe for even ones; nobody has ever found an odd one, and whether one exists is still open: see Problems nobody has solved.

Watch one: the smallest number with exactly 12 divisors

Work backwards from the formula. We need exponents whose "plus ones" multiply to 12. The ways to write 12 as a product of numbers bigger than 1 are \(12\), \(6 \times 2\), \(4 \times 3\) and \(3 \times 2 \times 2\). Subtract 1 from each factor to get the exponents, and put the biggest exponents on the smallest primes:

\[2^{11} = 2048, \qquad 2^5 \cdot 3 = 96, \qquad 2^3 \cdot 3^2 = 72, \qquad 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 60.\]

The smallest is 60. Check: \(d(60) = 3 \times 2 \times 2 = 12\).

To check whether a number like 31 or 127 is prime, use the sieve at infinityclub.net/demos/sieve.html.

Your turn

  1. Find \(d(12)\), \(d(100)\) and \(d(1000)\) from the prime factorisations. Then list the divisors of 12 to confirm the first one.
  2. Find \(d(16)\), \(d(36)\) and \(d(50)\). Two of the three are odd. Use the formula to explain exactly which numbers have an odd number of divisors.
  3. \(496 = 2^4 \cdot 31\). How many divisors does it have? List them all, then add every divisor except 496 itself and confirm that 496 is perfect.
  4. Find the smallest number with exactly 16 divisors. (There are five ways to write 16 as a product of factors bigger than 1. Try them all.)

Stretch: Euclid's recipe

Let \(q = 2^p - 1\) be prime and \(N = 2^{p-1} q\). The divisors of \(N\) are \(1, 2, \ldots, 2^{p-1}\) and those same powers times \(q\). Add each row and show all the divisors total exactly \(2N\). Then take \(p = 13\) (8191 is prime) and write down the fifth perfect number.

Answers

  1. \(12 = 2^2 \cdot 3\), so \(d(12) = 3 \times 2 = 6\): the divisors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. \(100 = 2^2 \cdot 5^2\), so \(d(100) = 3 \times 3 = 9\). \(1000 = 2^3 \cdot 5^3\), so \(d(1000) = 4 \times 4 = 16\).
  2. \(d(16) = 5\), \(d(36) = 3 \times 3 = 9\), \(d(50) = 2 \times 3 = 6\). The product \((a+1)(b+1)\cdots\) is odd only when every factor is odd, which means every exponent is even. A number whose exponents are all even is a perfect square. So the numbers with an odd number of divisors are exactly the squares. (Divisors pair up as \(k\) and \(n/k\); only a square has a divisor paired with itself.)
  3. \(d(496) = 5 \times 2 = 10\). Divisors: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 31, 62, 124, 248, 496. The first row adds to 31. The second row, without 496, adds to \(31 \times 15 = 465\). Total \(31 + 465 = 496\).
  4. \(16 = 16 = 8 \times 2 = 4 \times 4 = 4 \times 2 \times 2 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2\), giving \(2^{15} = 32768\), \(2^7 \cdot 3 = 384\), \(2^3 \cdot 3^3 = 216\), \(2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 120\) and \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 210\). The smallest is 120.

Stretch. The first row \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 2^{p-1}\) adds to \(2^p - 1 = q\). The second row is \(q\) times the first, so it adds to \(q^2\). Total \(q + q^2 = q(q + 1) = q \cdot 2^p = 2N\). Every divisor counted, including \(N\), gives \(2N\), so the divisors below \(N\) add to exactly \(N\): perfect. With \(p = 13\): \(N = 2^{12} \times 8191 = 33{,}550{,}336\). Euler later proved every even perfect number has Euclid's form, so finding perfect numbers means finding primes of the form \(2^p - 1\). Only about fifty are known.
