The birthday paradox

In a room of 23 people it is more likely than not that two share a birthday. Multiply the chances of no match and watch it fall.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

There are 365 days in a year, so you might guess you need about 183 people before a shared birthday becomes likely. The real number is 23.

Work out the opposite event, as in the complement trick: the chance that all \(n\) birthdays are different. Line the people up. The first person can have any birthday. The second must avoid one day: probability \(\tfrac{364}{365}\). The third must avoid two: \(\tfrac{363}{365}\). Each new person has one more day to avoid, and we multiply, because each of these must happen in turn.

\[P(\text{all different}) = \frac{365}{365} \cdot \frac{364}{365} \cdot \frac{363}{365} \cdots \frac{365 - n + 1}{365}, \qquad P(\text{shared}) = 1 - P(\text{all different}).\]

Each factor is a little less than 1, and a long product of numbers a little less than 1 shrinks fast. By the 23rd factor the product has dropped below one half.

Why so few? Because matches come from pairs, and 23 people make \(\tfrac{23 \times 22}{2} = 253\) pairs. Each pair has a \(\tfrac{1}{365}\) chance to match, and 253 attempts at a 1-in-365 event is a fair fight. The 183 guess is nearer to a different question, how many people before one shares a birthday with you, and even that takes 253 (question 4).

Watch one: 23 people

Twenty-two factors (the first person's factor is 1):

\[\frac{364}{365} \cdot \frac{363}{365} \cdot \frac{362}{365} \cdots \frac{343}{365} = 0.4927.\]

So \(P(\text{shared}) = 1 - 0.4927 = 0.507\), just over one half. With 22 people the product is 0.524 and the shared chance is 0.476, just under. On a calculator, multiply by \(\tfrac{364}{365}\), then \(\tfrac{363}{365}\), and so on, watching the display shrink.

Simulate 1000 rooms of up to 60 people and compare with the exact curve: infinityclub.net/demos/birthday.html.

Your turn

  1. Find \(P(\text{shared})\) for 3 people and for 4 people, to three decimal places.
  2. How many pairs are there in a room of 23 people? Of 50? Explain in one sentence why the number of pairs, not the number of people, is what matters.
  3. Birth months instead, with 12 equally likely months. Compute \(P(\text{shared month})\) for 4 people and for 5 people. How many people make a shared month more likely than not?
  4. The other question: the chance that at least one of \(n\) other people shares your birthday is \(1 - \left(\tfrac{364}{365}\right)^n\). Use a calculator to show that \(n = 253\) is the first \(n\) where this passes one half. Why is 253 so much bigger than 23?

Stretch: how many matches to expect

Each pair of people matches with probability \(\tfrac{1}{365}\). Using expected value, find the expected number of matching pairs in a class of 30, and in a school of 1000 students. Then explain why a class of 30 can have an expected 1.19 matches but only a 71% chance of any match.

Answers

  1. Three: \(1 - \tfrac{364 \cdot 363}{365^2} = 1 - 0.99180 = 0.008\). Four: \(1 - \tfrac{364 \cdot 363 \cdot 362}{365^3} = 1 - 0.98364 = 0.016\).
  2. \(\tfrac{23 \cdot 22}{2} = 253\) and \(\tfrac{50 \cdot 49}{2} = 1225\). A match is an event between two people, so every pair is a separate chance of a match, and the number of pairs grows roughly like the square of the number of people.
  3. Four people: \(1 - \tfrac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9}{12^4} = 1 - 0.573 = 0.427\). Five: \(1 - \tfrac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{12^5} = 1 - 0.382 = 0.618\). Five people suffice.
  4. \(\left(\tfrac{364}{365}\right)^{252} = 0.5009\) and \(\left(\tfrac{364}{365}\right)^{253} = 0.4995\), so the chance passes one half at 253. Only the \(n\) pairs that include you can count, instead of all \(\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\) pairs. You need about 253 chances at a 1-in-365 event either way; 23 people supply 253 pairs, but 253 "pairs with you" need 253 people.

Stretch. Expected matches \(= \text{pairs} \times \tfrac{1}{365}\). Class of 30: \(\tfrac{30 \cdot 29}{2} = 435\) pairs, so \(435/365 \approx 1.19\). School of 1000: \(\tfrac{1000 \cdot 999}{2} = 499{,}500\) pairs, so about 1368 matching pairs. An expected value is an average: some classes have no match (29%), and the rest average more than one, with some having two or three. The average of 1.19 says nothing about the chance of zero.
