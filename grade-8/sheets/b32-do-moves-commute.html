What is the chance two moves commute?

Pick two symmetries of a triangle at random. Does it matter which you do first? Exactly half the time it does not.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Cut out a paper triangle with three equal sides and trace round it. A symmetry is a move that picks the triangle up and puts it back inside its outline. There are six: do nothing, two turns and three flips.

These moves have three habits. Do one then another, and the result is one of the six. One is do-nothing. Each has an undo among the six.

A group is a set of moves with a way to combine them such that: combining two moves gives a move in the set; the set has a do-nothing move; every move has an undo in the set.

There is a fourth rule: doing \(x\) then \(y\), and then \(z\), must match doing \(x\), and then \(y\) then \(z\). It holds by itself for moves done one after another, and for adding or multiplying numbers.

No rule mentions order. Two moves commute when doing them in either order gives the same result. Tonight you pick two moves at random (each equally likely, repeats allowed) and find the chance they commute.

Watch one: the six moves of a triangle

Write 1, 2, 3 on the corners, on both sides of the paper. Start with 1 at the top, 2 bottom right, 3 bottom left. The moves are \(N\) (do nothing), \(R\) (a third of a turn clockwise), \(S\) (two thirds of a turn clockwise), and the flips \(A\), \(B\), \(C\): turn the paper over across the dashed line through the top, bottom left or bottom right spot. The lines belong to the table. They stay put when the paper moves.

Write \(RA\) for "\(R\) first, then \(A\)", reading left to right. \(R\) sends 3 to the top, 1 to the bottom right, 2 to the bottom left. Then \(A\) swaps the bottom two: 3, 2, 1 (top, right, left). Flip \(C\) alone does the same, so \(RA = C\). The other order: \(A\) gives 1, 3, 2, then \(R\) gives 2, 1, 3, which is \(B\) alone. So \(AR = B\), and \(R\) and \(A\) do not commute. Here is every pair, first move down the side, second move along the top:

then\(N\)\(R\)\(S\)\(A\)\(B\)\(C\)
\(N\)\(N\)\(R\)\(S\)\(A\)\(B\)\(C\)
\(R\)\(R\)\(S\)\(N\)\(C\)\(A\)\(B\)
\(S\)\(S\)\(N\)\(R\)\(B\)\(C\)\(A\)
\(A\)\(A\)\(B\)\(C\)\(N\)\(R\)\(S\)
\(B\)\(B\)\(C\)\(A\)\(S\)\(N\)\(R\)
\(C\)\(C\)\(A\)\(B\)\(R\)\(S\)\(N\)

Check the rules. Every entry is one of the six moves. Row \(N\) and column \(N\) copy the headings, so \(N\) is the do-nothing. Every row contains \(N\) exactly once, and that entry names the undo: \(R\) and \(S\) undo each other, and \(N\) and each flip undo themselves. The six moves form a group.

Your turn

  1. Moves \(x\) and \(y\) commute when \(xy = yx\): the entry in row \(x\), column \(y\) matches the entry in row \(y\), column \(x\). Count the ordered pairs \((x, y)\) that commute, out of all 36. Count \((R, S)\) and \((S, R)\) as two pairs, and include pairs with \(N\) and pairs like \((A, A)\). What is the chance that two random moves commute?
  2. Numbers can play the part of moves. Which of these are groups? If one is not, name a rule that fails. (a) The two numbers \(1\) and \(-1\), combined by multiplying. (b) The whole numbers \(0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\), combined by adding. (c) The odd numbers \(1, 3, 5, \ldots\), combined by adding.
  3. A square has 8 symmetries: four turns (do nothing, a quarter turn, a half turn, a three-quarter turn) and four flips. You may use these facts, or test them on a paper square. Any two turns commute. The half turn commutes with all 8 moves. Each flip commutes with exactly four moves: do-nothing, the half turn, itself, and the one flip whose line is at right angles to its own. For each of the 8 moves, find how many moves commute with it. Then find the chance that two random symmetries of a square commute.
  4. Take only the turns \(N\), \(R\), \(S\) of the triangle. (a) Use the table to check that these three form a group on their own. (b) What is the chance that two random moves from this small group commute? (c) Explain why the chance can never be 0, in any group with a finite number of moves.

Stretch: the five-eighths ceiling

A theorem published by W. H. Gustafson in 1973 says: in a group with a finite number of moves, if some pair of moves does not commute, then the chance that two random moves commute is at most \(\tfrac{5}{8}\). The proof rests on two facts about such a group. Fact 1: a move that fails to commute with some move commutes with at most half of the moves. Fact 2: the moves that commute with every move make up at most a quarter of the group. Check the theorem and both facts on the triangle and on the square. Then suppose a group has \(n\) moves, and \(z\) of them commute with every move. Use the two facts to show that at most \(\tfrac{5}{8}n^2\) of the \(n^2\) ordered pairs commute.

Answers

  1. Go row by row. \(N\) commutes with all 6 moves. \(R\) commutes with \(N\), \(R\), \(S\), which is 3, and so does \(S\). Each flip commutes only with \(N\) and itself, which is 2. Total \(6 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 18\) pairs out of 36, so the chance is \(\tfrac{18}{36} = \tfrac{1}{2}\).
  2. (a) A group. The products are \(1 \times 1 = 1\), \(1 \times (-1) = -1\), \((-1) \times 1 = -1\), \((-1) \times (-1) = 1\), all in the set. The do-nothing is 1, and each number undoes itself. (b) Not a group. Sums stay in the set and 0 is the do-nothing, but 1 has no undo, because that would need \(-1\). Allowing the negative whole numbers too would make a group. (c) Not a group. \(1 + 3 = 4\) is not odd, so combining leaves the set. There is also no do-nothing, since 0 is not odd.
  3. Do-nothing and the half turn: 8 each. Each flip: 4. A quarter turn or three-quarter turn commutes with the 4 turns. It commutes with no flip, because the four moves that commute with a flip do not include it. So 4 each. Total \(8 + 8 + 4 + 4 + 4 \times 4 = 40\) pairs out of \(8 \times 8 = 64\), and the chance is \(\tfrac{40}{64} = \tfrac{5}{8}\).
  4. (a) The top left 3 by 3 corner of the table contains only \(N\), \(R\), \(S\), so combining stays in the set. \(N\) is there, \(R\) and \(S\) undo each other, and \(N\) undoes itself. (b) That corner is the same when you swap rows with columns, so all 9 pairs commute and the chance is 1. (c) Do-nothing commutes with every move \(x\), because \(Nx\) and \(xN\) are both \(x\). So at least the pair \((N, N)\) commutes, and the chance is more than 0. Every pair \((x, x)\) commutes too.

Stretch. Triangle: \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) is below \(\tfrac{5}{8}\). Only \(N\) commutes with everything, and 1 is less than a quarter of 6. \(R\) and \(S\) commute with 3 moves, exactly half, and the flips with 2. Square: \(\tfrac{5}{8}\) exactly. Two moves of 8 commute with everything, exactly a quarter, and the other six each commute with 4, exactly half. Now the count. Each of the \(z\) moves commutes with all \(n\). Each of the other \(n - z\) commutes with at most \(\tfrac{n}{2}\), by Fact 1. So the number of commuting pairs is at most \(zn + (n - z)\tfrac{n}{2} = \tfrac{n^2}{2} + \tfrac{zn}{2}\). By Fact 2, \(z\) is at most \(\tfrac{n}{4}\), so this is at most \(\tfrac{n^2}{2} + \tfrac{n^2}{8} = \tfrac{5}{8}n^2\). The square reaches the ceiling, so \(\tfrac{5}{8}\) cannot be lowered. We used the two facts without proving them.
