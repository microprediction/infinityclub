Multiplying is rotating

Complex numbers as arrows: multiply to rotate and scale, De Moivre, and the n roots of 1 sitting on a circle.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

You met \(i\), with \(i^2 = -1\), as a number that lives off the number line (the grade 6 sheet Imaginary numbers are real introduced it as a point in the plane). Tonight we treat every complex number \(z = a + bi\) as an arrow from the origin to the point \((a, b)\), and describe the arrow two ways.

The first way is the pair \((a, b)\). The second is its modulus \(r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\), the length of the arrow, together with its argument \(\theta\), the angle it makes with the positive real axis. Trigonometry converts between them: \(a = r\cos\theta\), \(b = r\sin\theta\), so

\(z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\). We abbreviate \(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) as \(\operatorname{cis}\theta\).

Now the fact that makes complex numbers worth having. Take \(z = r\operatorname{cis}\alpha\) and \(w = s\operatorname{cis}\beta\) and multiply:

\[ zw = rs(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = rs\big[(\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta)\big]. \]

The brackets are the angle-addition formulas: \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)\) and \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)\). So \(zw = rs\operatorname{cis}(\alpha + \beta)\).

To multiply complex numbers, multiply the lengths and add the angles. Multiplying by \(w\) rotates the plane by \(\arg w\) and scales it by \(|w|\).

Multiplying by \(i\) (length 1, angle 90°) is a quarter turn. That is why \(i^2 = -1\): two quarter turns point you backwards. Repeat the rule \(n\) times and you get De Moivre's theorem: \((r\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)\). Powers that would take pages of algebra become one multiplication and one addition.

Watch one: a famous coincidence, and a big power

\((2 + i)(3 + i) = 6 + 2i + 3i + i^2 = 5 + 5i\). Check the rule. Lengths: \(\sqrt5 \times \sqrt{10} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2\), and \(|5 + 5i| = \sqrt{50}\). Angles: \(2 + i\) sits at \(\arctan\tfrac12 \approx 26.57°\), \(3 + i\) at \(\arctan\tfrac13 \approx 18.43°\), and \(5 + 5i\) at exactly 45°. So \(\arctan\tfrac12 + \arctan\tfrac13 = 45°\), a genuine identity, proved by one multiplication.

Now \((1 + i)^8\). Length \(\sqrt2\), angle 45°. De Moivre: length \((\sqrt2)^8 = 16\), angle \(8 \times 45° = 360°\), which is the same direction as 0°. So \((1+i)^8 = 16\). Try doing that by expanding brackets.

Your turn

  1. Find the modulus and argument of \(3 + 4i\), of \(-1 + i\), and of \(-2i\). Sketch all three arrows.
  2. Write \(\sqrt3 + i\) in the form \(r\operatorname{cis}\theta\), then compute \((\sqrt3 + i)^6\) with De Moivre. Give the answer in the form \(a + bi\).
  3. The point \((2, 1)\) is the complex number \(2 + i\). Rotate it 90° anticlockwise about the origin by multiplying by the right complex number. Then rotate the original point by 60° instead (multiply by \(\operatorname{cis}60° = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\)) and give the new coordinates to 3 decimal places.
  4. Solve \(z^3 = -8\). (Hint: \(-8\) has length 8 and angle 180°, but also angle 540° and 900°. Divide each by 3.) Give all three answers in the form \(a + bi\), and check one of the non-real ones by cubing it directly.
  5. Solve \(z^4 = -16\). Plot the four solutions. What shape do they make?
  6. Prove \(|zw| = |z|\,|w|\) without angles: with \(z = a + bi\) and \(w = c + di\), expand \((ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2\) and show it equals \((a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2)\). Then use this to write \(65 = 5 \times 13\) as a sum of two squares in two different ways, starting from \(5 = 2^2 + 1^2\) and \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\).

Stretch: where the golden ratio hides in a pentagon

Let \(z = \operatorname{cis}72°\), so \(z^5 = 1\) and the five powers \(1, z, z^2, z^3, z^4\) are the corners of a regular pentagon. Since \(z^5 - 1 = (z - 1)(1 + z + z^2 + z^3 + z^4)\) and \(z \ne 1\), you know \(1 + z + z^2 + z^3 + z^4 = 0\).

Let \(w = z + z^4\) and \(w' = z^2 + z^3\). Explain why \(w = 2\cos72°\) and \(w' = 2\cos144°\) (what is \(z^4\) compared with \(z\)?). Compute \(w + w'\) and \(w w'\) using only \(z^5 = 1\) and the sum above. You should find that \(w\) and \(w'\) are the two roots of \(t^2 + t - 1 = 0\). Solve it, decide which root is \(w\), and deduce an exact value for \(\cos72°\). Check it on a calculator.

Answers

  1. \(3 + 4i\): modulus 5, argument \(\arctan\tfrac43 \approx 53.13°\). \(-1 + i\): modulus \(\sqrt2\), argument 135°. \(-2i\): modulus 2, argument \(-90°\) (or 270°).
  2. \(r = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2\), \(\theta = 30°\), so \(\sqrt3 + i = 2\operatorname{cis}30°\). Then \((\sqrt3 + i)^6 = 2^6\operatorname{cis}180° = 64 \times (-1) = -64\).
  3. Multiply by \(i\): \(i(2 + i) = 2i + i^2 = -1 + 2i\), the point \((-1, 2)\). By 60°: \((\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i)(2 + i) = 1 + \tfrac12 i + \sqrt3\, i + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i^2 = (1 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) + (\tfrac12 + \sqrt3)i \approx 0.134 + 2.232i\), the point \((0.134, 2.232)\). Both have length \(\sqrt5\), as they must.
  4. Length \(\sqrt[3]{8} = 2\); angles \(180°/3 = 60°\), \(540°/3 = 180°\), \(900°/3 = 300°\). Solutions: \(2\operatorname{cis}60° = 1 + \sqrt3\, i\), \(2\operatorname{cis}180° = -2\), \(2\operatorname{cis}300° = 1 - \sqrt3\, i\). Check: \((1 + \sqrt3 i)^2 = 1 + 2\sqrt3 i - 3 = -2 + 2\sqrt3 i\); times \((1 + \sqrt3 i)\): \(-2 - 2\sqrt3 i + 2\sqrt3 i + 2\sqrt3^2 i^2 = -2 - 6 = -8\).
  5. Length \(\sqrt[4]{16} = 2\); angles \(45°, 135°, 225°, 315°\). Solutions \(\pm\sqrt2 \pm \sqrt2\, i\) (all four sign choices). They are the corners of a square of side 2 centred at the origin, tilted 45°.
  6. \((ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2 = a^2c^2 - 2abcd + b^2d^2 + a^2d^2 + 2abcd + b^2c^2 = a^2c^2 + a^2d^2 + b^2c^2 + b^2d^2 = (a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2)\). Since \(|zw|^2 = |z|^2|w|^2\), take square roots. For 65: \((2 + i)(3 + 2i) = 4 + 7i\), so \(65 = 4^2 + 7^2\); and \((2 + i)(3 - 2i) = 8 - i\), so \(65 = 8^2 + 1^2\). (This is why a product of two sums of two squares is always a sum of two squares.)

Stretch. \(z^4 = z^{-1} = \operatorname{cis}(-72°)\), the mirror image of \(z\) in the real axis, so \(z + z^4 = 2\cos72°\); likewise \(z^2 + z^3 = 2\cos144°\). \(w + w' = z + z^2 + z^3 + z^4 = -1\). \(ww' = z^3 + z^4 + z^6 + z^7 = z^3 + z^4 + z + z^2 = -1\) (using \(z^5 = 1\)). Two numbers with sum \(-1\) and product \(-1\) are the roots of \(t^2 + t - 1 = 0\), so \(t = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt5}{2}\). \(w = 2\cos72°\) is positive, so \(2\cos72° = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2}\) and \(\cos72° = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{4} \approx 0.309\). The other root, \(-\frac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} = -\varphi\), is minus the golden ratio: \(2\cos144° = -\varphi\). The pentagon's diagonals are \(\varphi\) times its sides for exactly this reason.
