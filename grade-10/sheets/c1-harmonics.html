Why a violin doesn't sound like a flute

Harmonics, Fourier's idea that every sound is a sum of sines, and beats between two close notes.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A violin and a flute both play the A below middle C. A tuner says both are 220 Hz; your ear says they are obviously different. Same pitch, different timbre.

A string of length \(L\) fixed at both ends can only vibrate in shapes that are still at the ends. Each allowed shape is a mode; a point that stays still is a node. One hump: half a wavelength fits, \(\lambda_1 = 2L\). Two humps: \(\lambda_2 = L\). In general \(\lambda_n = 2L/n\).

Waves on the string travel at speed \(v\), and frequency is speed over wavelength, so

\(f_n = \dfrac{v}{\lambda_n} = n \cdot \dfrac{v}{2L} = n f_1.\) The allowed frequencies are whole-number multiples of the lowest: the harmonics.

A real string does several modes at once. The lowest, \(f_1\), is the pitch you name; the rest colour it. A flute is close to a pure sine; a bowed violin is rich in upper harmonics, so it sounds bright. A clarinet's closed pipe removes the even harmonics, and the missing ones are what makes it sound hollow.

Fourier's theorem (1807): any repeating sound with period \(T\) is a sum of sines at frequencies \(1/T, 2/T, 3/T, \ldots\), each with its own amplitude. That list of amplitudes, the spectrum, is the whole of timbre. Build one at infinityclub.net/demos/fourier.html.

Watch one: the harmonics of A = 220 Hz, and beats

A string at 220 Hz also contains 440, 660, 880 and 1100 Hz. 440 is A an octave up. 660 is 3:2 above 440, the interval musicians call a perfect fifth: E. 880 is A again; 1100, a major third above that, is roughly C♯. Together: an A major chord.

Two flutes play 440 Hz and 444 Hz. With \(\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\frac{A-B}{2}\):

\[\sin(2\pi\cdot 440 t) + \sin(2\pi\cdot 444 t) = 2\cos(2\pi\cdot 2t)\,\sin(2\pi\cdot 442 t).\]

You hear 442 Hz swelling and fading with the slow cosine. Loudness depends on size, not sign, and a cosine passes through zero twice per cycle, so the 2 Hz cosine gives 4 quiet moments a second: 4 beats a second, the difference of the frequencies. Piano tuners tune by beats.

Your turn

  1. The low A string of a guitar is 65 cm long and sounds 110 Hz. List its first six harmonics, the wavelength of each of the first three modes, and the speed of waves on the string.
  2. Guitarists touch a string lightly to force a node there. Touch it at its midpoint: which harmonic sounds, at what frequency? At one third of its length? At one quarter? Why does one quarter not give the second harmonic?
  3. You hold a 440 Hz tuning fork against a string and hear 3 beats per second. You tighten the string a little and the beats slow to 1 per second. Was the string at 437 Hz or 443 Hz before? Explain.
  4. Notes at 300 Hz and 303 Hz are played together. Write the sum of the two sines as a product, state the frequency you hear and the number of beats per second.

Stretch: a square wave, and a formula for \(\pi\)

Fourier's recipe for the square wave that jumps between \(+1\) and \(-1\) is

\[\text{square}(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin x + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} + \frac{\sin 5x}{5} + \cdots\right).\]

Put \(x = \pi/2\), where the square wave equals 1 and every sine is \(\pm1\). Rearrange into a formula for \(\pi\), and add the first five terms. How close are you?

Answers

  1. 110, 220, 330, 440, 550, 660 Hz. Wavelengths \(2L/n\): 130 cm, 65 cm, 43.3 cm. Speed \(v = f\lambda = 110 \times 1.30 = 143\) m/s.
  2. Midpoint: 2nd harmonic, 220 Hz. One third: 3rd, 330 Hz. One quarter: 4th, 440 Hz. The 2nd mode's only interior still point is at \(L/2\); the lowest mode that is still at \(L/4\) is the 4th, and that is the one that sounds.
  3. 437 Hz. Tightening raises the pitch. A string at 443 would move further from 440 and the beats would speed up. At 437 it moves to 439, and \(440 - 439 = 1\) beat per second.
  4. \(\sin(2\pi\cdot300t) + \sin(2\pi\cdot303t) = 2\cos(2\pi\cdot1.5t)\sin(2\pi\cdot301.5t)\). You hear 301.5 Hz, and the envelope \(|\cos(2\pi\cdot 1.5t)|\) peaks 3 times a second: 3 beats per second.

Stretch. \(1 = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \frac17 + \cdots\right)\), so \(\pi = 4\left(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots\right)\), Leibniz's series. Five terms: \(0.8349 \times 4 = 3.340\). Ten terms give 3.042. The error after \(n\) terms is about \(1/n\), so nobody computes \(\pi\) this way, but a sound wave knows about \(\pi\).
