Why a violin doesn't sound like a flute

Harmonics, Fourier's idea that every sound is a sum of sines, and beats between two close notes.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Play the A below middle C on a violin and on a flute. A tuner says both are 220 Hz. Your ear says they are obviously different instruments. Same pitch, different timbre. Tonight is about where timbre comes from, and the answer is a piece of mathematics that also runs your phone, your MRI scanner and JPEG.

Start with a string of length \(L\) fixed at both ends. It can only vibrate in shapes that are still at the ends. The simplest is one hump: half a wavelength fits on the string, so \(\lambda_1 = 2L\). Next, two humps with a still point in the middle: \(\lambda_2 = L\). In general \(n\) humps give \(\lambda_n = 2L/n\). A wave on the string travels at some speed \(v\), and frequency is speed over wavelength, so

\(f_n = \dfrac{v}{\lambda_n} = n \cdot \dfrac{v}{2L} = n f_1.\) The allowed frequencies are exact whole-number multiples of the lowest one. These are the harmonics.

A real plucked or bowed string does not pick one shape. It does several at once, each with its own strength. The lowest, \(f_1\), is the pitch you name. The rest colour it. A flute's note is close to a pure sine: strong fundamental, weak upper harmonics. A violin's bowed string is rich in upper harmonics, which is why it sounds bright and edgy. A clarinet is a pipe closed at one end, which kills the even-numbered harmonics, and that hollow odd-harmonics-only recipe is exactly the clarinet sound.

Here is the deep part, due to Joseph Fourier (1807). Any repeating sound with period \(T\) can be written as a sum of sines and cosines whose frequencies are \(1/T, 2/T, 3/T, \ldots\), each with its own amplitude. The list of amplitudes is the sound's recipe, its spectrum. Two instruments playing the same note have the same list of frequencies and different lists of amplitudes. That is the whole of timbre. (Fourier's claim needs care for signals with jumps, which is why mathematicians spent a century on it, but for the smooth periodic waves of music it holds exactly.)

Play with the recipe at infinityclub.net/demos/fourier.html: slide the harmonics up and down and listen. The grade 6 sheet Why there are twelve notes explains why the ratios 2:1 and 3:2 sound good; tonight explains why the instruments differ.

Watch one: the harmonics of A = 220 Hz

A string sounding 220 Hz also contains 440, 660, 880, 1100, 1320 and 1540 Hz. Which notes are those? 440 is A, one octave up (ratio 2). 660 is a ratio of 3:2 above 440, a perfect fifth: E. 880 is A again. 1100 is \(5 \times 220\), a ratio of 5:4 above 880, a major third: roughly C♯. So the first five harmonics spell out an A major chord. That is not a coincidence; it is why a major chord sounds "built in".

Roughly C♯, because a piano's C♯6 is \(220 \times 2^{28/12} = 1108.7\) Hz, not 1100. The harmonic is about 14 cents flat of the piano note (a cent is a hundredth of a semitone, \(1200\log_2\) of the ratio). The seventh harmonic, 1540 Hz, is 31 cents flat of the nearest piano key, which is why it sounds out of tune and why nobody builds chords on it.

Now two flutes play 440 Hz and 444 Hz together. Using the identity \(\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\frac{A-B}{2}\) with \(A = 2\pi\cdot 440t\) and \(B = 2\pi\cdot 444t\):

\[\sin(2\pi\cdot 440 t) + \sin(2\pi\cdot 444 t) = 2\cos(2\pi\cdot 2t)\,\sin(2\pi\cdot 442 t).\]

You hear a 442 Hz note whose loudness swells and fades with the slow \(\cos(2\pi \cdot 2t)\) factor. That cosine passes through zero twice per cycle, and loudness depends on its size, not its sign, so the note pulses 4 times a second: the difference of the frequencies. These pulses are beats, and they are how every piano tuner and orchestral player tunes.

Your turn

  1. The low A string of a guitar is 65 cm long and sounds 110 Hz. List its first six harmonics. Give the wavelengths on the string of the first three modes, and the speed of waves on that string.
  2. Guitarists touch a string lightly (without pressing) to force a still point. Touch it at its midpoint: which harmonic sounds, and at what frequency? At one third of its length? At one quarter? Explain why touching at one quarter does not give the second harmonic even though a quarter is "closer to the end".
  3. A piano is tuned so that each semitone multiplies frequency by \(2^{1/12}\). The piano's E above A440 is \(440 \times 2^{7/12}\) Hz; the string's third harmonic suggests 660 Hz. Compute both, and the gap in cents, \(1200\log_2(\text{ratio})\). Do the same for the piano's C♯ (\(440 \times 2^{4/12}\)) against the natural major third \(440 \times 5/4\). Which is the bigger compromise?
  4. You hold a 440 Hz tuning fork against a string and hear 3 beats per second. You tighten the string a little and the beats slow to 1 per second. Was the string at 437 Hz or 443 Hz before you tightened it? Explain.
  5. Two notes at \(f_1 = 300\) Hz and \(f_2 = 303\) Hz are played together. Write the sum of the two sines as a product using the identity above, state the frequency you hear and the number of beats per second, and sketch one second of the loudness envelope.
  6. A pipe open at both ends behaves like the string: \(f_n = n v/(2L)\). A pipe closed at one end only allows odd harmonics, \(f_n = n v/(4L)\) with \(n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots\) Take \(v = 343\) m/s and \(L = 0.5\) m. List the first three frequencies of each pipe. Which is like a flute and which like a clarinet? Why does a clarinet play lower than a flute of about the same length?

Stretch: a square wave, and a formula for \(\pi\)

The square wave (a signal that jumps between \(+1\) and \(-1\)) is the harshest periodic sound, and Fourier's recipe for it is famous:

\[\text{square}(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin x + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} + \frac{\sin 5x}{5} + \frac{\sin 7x}{7} + \cdots\right).\]

Only odd harmonics, with amplitudes falling like \(1/n\). Now put \(x = \pi/2\), where the square wave equals 1. Every sine becomes \(\pm 1\). Write out what the equation says and rearrange it into a formula for \(\pi\). Add up the first five terms and multiply by 4. How close are you? (This series is correct but painfully slow. Ten terms get you 3.04.)

In the demo, set the amplitudes to \(1, 0, 1/3, 0, 1/5, 0, 1/7\) and watch the waveform try to become square. The little ears at the corners that never go away are called the Gibbs phenomenon, and they are real.

Answers

  1. 110, 220, 330, 440, 550, 660 Hz. Wavelengths \(2L/n\): 130 cm, 65 cm, 43.3 cm. Speed \(v = f\lambda = 110 \times 1.30 = 143\) m/s.
  2. Midpoint: 2nd harmonic, 220 Hz. One third: 3rd harmonic, 330 Hz. One quarter: 4th harmonic, 440 Hz. A still point at \(L/4\) is allowed for the 4th mode (still points every \(L/4\)) and the 8th, but not the 2nd, whose only interior still point is at \(L/2\). The lowest mode that is still at the touched point is the one that sounds.
  3. Piano E: \(440 \times 2^{7/12} = 659.26\) Hz against 660, a gap of \(1200\log_2(660/659.26) \approx 2\) cents. Piano C♯: \(554.37\) Hz against \(550\), a gap of about 13.7 cents. The major third is the bigger compromise, almost seven times the fifth's error. This is why equal-temperament thirds sound slightly restless to a trained ear and fifths do not.
  4. 437 Hz. Tightening raises the pitch. A string at 443 would move further from 440 and the beats would speed up. A string at 437 moves to 439, and 440 − 439 = 1 beat per second.
  5. \(\sin(2\pi\cdot300t) + \sin(2\pi\cdot303t) = 2\cos(2\pi\cdot1.5t)\sin(2\pi\cdot301.5t)\). You hear 301.5 Hz. The envelope \(|\cos(2\pi\cdot 1.5t)|\) peaks 3 times per second: 3 beats per second. The sketch is a fast wave inside a slow bulging outline with three bulges in one second.
  6. Open pipe: 343, 686, 1029 Hz (all harmonics). Closed pipe: 171.5, 514.5, 857.5 Hz (odd harmonics only). The open pipe is the flute, the closed pipe the clarinet (the reed end is effectively closed). The closed pipe's fundamental fits a quarter wavelength instead of a half, so its lowest note is an octave lower for the same length.

Stretch. At \(x = \pi/2\): \(1 = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \frac17 + \cdots\right)\), so \(\pi = 4\left(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \frac17 + \cdots\right)\). This is Leibniz's series. Five terms: \(1 - 0.3333 + 0.2 - 0.1429 + 0.1111 = 0.8349\), times 4 is 3.340. Ten terms give 3.042. The error after \(n\) terms is about \(1/n\), which is why nobody computes \(\pi\) this way, and why it is still astonishing that a sound wave knows about \(\pi\).
