Information and entropy

How many yes/no questions does a message need? Bits, Huffman codes, and why compression has a limit.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

In 1948 Claude Shannon asked: how much information is in a message? Start with a game. If I think of a number from 1 to 16, you can find it with four yes/no questions, each halving what is left, because \(2^4 = 16\). One yes/no answer is a bit, and a choice among \(N\) equally likely things is worth \(\log_2 N\) bits.

Now the clever step. If the possibilities are not equally likely, a coin that lands heads 90% of the time is less surprising than a fair one, so its flips should need fewer bits to write down. Shannon set the information in an outcome of probability \(p\) at \(\log_2(1/p)\), and the average per symbol is the entropy:

\(\displaystyle H = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{1}{p_i}\) bits per symbol.

A fair coin has \(H = 1\) bit. The 90% coin has \(H \approx 0.469\) bits: you can store a thousand of its flips in about 469 bits, and you can never average fewer. Entropy is the exact price of a message.

Watch one: a Huffman code

Five letters have frequencies A 40%, B 30%, C 15%, D 10%, E 5%. A fixed 3-bit code costs 300 bits per 100 letters. Huffman's recipe: repeatedly merge the two least likely symbols. \(D + E = 15\%\); then C and DE make 30%; then B and CDE make 60%; then A joins. Reading the merges as a tree (left 0, right 1):

A = 0, B = 10, C = 110, D = 1110, E = 1111.

No codeword starts another, so 0100110 splits only one way: A B A C. Average length \(0.4(1) + 0.3(2) + 0.15(3) + 0.1(4) + 0.05(4) = 2.05\) bits, so about 205 bits per 100 letters. The entropy is \(H \approx 2.009\): Huffman is within 0.04 bits of a floor no code can beat.

Your turn

  1. With twenty yes/no questions, how many equally likely possibilities can you tell apart? How many questions identify one of 52 cards for certain, and why isn't the answer \(\log_2 52\) exactly?
  2. Compute the entropy of a fair six-sided die, to three decimal places, and of a coin that lands heads 75% of the time. Which is more uncertain?
  3. A weather station reports sunny \(\tfrac12\), cloudy \(\tfrac14\), rain \(\tfrac18\), snow \(\tfrac18\). Build a Huffman code, then compute its average length and the entropy. Why do they agree exactly here when they did not in the example?
  4. Twelve coins. One of twelve coins is counterfeit, heavier or lighter; a balance scale has three outcomes per weighing. How many possibilities are there? Use information to prove two weighings cannot be enough.

Stretch: why no program can compress everything

A company advertises a compressor that shrinks any file. Count the files of exactly \(n\) bits and the files shorter than \(n\) bits. Why does the pigeonhole principle prove the advertisement false?

Answers

  1. \(2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576\). For 52 cards, \(\log_2 52 \approx 5.70\), so five questions (32 outcomes) are not enough and six (64) are. Questions come in whole numbers, so round up; the fraction means some branches of your question tree finish early.
  2. Die: \(\log_2 6 \approx 2.585\) bits. The 75% coin: \(0.75\log_2(4/3) + 0.25\log_2 4 \approx 0.311 + 0.5 = 0.811\) bits. The die is far more uncertain.
  3. Merge rain and snow (\(\tfrac14\)), then with cloudy (\(\tfrac12\)), then with sunny. Code: sunny 0, cloudy 10, rain 110, snow 111. Average length \(\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3) = 1.75\), and the entropy is also 1.75. They agree because every probability is a power of \(\tfrac12\), so each ideal length \(\log_2(1/p)\) is a whole number and Huffman can hit it exactly.
  4. \(12 \times 2 = 24\) possibilities (which coin, heavy or light). Two weighings have \(3^2 = 9\) outcomes and \(9 \lt 24\), so some outcome would cover several possibilities. Three weighings give \(27 \ge 24\), so the bound allows it, and a strategy does exist. In bits: \(\log_2 24 \approx 4.58\), each weighing gives at most \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\), so at least three.

Stretch. There are \(2^n\) files of length \(n\) and \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 2^{n-1} = 2^n - 1\) shorter files. A compressor that shrinks every \(n\)-bit file sends \(2^n\) inputs into \(2^n - 1\) outputs, so two inputs share an output and cannot both be recovered. Compression works in practice because photos and text are far from random and live in the small compressible corner.
