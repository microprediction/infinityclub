Perspective, projective geometry and the golden ratio myth

Vanishing points, why parallel lines meet, and a sceptical look at φ in art.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Put your eye at the origin, look along the \(z\)-axis, and hang a sheet of glass at distance \(d\) in front of you. A point \((X, Y, Z)\) in the world is seen where the line from your eye to it pierces the glass. By similar triangles that is

\[ x = \frac{dX}{Z}, \qquad y = \frac{dY}{Z}. \] Everything about perspective is this one fact: divide by depth.

Take a railway track running straight away from you. Its two rails are the lines \((\pm 1, -h, Z)\) for all \(Z\), where \(h\) is the height of your eye above the ground. Their images are \((\pm d/Z, -dh/Z)\). As \(Z\) grows both coordinates shrink toward 0. The rails, which never meet in the world, meet in the picture at the point \((0, 0)\): the vanishing point.

This works for any family of parallel lines. A line through \(P\) in direction \((a, b, c)\) with \(c \ne 0\) is \(P + t(a, b, c)\). Its image as \(t \to \infty\) tends to \(\left(\dfrac{da}{c}, \dfrac{db}{c}\right)\), which does not depend on \(P\) at all. Every line in that direction heads to the same point. Horizontal directions have \(b = 0\), so all their vanishing points lie on \(y = 0\): that is the horizon, always at eye level. Directions with \(c = 0\) run parallel to the glass and never converge; that is why vertical edges stay vertical in an ordinary photograph.

Renaissance painters worked this out around 1420 (Brunelleschi, then Alberti wrote the rules down). Mathematicians later took it further: pretend that each direction really does have a "point at infinity" where its parallel lines meet, and add those points to the plane. In this projective plane any two lines meet in exactly one point, with no exception for parallels, and perspective drawing is just a map from one plane to another. Lengths and angles are not preserved. What is preserved is a stranger quantity, the cross-ratio of four points on a line, which you will meet in question 5.

Push the glass to an extreme angle and you get anamorphosis: a picture that looks like a smear from the front and snaps into shape from one specific spot. Holbein's The Ambassadors (1533) has a skull across the floor that only resolves when you stand to the right of the painting; pavement artists do the same trick with chalk.

And the golden ratio? \(\varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2 \approx 1.618\) is a genuinely interesting number. The claims that the Parthenon, the pyramids and the Mona Lisa were built around it are not supported by measurement; they mostly date from the 19th century and survive by drawing rectangles generously. Mathematician George Markowsky went through the famous examples in 1992 and found none that held up. Where \(\varphi\) really does appear is in the regular pentagon (the stretch) and in the spiral counts of sunflowers and pinecones, which you met in Fibonacci.

Watch one: the floor tiles

Eye height \(h = 1.6\) m, glass at \(d = 1\) m. A line across the floor at depth \(Z = 4\) m has \(Y = -1.6\), so its image is at \(y = -1.6/4 = -0.4\). At \(Z = 8\): \(y = -0.2\). At \(Z = 16\): \(y = -0.1\). Doubling the distance halves the drop below the horizon, so equally spaced floor lines bunch up as they recede.

A 2 m fence post at \(Z = 4\) runs from \(Y = -1.6\) to \(Y = 0.4\). Its image runs from \(-0.4\) to \(0.1\): half a metre tall on the glass. At \(Z = 8\) it is a quarter of a metre. Image size is inversely proportional to distance, which is why a person twice as far away looks half as tall, and why the top of the post is above the horizon when the post is taller than you.

Your turn

  1. With \(d = 1\), find the image of \((3, 2, 6)\), of \((3, 2, 12)\) and of \((3, 2, 24)\). These three points lie on a line parallel to the \(z\)-axis. Where are their images heading?
  2. Find the vanishing point (\(d = 1\)) of the lines in direction \((1, 0, 2)\); of the lines in direction \((1, 1, 1)\); of the lines in direction \((1, 0, 0)\). Which of these lie on the horizon, and what happens to the third family?
  3. Railway sleepers lie across the track at depths \(Z = 3, 6, 9, 12\) m. Your eye is 1.5 m above the rails and \(d = 1\). Find the four image heights \(y\). Then find the three gaps between consecutive images. Equal spacing in the world becomes what kind of spacing in the picture?
  4. A box sits on the floor turned 45° to you, so its horizontal edges run in directions \((1, 0, 1)\) and \((-1, 0, 1)\), and its vertical edges in direction \((0, 1, 0)\). With \(d = 1\), find the vanishing points of the two horizontal families. What happens to the vertical edges? How far apart are the two vanishing points if the artist stands further from the canvas, say \(d = 3\)?
  5. Cross-ratio. For four points \(A, B, C, D\) in order on a line, define \((A, B; C, D) = \dfrac{AC \cdot BD}{AD \cdot BC}\). For the sleepers in question 3 (positions 3, 6, 9, 12 along the track) compute it in the world. Now compute it from the four image heights you found. Perspective ruins distances; what does it do to the cross-ratio?
  6. A website draws a rectangle around a face in a painting, measures it at 10.0 cm by 6.2 cm, and announces the golden ratio. (a) What ratio did they get? (b) Their measurements are good to \(\pm 2\) mm. What is the smallest and largest ratio consistent with that? Does the data prefer \(\varphi = 1.618\) over \(1.6\) or \(5/3\)? (c) List three choices the person drawing the rectangle got to make.

Stretch: where φ genuinely lives

Take a regular pentagon with side 1 and let \(d\) be the length of a diagonal. Every interior angle is 108°, so in triangle \(ACD\) (two diagonals and a side) the apex angle at \(A\) is 36° and the base angles at \(C\) and \(D\) are 72°.

Bisect the 72° angle at \(C\), and let the bisector meet \(AD\) at \(Q\). Now triangle \(CDQ\) has angles 36°, 72°, 72°, the same as triangle \(ACD\). Use the similarity to show \(DQ = 1/d\). Then explain why \(AQ = CQ = CD = 1\) (look for isosceles triangles). Since \(AD = AQ + QD\), you get an equation in \(d\). Solve it, and recognise the answer.

Answers

  1. \((0.5, 0.333)\), \((0.25, 0.167)\), \((0.125, 0.083)\). Each doubling of depth halves both coordinates; the images head to \((0, 0)\), the vanishing point of all lines parallel to the \(z\)-axis.
  2. \((1, 0, 2)\): \((d \cdot 1/2, 0) = (0.5, 0)\), on the horizon. \((1, 1, 1)\): \((1, 1)\), above the horizon since the lines rise as they recede. \((1, 0, 0)\) has \(c = 0\): no vanishing point. Those lines are parallel to the glass and their images stay parallel and horizontal.
  3. \(y = -1.5/Z\): \(-0.5, -0.25, -0.167, -0.125\). Gaps: \(0.25, 0.083, 0.042\). Each gap is \(1.5\left(\frac1Z - \frac1{Z+3}\right)\), shrinking roughly like \(1/Z^2\). Equal spacing becomes spacing that bunches up toward the horizon.
  4. \((1, 0, 1) \to (d, 0) = (1, 0)\) and \((-1, 0, 1) \to (-1, 0)\): both on the horizon, one each side of centre. This is two-point perspective. The verticals have \(c = 0\), so they stay vertical. With \(d = 3\) the vanishing points are at \((\pm 3, 0)\), six units apart instead of two: a more distant viewpoint spreads the vanishing points and flattens the drawing.
  5. World: \(AC = 6\), \(BD = 6\), \(AD = 9\), \(BC = 3\), so \((A,B;C,D) = 36/27 = 4/3\). Image: heights \(-0.5, -0.25, -0.1667, -0.125\) give \(AC = 0.3333\), \(BD = 0.125\), \(AD = 0.375\), \(BC = 0.0833\), and \(\dfrac{0.3333 \times 0.125}{0.375 \times 0.0833} = \dfrac{0.04167}{0.03125} = 4/3\). The cross-ratio is unchanged. It is the quantity perspective cannot destroy, which is why it is the central invariant of projective geometry.
  6. (a) \(10.0/6.2 \approx 1.613\). (b) Smallest \(9.8/6.4 \approx 1.53\), largest \(10.2/6.0 = 1.70\). That range contains 1.6, \(\varphi\), 5/3 and a lot else; the measurement cannot tell them apart. (c) Where the rectangle's edges go (chin or neck? hairline or top of head?), whether it is tilted, which painting reproduction and crop to use, and, before any of that, which of the hundreds of possible rectangles in the painting to measure. With that much freedom you can find a golden rectangle in almost any picture, which is exactly why finding one proves nothing.

Stretch. Triangles \(CDQ\) and \(ACD\) are similar (36°, 72°, 72°), with \(CD\) in the small one corresponding to \(AC = d\) in the big one, so \(\dfrac{DQ}{CD} = \dfrac{CD}{AD}\), i.e. \(\dfrac{DQ}{1} = \dfrac{1}{d}\) and \(DQ = 1/d\). Triangle \(CQD\) is isosceles (two 72° angles) so \(CQ = CD = 1\); triangle \(ACQ\) has angles 36° at \(A\) and 36° at \(C\) (half of 72°), so \(AQ = CQ = 1\). Then \(d = AD = AQ + QD = 1 + 1/d\), giving \(d^2 - d - 1 = 0\) and \(d = \dfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} = \varphi\). The diagonal of a regular pentagon is \(\varphi\) times its side. That is real; the Parthenon is not.
