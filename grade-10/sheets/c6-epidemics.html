R₀ and herd immunity

Exponential starts, the SIR model, and the threshold 1 − 1/R₀.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Every epidemic has one number that matters more than any other. \(R_0\) (say "R nought") is the average number of people one infected person passes the disease to, when everybody around them can still catch it. Measles has an \(R_0\) between 12 and 18; the 1918 flu was around 2. If \(R_0 \gt 1\) each generation of cases is bigger than the last: 1, \(R_0\), \(R_0^2\), \(R_0^3\), exponential growth. If \(R_0 \lt 1\) the outbreak fizzles.

But \(R_0\) assumes everyone is susceptible, and that stops being true as people recover. The simplest honest model splits \(N\) people into three boxes, Susceptible, Infectious and Recovered. Each day:

\[\Delta S = -\beta\,\frac{S\,I}{N}, \qquad \Delta I = \beta\,\frac{S\,I}{N} - \gamma I, \qquad \Delta R = \gamma I.\] \(\beta\) is infections per infectious person per day if everyone were susceptible; \(\gamma\) is the fraction who recover each day, so \(1/\gamma\) is the infectious period. And \(R_0 = \beta/\gamma\).

An infectious person only passes the disease on when they meet a susceptible, so the effective reproduction number is \(R_{\text{eff}} = R_0 \cdot S/N\). The epidemic grows while \(R_{\text{eff}} \gt 1\) and turns when \(S/N = 1/R_0\). The fraction who are no longer susceptible at that moment is

Herd-immunity threshold: \(\;1 - \dfrac{1}{R_0}\). With \(R_0 = 2\) that is 50%. With \(R_0 = 12\) it is 92%.

Immunity can come from infection or vaccination. Once that fraction is immune, each case produces fewer than one new case, and the remaining susceptibles are protected by the herd.

Watch one: two days by hand

\(N = 1000\), with \(S = 990\), \(I = 10\), \(R = 0\). Take \(\beta = 0.5\) and \(\gamma = 0.25\) (a four-day infectious period), so \(R_0 = 2\).

Day 1. New infections: \(0.5 \times 990 \times 10 / 1000 = 4.95\). Recoveries: \(0.25 \times 10 = 2.5\). So \(S = 985.05\), \(I = 12.45\), \(R = 2.5\).

Day 2. New infections: \(0.5 \times 985.05 \times 12.45 / 1000 \approx 6.13\). Recoveries \(\approx 3.11\). So \(S \approx 978.92\), \(I \approx 15.47\), \(R \approx 5.61\).

Fractional people are fine: the model tracks averages. \(I\) grows about 25% a day at first, because \(\beta S/N - \gamma \approx 0.25\), and that rate falls as \(S\) falls. The demo at infinityclub.net/demos/sir.html runs this for hundreds of days.

Your turn

  1. A disease has \(R_0 = 3\) and a generation time of 5 days. Starting from one case, how many new cases are there in the fourth generation? How many cases in total by then, and how many days have passed?
  2. Compute the herd-immunity threshold for \(R_0 = 1.5\), \(2\), \(3\), \(5\) and \(12\). Give each as a fraction and a percentage.
  3. A vaccine stops infection in 90% of the people who receive it. The disease has \(R_0 = 4\). What fraction of the population must be vaccinated to reach the herd-immunity threshold? Why is the answer bigger than the threshold itself?
  4. \(R_0 = 2.5\) in a city of 10,000 where everyone starts susceptible. How many people are still susceptible when daily cases peak, and how many have therefore been infected by the peak? If 40% had been vaccinated beforehand, what would \(R_{\text{eff}}\) be on day 0?

Stretch: the overshoot

Herd immunity is where the epidemic stops growing, not where it stops: the infectious people at the peak keep infecting. The fraction \(s_\infty\) still susceptible at the very end satisfies \(s_\infty = e^{-R_0(1 - s_\infty)}\). For \(R_0 = 2\), start from \(s = 0.5\), compute \(e^{-2(1-s)}\), feed it back in, and repeat until it settles. What fraction is infected in total, against the 50% threshold?

Answers

  1. Fourth generation: \(3^4 = 81\) new cases. Total \(1 + 3 + 9 + 27 + 81 = 121\). Four generations is 20 days.
  2. \(1 - 1/R_0\): \(1/3 \approx 33\%\), \(1/2 = 50\%\), \(2/3 \approx 67\%\), \(4/5 = 80\%\), \(11/12 \approx 92\%\).
  3. Threshold \(1 - 1/4 = 75\%\) must be immune. If a fraction \(v\) is vaccinated, \(0.9v\) are immune, so \(0.9v \ge 0.75\) gives \(v \ge 0.833\): about 83%. Only 90% of each dose counts, so you must over-vaccinate to make up for the 10% in whom it fails.
  4. Peak when \(S/N = 1/2.5 = 0.4\): 4,000 still susceptible, so 6,000 have been infected by the peak (very nearly; the handful of initial cases were never susceptible). With 40% vaccinated, \(R_{\text{eff}} = 2.5 \times 0.6 = 1.5\): still an epidemic, but a slower one.

Stretch. From 0.5 the iterates go 0.368, 0.282, 0.238, 0.218, … and settle at \(s_\infty \approx 0.203\). About 80% are infected in total, far more than the 50% threshold: a 30-point overshoot. Vaccination reaches the threshold while \(I\) is still essentially zero, so there is no stock of infectious people to drive an overshoot; natural infection reaches it at the exact moment infections are most numerous.
