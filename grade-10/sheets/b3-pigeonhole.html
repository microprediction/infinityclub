The pigeonhole principle

Obvious, and yet it proves two Londoners have the same number of hairs and that some multiple of 7 is all 1s and 0s.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

If 10 pigeons fly into 9 pigeonholes, some hole gets at least two pigeons. Nobody argues with that. The surprise is how much it proves.

Pigeonhole principle. If \(N\) objects are placed in \(k\) boxes and \(N > k\), some box holds at least 2. More precisely, some box holds at least \(\lceil N/k \rceil\) objects (that is \(N/k\) rounded up). Proof: if every box held fewer than \(N/k\), the total would be less than \(N\).

Hairs. A human head has at most about 150,000 hairs. London has about 9 million people. Boxes: the possible hair counts 0, 1, 2, ..., 150,000, which is 150,001 boxes. Pigeons: 9 million Londoners. So two Londoners have exactly the same number of hairs on their heads. In fact some hair count is shared by at least \(\lceil 9{,}000{,}000 / 150{,}001 \rceil = 60\) people. We have no idea who they are. Pigeonhole proves existence without finding anything, and that is its character.

The craft is in choosing the boxes. Once the boxes are right, the proof is one line. Every problem tonight is really the question: what are the pigeons, and what are the holes?

Watch one: a multiple of 7 made of 1s and 0s

Claim: some multiple of 7 is written using only the digits 1 and 0. (The same works for any number in place of 7.)

Look at the eight numbers \(1, 11, 111, 1111, \ldots, 11111111\). Divide each by 7 and note the remainder. There are only 7 possible remainders (0 to 6) and 8 numbers, so two of them, say the one with \(a\) ones and the one with \(b\) ones (\(a > b\)), leave the same remainder. Then their difference is a multiple of 7. And the difference is \(\underbrace{11\ldots1}_{a-b}\underbrace{00\ldots0}_{b}\): all 1s and 0s.

Pigeons: the 8 repunits. Holes: the 7 remainders. The proof never found the multiple; it just proved one exists. (Here it is anyway: \(111111 = 7 \times 15873\). But for a nasty divisor like 2027 the proof still works and the search would be long.)

Your turn

  1. In any group of 13 people, two share a birth month. How many people are needed to guarantee that three share a birth month? Explain with the \(\lceil N/k \rceil\) form.
  2. A drawer has 10 black, 10 blue and 10 grey socks. You pull socks out in the dark. How many must you take to be sure of a matching pair? How many to be sure of a black pair?
  3. Five points are placed anywhere inside a \(1 \times 1\) square. Prove that two of them are at most \(\sqrt{2}/2 \approx 0.707\) apart. (Hint: cut the square into four smaller squares.)
  4. Pick any 10 whole numbers, \(a_1, \ldots, a_{10}\). Prove that some of them, taken consecutively, add up to a multiple of 10. (Hint: the pigeons are the running totals \(a_1\), \(a_1 + a_2\), \(a_1 + a_2 + a_3\), and so on. What are the holes? What if one running total is already a multiple of 10?)
  5. Choose any 51 numbers from 1 to 100. Prove that (a) two of them are consecutive, and (b) two of them have no common factor bigger than 1.
  6. Choose any 51 numbers from 1 to 100. Prove that one of them divides another. (Hint: write each number as \(2^j \times m\) with \(m\) odd. How many possible odd parts \(m\) are there? If two numbers share the same odd part, what can you say?)

Stretch: order out of any ten numbers

Write down any 10 different numbers in any order. Claim: you can always find 4 of them, read left to right, that are increasing, or 4 that are decreasing. For example in 4, 8, 1, 9, 2, 7, 3, 6, 5, 10 the subsequence 4, 8, 9, 10 is increasing. Try to design a sequence of 10 that defeats the claim, and then prove it cannot be done.

Proof sketch to complete. For each position \(i\), let \(u_i\) be the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at position \(i\), and \(d_i\) the longest decreasing one ending there. Suppose no monotone subsequence of length 4 exists, so every \(u_i\) and \(d_i\) is 1, 2 or 3. (a) How many possible pairs \((u_i, d_i)\) are there? (b) How many positions? (c) Show that two different positions cannot have the same pair: if \(i \lt j\) and \(x_i \lt x_j\), compare \(u_j\) with \(u_i\); if \(x_i > x_j\), compare \(d_j\) with \(d_i\). (d) Conclude. Then find a sequence of 9 numbers with no monotone subsequence of length 4, showing 10 is the smallest that works.

Answers

  1. 25 people. With 12 months as boxes, \(\lceil 25/12 \rceil = 3\), so some month has at least 3. With 24 people, two per month is possible, so 24 is not enough.
  2. 4 socks: three colours are the boxes, so 4 socks force two of one colour. For a black pair, the worst case is all 10 blue and all 10 grey first, then two black: 22 socks.
  3. Cut the unit square into four \(\frac12 \times \frac12\) squares. Five points, four small squares: two points share a small square (a point on a boundary can be assigned to either). Two points in a \(\frac12 \times \frac12\) square are at most a diagonal apart: \(\sqrt{(\frac12)^2 + (\frac12)^2} = \sqrt{\frac12} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\).
  4. Let \(s_i = a_1 + \cdots + a_i\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, 10\). If some \(s_i\) is a multiple of 10, done: \(a_1, \ldots, a_i\) is the block. Otherwise each \(s_i\) has one of the 9 remainders 1 to 9 when divided by 10. Ten sums, nine remainders: two of them, \(s_i\) and \(s_j\) with \(i \lt j\), share a remainder, so \(s_j - s_i = a_{i+1} + \cdots + a_j\) is a multiple of 10. (The same argument works for any \(n\) numbers and divisor \(n\).)
  5. (a) Boxes: the 50 pairs \(\{1,2\}, \{3,4\}, \ldots, \{99,100\}\). 51 numbers in 50 boxes: two in the same pair, and they are consecutive. (b) Consecutive numbers are coprime: any common factor of \(n\) and \(n+1\) divides their difference, 1. So the same pair works.
  6. Every number from 1 to 100 is \(2^j \times m\) with \(m\) odd, and \(m\) is one of the 50 odd numbers 1, 3, ..., 99. Boxes: the 50 odd parts. 51 numbers: two share an odd part, say \(2^a m\) and \(2^b m\) with \(a \lt b\). Then the first divides the second (the ratio is \(2^{b-a}\)). With only 50 numbers the claim can fail: 51, 52, ..., 100 has no number dividing another.

Stretch. (a) \(u_i, d_i \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) gives \(3 \times 3 = 9\) possible pairs. (b) 10 positions. (c) If \(i \lt j\) and \(x_i \lt x_j\), then any increasing subsequence ending at \(i\) extends by \(x_j\), so \(u_j \ge u_i + 1\) and the pairs differ. If \(x_i > x_j\), the same with \(d\): \(d_j \ge d_i + 1\). Either way positions \(i\) and \(j\) get different pairs. (d) 10 positions, 9 pairs, all pairs distinct: impossible. So some \(u_i\) or \(d_i\) is at least 4, which is a monotone subsequence of length 4. A sequence of 9 with none: 3, 2, 1, 6, 5, 4, 9, 8, 7 (longest increasing and decreasing are both 3). In general \(n^2 + 1\) numbers force a monotone subsequence of length \(n + 1\); this is the Erdős–Szekeres theorem.
