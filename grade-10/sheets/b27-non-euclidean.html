Geometry on a sphere and beyond

Triangles with three right angles, the parallel postulate, and the geometry Einstein needed.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Around 300 BC Euclid built all of geometry from five assumptions. Four are modest: you can draw a straight line between two points; you can extend a line; you can draw a circle with any centre and radius; all right angles are equal. The fifth is a mouthful, and is equivalent to this: through a point not on a line, there is exactly one line parallel to it. For two thousand years mathematicians tried to prove the fifth from the other four. They failed, and in the 1820s Bolyai and Lobachevsky, and privately Gauss, saw why: it cannot be proved, because there are perfectly consistent geometries where it is false.

You are standing on one. On a sphere, the straightest possible path is a great circle, a circle whose centre is the centre of the sphere: the equator, any meridian, the route a plane flies from London to Tokyo. Great circles are the sphere's "lines". And any two of them meet, twice. There are no parallels at all.

So the theorems change. Start at the North Pole, walk down a meridian to the equator, turn 90° and walk a quarter of the way round the equator, turn 90° again and walk back up to the pole. You have drawn a triangle with three right angles, 270° in all. On a sphere the angles of a triangle always add to more than 180°, and the excess is proportional to the area. That is Girard's theorem (1629): for a triangle with angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) in radians on a sphere of radius \(R\),

\(\text{Area} = R^2(\alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi)\). In degrees: angle sum \(= 180° + 720° \times \dfrac{\text{area}}{\text{area of sphere}}\).

Check it on the three-right-angle triangle: excess \(= 3\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{2}\), area \(= \frac{\pi}{2}R^2\), exactly one eighth of the sphere's \(4\pi R^2\). Look at a globe and count: the eight triangles cut out by the equator and two perpendicular meridians are all congruent to it.

Small triangles on a huge sphere have tiny excess, which is why a surveyor's field looks Euclidean and why it took so long to notice. There is also a geometry in the opposite direction, the hyperbolic plane, where through a point there are infinitely many parallels and triangles add to less than 180°, with the deficit again proportional to area. Escher's Circle Limit prints are maps of it. Einstein's general relativity says space itself is curved by mass: Euclid's fifth postulate is, in the end, a question for physics, and the answer is no.

Watch one: how big is a triangle with angles 90°, 90°, 45°?

Take the Earth as a sphere of radius \(R = 6371\) km. Vertices: the North Pole, the point on the equator at longitude 0°, and the point on the equator at longitude 45° E. The two equator angles are right angles (meridians meet the equator at 90°), and the angle at the pole is the difference in longitude, 45°.

Excess \(= 90° + 90° + 45° - 180° = 45° = \frac{\pi}{4}\) radians. Area \(= R^2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = 6371^2 \times 0.7854 \approx 3.19 \times 10^7\) km², about 31.9 million km². Sanity check: the sphere's area is \(4\pi R^2 \approx 510\) million km², and this triangle is \(\frac{\pi/4}{4\pi} = \frac{1}{16}\) of it: \(510/16 \approx 31.9\). It works.

Going the other way: a triangle of area one million km² (about the size of Egypt) has excess \(10^6 / 6371^2 \approx 0.0246\) radians \(\approx 1.41°\). An equilateral triangle that size has angles of about \(60.47°\), not 60°. Surveyors really do correct for this.

Your turn

  1. Which of these are "straight lines" (great circles) on the Earth: the equator; the Arctic Circle; the Greenwich meridian; the 180° meridian together with the Greenwich meridian; the Tropic of Cancer? For each non-line, say why not.
  2. A triangle has vertices at the North Pole and at two points on the equator 120° of longitude apart. Find its three angles, its angle sum, and what fraction of the Earth's surface it covers. Then compute the area in km² with \(R = 6371\) km.
  3. Can a spherical triangle have angle sum 540°? 541°? Use Girard's theorem and the fact that a triangle must fit in a hemisphere (area less than \(2\pi R^2\)) to find the full range of possible angle sums.
  4. A lune is the region between two meridians, with angle \(\theta\) at each pole (an orange segment). Explain why its area is \(\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi} \cdot 4\pi R^2 = 2\theta R^2\). What is the area of the lune with \(\theta = 90°\) as a fraction of the sphere?
  5. Two cities both sit at latitude 60° N, 90° of longitude apart. The distance along the 60th parallel is a quarter of that parallel's circumference, \(\frac14 \cdot 2\pi R \cos 60°\). Compute it. The great-circle distance satisfies \(\cos d = \sin^2 60° + \cos^2 60° \cos 90°\), with \(d\) the angle at the Earth's centre. Compute \(d\) and the great-circle distance, and how much a pilot saves. (See the grade 6 maps sheet for why this looks wrong on a Mercator map.)
  6. On the hyperbolic plane, the angle sum of a triangle is less than 180°, with deficit proportional to area. Sketch (roughly) what this means for a quadrilateral: can a hyperbolic quadrilateral have four right angles? Can one exist on a sphere? Explain using angle sums.

Stretch: prove Girard's theorem

Here is the whole proof, with the gaps left for you. Draw a spherical triangle \(T\) with angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\). Extend its three sides to full great circles. Each pair of great circles cuts the sphere into four lunes; the pair through vertex \(A\) makes a lune of angle \(\alpha\) that contains \(T\), and an identical lune on the far side containing the antipodal triangle \(T'\). Likewise for \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\).

(1) Using question 4, write down the total area of the six lunes. (2) Explain why these six lunes together cover the whole sphere once, except that \(T\) and \(T'\) are each covered three times. (3) Turn (1) and (2) into the equation \(2(2\alpha + 2\beta + 2\gamma)R^2 = 4\pi R^2 + 4\,\text{Area}(T)\) and solve for the area. You have proved that the angle sum of every spherical triangle exceeds 180°, with no coordinates and no calculus.

Answers

  1. Great circles: the equator; the Greenwich meridian (as half of the great circle it shares with the 180° meridian); the Greenwich and 180° meridians together form one complete great circle. Not great circles: the Arctic Circle and the Tropic of Cancer, because their centres are on the Earth's axis, not at the Earth's centre; they are smaller circles, and following one means constantly turning.
  2. Angles 90°, 90°, 120°; sum 300°; excess 120°. Fraction \(= 120/720 = \frac16\) of the sphere (the pole-to-equator triangles with longitude gap \(\theta\) always cover \(\theta/360\) of a hemisphere, here a third of the northern hemisphere). Area \(= 510.1/6 \approx 85.0\) million km², or directly \(R^2 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} \approx 8.50 \times 10^7\) km².
  3. Angle sum \(= 180° + 720° \times (\text{area}/4\pi R^2)\). The area is strictly between 0 and \(2\pi R^2\) (half the sphere), so the sum is strictly between 180° and \(180° + 360° = 540°\). 540° is the unreachable limit; 541° is impossible. (Each angle is also less than 180°, which gives the same bound.)
  4. A lune of angle \(\theta\) is the fraction \(\theta/2\pi\) of the full turn around the axis, so it takes that fraction of the sphere's area: \(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot 4\pi R^2 = 2\theta R^2\). With \(\theta = 90° = \frac{\pi}{2}\): \(\pi R^2\), one quarter of the sphere.
  5. Parallel: \(\frac14 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 6371 \cdot 0.5 \approx 5004\) km. Great circle: \(\cos d = 0.75 + 0.25 \cdot 0 = 0.75\), so \(d \approx 41.41° = 0.7227\) rad and the distance is \(6371 \times 0.7227 \approx 4604\) km. The pilot saves about 400 km by heading north of the parallel, which is why flights between Europe and North America arc over Greenland.
  6. Split a quadrilateral along a diagonal into two triangles. In hyperbolic geometry each has angle sum below 180°, so the quadrilateral's sum is below 360° and four right angles are impossible: a hyperbolic "rectangle" does not exist. On a sphere the sum exceeds 360°, so four right angles are also impossible there, for the opposite reason: two meridians and two parallels make two right angles at the equator and two obtuse angles above. Only in Euclidean geometry do rectangles exist, and that is equivalent to the fifth postulate.

Stretch. (1) The lunes have angles \(\alpha, \alpha, \beta, \beta, \gamma, \gamma\), total area \(2R^2(2\alpha + 2\beta + 2\gamma) = 4R^2(\alpha + \beta + \gamma)\). (2) The three great circles cut the sphere into eight triangles: \(T\), \(T'\), and three pairs of triangles each sharing a side with \(T\) or \(T'\). Each lune is \(T\) (or \(T'\)) plus one neighbour. Every neighbouring triangle lies in exactly one lune, while \(T\) lies in three (one per vertex) and so does \(T'\). (3) Lune total \(= \text{sphere} + 2\,\text{Area}(T) + 2\,\text{Area}(T')\), and \(T'\) is congruent to \(T\), so \(4R^2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma) = 4\pi R^2 + 4\,\text{Area}(T)\), giving \(\text{Area}(T) = R^2(\alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi)\). Since area is positive, \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma \gt \pi\).
