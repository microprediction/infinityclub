Graphs: trees, planarity and Ramsey

Trees have n − 1 edges, K₅ cannot be drawn flat, and among any six people three are mutual friends or strangers.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A graph is dots (vertices) joined by lines (edges). You met them in Königsberg; tonight you prove three things about them, each with a different kind of argument.

1. Trees. A tree is a connected graph with no cycles: a family tree, a river system, the folders on a computer. Every tree with \(n\) vertices has exactly \(n - 1\) edges. Proof by induction on \(n\). One vertex, no edges: true. Now take a tree with \(n \ge 2\) vertices. It has a leaf, a vertex with only one edge (follow a path without repeating vertices; it cannot go on forever, and where it stops is a leaf). Remove that leaf and its edge: what is left is still connected and still has no cycles, a tree with \(n - 1\) vertices, which by induction has \(n - 2\) edges. Put the leaf back: \(n - 1\) edges. Done.

2. Planarity. A graph is planar if it can be drawn with no two edges crossing. Euler's formula says that for any connected planar drawing, \(V - E + F = 2\), where \(F\) counts the regions including the outside. (Prove it by building the graph one edge at a time: an edge that joins a new vertex changes \(V\) and \(E\) by 1 each; an edge between old vertices closes a new region, changing \(E\) and \(F\) by 1 each.) Now the trick. In a planar graph with at least 3 vertices, every region is bounded by at least 3 edges, and every edge borders at most 2 regions, so \(3F \le 2E\). Substitute \(F = 2 - V + E\):

\(E \le 3V - 6\) for every planar graph with \(V \ge 3\). If the graph has no triangles, every region needs at least 4 edges and the bound tightens to \(E \le 2V - 4\).

The complete graph \(K_5\) (five vertices, every pair joined) has \(V = 5\), \(E = 10\), and \(3 \times 5 - 6 = 9 \lt 10\). It cannot be drawn flat. The utility graph \(K_{3,3}\) (three houses, three utilities, nine connections) has \(V = 6\), \(E = 9\), no triangles, and \(2 \times 6 - 4 = 8 \lt 9\). Impossible too. Kuratowski proved in 1930 that these two are the only obstacles: a graph is non-planar exactly when it contains a stretched copy of \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\).

3. Ramsey. At any party of six people, there are three who all know each other or three who are all strangers. Draw the six people as vertices, colour an edge red for "know each other" and blue for "strangers". Pick any person \(A\). She has 5 edges, so at least 3 are the same colour, say red, going to \(B, C, D\). If any edge among \(B, C, D\) is red, that edge plus \(A\) makes a red triangle. If none is, \(B, C, D\) is a blue triangle. Either way, done. Five people are not enough: colour a pentagon red and its inside star blue and there is no single-colour triangle. In the notation of Ramsey theory, \(R(3,3) = 6\). The next number, \(R(4,4) = 18\), was found in 1955; \(R(5,5)\) is somewhere between 43 and 46, and nobody knows which.

Watch one: \(K_5\) minus an edge

Remove one edge from \(K_5\): \(V = 5\), \(E = 9\). The bound \(E \le 3V - 6 = 9\) is now satisfied, so planarity is not ruled out. But the inequality only says "not impossible"; to show the graph is planar you must draw it. Label the vertices 1 to 5 and let the missing edge be 45. Draw 1, 2, 3 as a triangle. Put 4 inside the triangle and join it to 1, 2, 3. Put 5 outside the triangle and join it to 1, 2, 3. Edges: three sides, three spokes inside, three spokes outside: \(9\), and nothing crosses.

Check Euler: \(F = 2 - V + E = 2 - 5 + 9 = 6\). Count on the drawing: three small triangles inside around vertex 4, two regions outside between the spokes to 5, and the unbounded region. Six.

The moral: the inequality is a one-way test. Fail it and you are certainly non-planar; pass it and you still have to draw the thing. The Petersen graph in the stretch is the classic example of passing the test and still being impossible.

Your turn

  1. A tree has 12 vertices. How many edges? A connected graph has 12 vertices and 15 edges; how many edges must you delete to leave a tree, and does it matter which? (Think about what each deleted edge must break.)
  2. Draw a tree with 8 vertices in which every vertex has degree 1 or 3. How many leaves does it have? Prove in general that in such a tree the number of leaves is two more than the number of degree-3 vertices. (Add up the degrees: the total is \(2E = 2(n-1)\).) Then explain why no such tree has 7 vertices.
  3. Check Euler's formula on the cube (as a graph: 8 corners, 12 edges) and on the dodecahedron (20 vertices, each of degree 3). For the dodecahedron compute \(E\) from the degrees first, then \(F\).
  4. Use \(E \le 3V - 6\) to prove that every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5. (Suppose every vertex had degree \(\ge 6\) and add up the degrees.) This fact is the first step of every proof of the Four Colour Theorem.
  5. Draw the five-person party with no single-colour triangle: a pentagon of "friends" and a pentagram of "strangers". Explain why adding a sixth person forces a triangle, using the argument from tonight's idea, but this time start with the colour that gives the sixth person at least three edges.
  6. Suppose every edge of \(K_6\) is coloured red or blue. Tonight's argument finds one single-colour triangle. Show there must be at least two. (Hint: a triangle that is not single-coloured has exactly two vertices where the colours change. Count "colour-changing corners" over the whole graph: each vertex with \(r\) red edges and \(5 - r\) blue edges contributes \(r(5 - r)\) of them, which is at most 6. There are 20 triangles in all.)

Stretch: the Petersen graph

The Petersen graph has 10 vertices and 15 edges: an outer pentagon, an inner pentagram, and five spokes joining them. It passes \(E \le 3V - 6\) (15 against 24) and even the triangle-free bound \(E \le 2V - 4\) (15 against 16). Yet it is not planar. The reason is that its shortest cycle has length 5, so in any flat drawing every region would be bounded by at least 5 edges. Redo the Euler argument with that fact: show that a planar graph whose shortest cycle has length 5 satisfies \(5F \le 2E\), hence \(E \le \tfrac53(V - 2)\), and check what that gives for \(V = 10\). Then find a stretched \(K_{3,3}\) inside the Petersen graph, which Kuratowski says must be there: three "house" vertices and three "utility" vertices joined by nine paths that share no vertices except their ends. Label the outer pentagon \(u_0, \ldots, u_4\) in order, the inner star \(v_0, \ldots, v_4\) with \(v_i\) joined to \(v_{i+2}\), and spokes \(u_i v_i\). One solution uses every vertex of the graph.

Answers

  1. 11 edges. You must delete \(15 - 11 = 4\) edges, and each deletion must break a cycle without disconnecting the graph; which four you choose matters (deleting a bridge disconnects it), but the number is always 4.
  2. One example: a centre \(C\) joined to \(P, Q, R\); \(P\) joined to two more leaves; \(Q\) joined to two more leaves. Degrees 3, 3, 3 and five 1s: eight vertices, 5 leaves. In general with \(L\) leaves and \(T\) vertices of degree 3, \(n = L + T\) and the degree sum is \(L + 3T = 2(n - 1) = 2L + 2T - 2\), so \(T = L - 2\), that is \(L = T + 2\). Then \(n = 2T + 2\) is always even, so 7 vertices is impossible.
  3. Cube: \(8 - 12 + 6 = 2\). Dodecahedron: degree sum \(20 \times 3 = 60 = 2E\), so \(E = 30\); \(F = 2 - 20 + 30 = 12\) faces, the twelve pentagons.
  4. If every degree were \(\ge 6\), the degree sum \(2E \ge 6V\), so \(E \ge 3V\). But \(E \le 3V - 6 \lt 3V\). Contradiction, so some vertex has degree 5 or less.
  5. In the pentagon-plus-pentagram colouring each vertex has 2 red and 2 blue edges, and every triangle uses at least one pentagon edge and at least one star edge, so none is single-coloured. Add a sixth person \(F\): she has 5 edges, so at least 3 share a colour, say blue, to \(X, Y, Z\). If any of \(XY, YZ, XZ\) is blue, it forms a blue triangle with \(F\); otherwise all three are red and \(X, Y, Z\) is a red triangle.
  6. Each non-single-coloured triangle has exactly 2 corners where a red edge meets a blue edge. Vertex with \(r\) red edges contributes \(r(5 - r)\) such corners, maximised at \(r = 2\) or \(3\): \(6\). Six vertices give at most 36 corners, so at most 18 two-coloured triangles. There are \(\binom63 = 20\) triangles, so at least 2 are single-coloured. (This bound is sharp: a colouring with exactly 2 exists.)

Stretch. Every region bounded by at least 5 edges and every edge bordering at most 2 regions gives \(5F \le 2E\). With \(F = 2 - V + E\): \(5(2 - V + E) \le 2E\), so \(3E \le 5V - 10\), \(E \le \tfrac53(V - 2)\). For \(V = 10\) that is \(E \le 13.3\), but Petersen has 15 edges: not planar. For the \(K_{3,3}\): take houses \(u_0, u_2, v_1\) and utilities \(u_1, u_3, v_2\). The nine paths: \(u_0u_1\); \(u_0u_4u_3\); \(u_0v_0v_2\); \(u_2u_1\); \(u_2u_3\); \(u_2v_2\); \(v_1u_1\); \(v_1v_3u_3\); \(v_1v_4v_2\). Every edge used exists (check the spokes and the \(v_iv_{i+2}\) rule) and the four middle vertices \(u_4, v_0, v_3, v_4\) each appear in only one path. Other solutions exist and any correct one earns full marks.
