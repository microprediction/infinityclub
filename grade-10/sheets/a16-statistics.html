Standard deviation and the bell curve

What σ measures, 68–95–99.7, z-scores, and why correlation is not causation (with a real example).

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The mean \(\mu\) tells you where a data set sits. The standard deviation \(\sigma\) tells you how spread out it is, and it is built in the most natural way possible once you see the obstacle.

Take each value's deviation from the mean, \(x - \mu\). You might average these to measure spread, but they always sum to exactly zero: the values above the mean cancel the values below (that is what the mean is). So square them first. The average of the squared deviations is the variance, and its square root, which puts the units back to normal, is the standard deviation:

\[\sigma^2 = \dfrac{(x_1-\mu)^2 + (x_2-\mu)^2 + \cdots + (x_n-\mu)^2}{n}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}.\]

(When the data are a sample from a bigger population, statisticians divide by \(n - 1\) instead and call it \(s\). It corrects a small bias. For tonight, divide by \(n\).)

Why care? Because an enormous number of real measurements, heights, measurement errors, exam scores, the sum of many dice, pile up in the same bell-shaped normal curve, and on that curve \(\sigma\) is a ruler:

68–95–99.7. About 68% of the data lie within one \(\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three.

A z-score measures a value in those units: \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\). A \(z\) of 2 means two standard deviations above the mean, in the top 2.3% on a normal curve, whatever the original units were. That is how you compare a maths score with a running time.

One warning that belongs in the same breath. Two quantities can rise and fall together, a correlation, without either causing the other. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in the same months. Nobody thinks ice cream causes drowning: hot weather sends people to both the ice cream van and the water. The hidden third variable is called a confounder, and finding it is the first thing to try whenever a headline says "linked to".

Watch one: eight numbers by hand

Data: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9. Sum 40, so \(\mu = 5\). Deviations: \(-3, -1, -1, -1, 0, 0, 2, 4\) (check: they sum to 0). Squares: \(9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16\), total 32. Variance \(32/8 = 4\), so \(\sigma = 2\).

So the value 9 has \(z = (9 - 5)/2 = 2\): two standard deviations above the mean. If these were drawn from a normal population, a value that high or higher would show up about 2.3% of the time.

A shortcut worth knowing: \(\sigma^2\) also equals (mean of the squares) minus (square of the mean). Here the squares are 4, 16, 16, 16, 25, 25, 49, 81, mean \(232/8 = 29\), and \(29 - 5^2 = 4\). Same answer, no deviations needed. The stretch asks you to prove it.

Your turn

  1. Compute \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) for the data 3, 7, 7, 19, showing the deviations and their squares.
  2. Without recomputing from scratch, give \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) when (a) 10 is added to every value in question 1, and (b) every value in question 1 is doubled. Explain each in one sentence using the formula.
  3. Adult heights in a population are roughly normal with \(\mu = 170\) cm and \(\sigma = 8\) cm. Estimate the fraction taller than 178 cm, the fraction between 154 and 186 cm, and the fraction shorter than 194 cm. Then find the z-score of someone 190 cm tall and, using the table, the fraction taller than them.
  4. On test A (\(\mu = 60\), \(\sigma = 10\)) Priya scored 75. On test B (\(\mu = 70\), \(\sigma = 4\)) Sam scored 76. Who did better relative to their class? What would Sam have needed on test B to reach \(z = 2\)?
  5. Two data sets both have mean 5: \(\{1, 1, 1, 9, 9, 9\}\) and \(\{1, 3, 5, 5, 7, 9\}\). Predict which has the larger \(\sigma\) before calculating, then calculate both. What is the smallest possible \(\sigma\) for any data set, and when does it happen?
  6. For each correlation, name a plausible confounder, and say what study would separate cause from coincidence: (a) cities with more firefighters suffer more fire damage; (b) children with bigger feet read better; (c) a 2012 medical-journal note found countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel prizes.

Stretch: why the mean, and the shortcut formula

Why measure spread around the mean rather than around some other centre \(c\)? Write each deviation as \((x - c) = (x - \mu) + (\mu - c)\), square it, and sum over the data. Show that

\[\sum (x - c)^2 = \sum (x - \mu)^2 + n(\mu - c)^2 .\]

(The cross term vanishes. Why?) Conclude that the sum of squared deviations is smallest when \(c = \mu\): the mean is the point the data are, on average, squared-closest to.

Then set \(c = 0\) in the same identity and divide by \(n\) to prove the shortcut from the example: \(\sigma^2 = \overline{x^2} - \mu^2\).

Answers

  1. Sum 36, \(\mu = 9\). Deviations \(-6, -2, -2, 10\); squares \(36, 4, 4, 100\), total 144; variance \(144/4 = 36\); \(\sigma = 6\).
  2. (a) \(\mu = 19\), \(\sigma = 6\): adding 10 shifts every value and the mean together, so every deviation is unchanged. (b) \(\mu = 18\), \(\sigma = 12\): doubling doubles every deviation, so squares quadruple, the variance quadruples, and \(\sigma\) doubles. In general \(\sigma\) scales with the data and ignores shifts.
  3. 178 is one \(\sigma\) above: about 16% taller. 154 to 186 is \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\): about 95%. 194 is \(\mu + 3\sigma\): about 99.85% shorter (all but half of the outer 0.3%). 190 cm: \(z = 20/8 = 2.5\), so about 0.6% are taller.
  4. Priya: \(z = 15/10 = 1.5\). Sam: \(z = 6/4 = 1.5\). Equal, relative to their classes. For \(z = 2\) on test B Sam needs \(70 + 2 \times 4 = 78\).
  5. The first set has every value 4 away from the mean, so \(\sigma = 4\) exactly. The second: deviations \(-4, -2, 0, 0, 2, 4\), squares sum to 40, variance \(40/6 \approx 6.67\), \(\sigma \approx 2.58\). The first is more spread, as predicted: its values are all at the extremes. The smallest possible \(\sigma\) is 0, when every value equals the mean.
  6. (a) Confounder: the size of the fire. Big fires attract more firefighters and do more damage. Compare fires of similar size with different numbers of crews. (b) Age: older children have bigger feet and read better. Compare children of the same age. (c) National wealth: rich countries buy more chocolate and fund more research. Compare countries at similar incomes, or look within one country over time as chocolate consumption changes. In all three, the convincing test is an experiment or a comparison that holds the confounder fixed.

Stretch. \(\sum (x-c)^2 = \sum\big[(x-\mu)^2 + 2(x-\mu)(\mu-c) + (\mu-c)^2\big] = \sum(x-\mu)^2 + 2(\mu-c)\sum(x-\mu) + n(\mu-c)^2\). The middle sum is zero because deviations from the mean sum to zero, leaving the identity. Since \(n(\mu-c)^2 \ge 0\) with equality only at \(c = \mu\), the mean minimises the sum of squared deviations. With \(c = 0\): \(\sum x^2 = \sum(x-\mu)^2 + n\mu^2\); divide by \(n\): \(\overline{x^2} = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\), so \(\sigma^2 = \overline{x^2} - \mu^2\).
