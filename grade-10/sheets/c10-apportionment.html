Fair shares: apportionment and Arrow's theorem

Why the US House had a paradox, Hamilton versus Jefferson, and why no voting system is perfect.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The US Constitution shares House seats among the states in proportion to population. But a state's share comes out as 7.8 seats, and you cannot send 0.8 of a person to Washington. Deciding how to round is apportionment, and it caused the first presidential veto in American history.

Hamilton's method: compute each state's exact share, its quota; give every state the whole-number part; hand the leftover seats, one each, to the states with the largest fractional parts. Washington vetoed it and Congress adopted Jefferson's method: pick a divisor \(D\), give each state \(\lfloor \text{population}/D \rfloor\) seats (\(\lfloor x \rfloor\) means \(x\) rounded down to a whole number), and adjust \(D\) until the seats add up. Rounding down favours big states, and Jefferson was from the biggest one.

Hamilton's method came back and in 1880 a clerk found that Alabama would get 8 seats in a house of 299 but only 7 in a house of 300: the Alabama paradox. Balinski and Young proved in 1982 that no method can both stay within quota and avoid such paradoxes.

Voting is worse. Arrow (1951) asked for a rule turning everyone's rankings into one group ranking such that:

  1. it always gives an answer, whatever the rankings;
  2. unanimity: if everyone prefers A to B, so does the group;
  3. independence of irrelevant alternatives: the group's order of A against B depends only on how voters rank A against B;
  4. no dictator: no one voter's ranking is automatically the group's.

Arrow's theorem: with three or more options, no rule satisfies all four.

Watch one: Hamilton with three states

Populations 7, 6 and 2 million; 10 seats. Total 15 million, so one seat per 1.5 million. Quotas: \(7/1.5 = 4.667\), \(6/1.5 = 4\), \(2/1.5 = 1.333\). Whole parts 4, 4, 1, which is 9 seats. The largest fractional part is state A's 0.667, so it gets the spare seat. Final: 5, 4, 1. Jefferson with \(D = 1.4\) million gives \(\lfloor 5 \rfloor, \lfloor 4.29 \rfloor, \lfloor 1.43 \rfloor = 5, 4, 1\) too.

Your turn

  1. The Alabama paradox. Three states with populations 6, 6 and 2 million. Apportion 10 seats by Hamilton's method, then 11 seats. What happens to the small state, and exactly why?
  2. Jefferson. Five states with populations 650, 100, 100, 100 and 50 thousand share 12 seats. Compute the quotas. Show that \(D = 72\) thousand works for Jefferson's method and write down the result. Which state gets more than its quota rounded up? Which gets nothing?

Stretch: the theorem that says you must choose

(a) Hamilton's method always stays within quota: explain why that is built in. (b) Check that a dictatorship satisfies Arrow's conditions (1), (2) and (3); so the theorem really says the only rules respecting (1) to (3) are dictatorships. (c) Why does a Condorcet cycle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A) make pairwise majority rule fail condition (1)?

Answers

  1. Ten seats: quotas 4.286, 4.286, 1.429; whole parts 4, 4, 1; the largest fraction (0.429) is the small state's, so 4, 4, 2. Eleven seats: quotas 4.714, 4.714, 1.571; whole parts 4, 4, 1; the two largest fractions are now the big states' 0.714, so 5, 5, 1. The small state loses a seat as the house grows: multiplying every quota by 11/10 grew the big states' fractional parts by 0.429 each and the small state's by only 0.143.
  2. Quotas 7.8, 1.2, 1.2, 1.2, 0.6. With \(D = 72\): \(650/72 = 9.03\), \(100/72 = 1.39\), \(50/72 = 0.69\); rounding down gives 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, which is 12 seats. The big state gets 9 against a quota of 7.8, more than its quota rounded up. The smallest state gets nothing despite a quota of 0.6.
  3. Plurality: A 5, B 4, C 3, A wins. Runoff A against B: C's voters prefer B, so B wins 7 to 5. Head to head B beats A 7–5 and C 9–3, so B is the Condorcet winner, and plurality failed to elect it.
  4. Without C, its three voters go to B: B 7, A 5, B wins. The A-versus-B result changed because of C, who was not going to win either way: a violation of independence of irrelevant alternatives (3). Everyday name: the spoiler effect.

Stretch. (a) Hamilton starts every state at \(\lfloor q \rfloor\) and adds at most one seat, so each ends at \(\lfloor q \rfloor\) or \(\lfloor q \rfloor + 1\). (b) Dictatorship: it gives a ranking for any input (1); if everyone, including the dictator, ranks A above B so does the output (2); the output's A-versus-B order is the dictator's, which depends on nothing else (3). It fails only (4). (c) A Condorcet cycle makes pairwise majority rule output A > B > C > A, which is not a ranking, so the rule has no answer for that profile and fails (1). Every repair lets a third candidate's position change the A-versus-B outcome, the failure of (3) that Arrow proved unavoidable.
