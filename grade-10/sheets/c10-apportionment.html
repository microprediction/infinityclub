Fair shares: apportionment and Arrow's theorem

Why the US House had a paradox, Hamilton versus Jefferson, and why no voting system is perfect.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The US Constitution says seats in the House of Representatives are shared among the states in proportion to population. Easy: state's population divided by the national population, times the number of seats. Except that gives 7.8 seats, and you cannot send 0.8 of a person to Washington. Deciding how to round is called apportionment, and it caused the first presidential veto in American history.

Hamilton's method (1792) is the obvious one. Compute each state's exact share, its quota. Give every state the whole-number part. Then hand out the leftover seats, one each, to the states with the largest fractional parts. Washington vetoed it, and Congress adopted Jefferson's method instead: pick a divisor \(D\), give each state \(\lfloor \text{population}/D \rfloor\) seats, and adjust \(D\) until the seats add up to the house size. Rounding everything down systematically favours big states, which is not unrelated to Jefferson being from Virginia. Webster's method (1832) is the same idea with ordinary rounding.

Hamilton's method came back in 1850 and ran into trouble in 1880, when a Census clerk, C. W. Seaton, computed the apportionment for every house size from 275 to 350 and found that Alabama would get 8 seats in a house of 299 but only 7 in a house of 300. More seats in total, fewer for Alabama. This is the Alabama paradox, and it happens because when the house grows every quota grows, but big states' fractional parts can overtake a small state's. Later clerks found the population paradox (a state can grow faster than another and still lose a seat to it) and the new-states paradox.

Divisor methods like Jefferson's and Webster's are immune to all three paradoxes. But they can violate quota: a state whose exact share is 7.8 can end up with 9 seats, or 6. In 1982 Michel Balinski and Peyton Young proved there is no way out: no apportionment method can both stay within quota and avoid the population paradox. Fairness has to be defined by choosing which failure you can live with. The US has used the Huntington–Hill method (rounding at the geometric mean) since 1941.

Voting has the same flavour, only worse. In How an election can have no winner you met the Condorcet cycle: majorities can prefer A to B, B to C and C to A. In 1951 Kenneth Arrow asked what a fair way of turning everyone's rankings into one group ranking would look like, and wrote down four modest demands: (1) it works for any set of rankings; (2) unanimity: if everyone ranks A above B, so does the group; (3) independence of irrelevant alternatives: the group's ranking of A against B depends only on how voters rank A against B, not on where they put C; (4) no dictator: no single voter's ranking is automatically the group's. Arrow's theorem: with three or more options, no rule satisfies all four. Every voting system you have ever heard of violates (3), which is why "spoiler" candidates can swing elections.

Watch one: Hamilton with three states

Populations 7, 6 and 2 million; 10 seats. Total population 15 million, so one seat per 1.5 million. Quotas: \(7/1.5 = 4.667\), \(6/1.5 = 4\), \(2/1.5 = 1.333\). Whole parts: 4, 4, 1, which is 9 seats. One seat left over, and the largest fractional part is state A's 0.667. Final: 5, 4, 1.

Jefferson for the same case: try \(D = 1.4\) million: \(\lfloor 5 \rfloor, \lfloor 4.29 \rfloor, \lfloor 1.43 \rfloor = 5, 4, 1\). Ten seats, same answer. The methods usually agree. The interesting cases are the ones where they don't.

Your turn

  1. Four states with populations 453, 367, 697 and 283 thousand share 20 seats. Use Hamilton's method: compute the quotas to three decimals, the whole parts, and who gets the leftover seats. Then redo it for 21 seats. Did anybody lose a seat?
  2. The Alabama paradox. Three states with populations 6, 6 and 2 million. Apportion 10 seats by Hamilton's method, then 11 seats. What happens to the small state, and exactly why?
  3. Jefferson. Five states with populations 650, 100, 100, 100 and 50 thousand share 12 seats. Compute the quotas. Show that the divisor \(D = 72\) thousand works for Jefferson's method and write down the apportionment. Which state gets more than its quota rounded up? Which state gets nothing?
  4. Webster. Same five states, 12 seats, ordinary rounding. Show that \(D = 80\) thousand works, and compare the result with Hamilton's method on the same numbers.
  5. Twelve voters, three candidates. 5 rank A > B > C, 4 rank B > C > A, 3 rank C > B > A. Who wins by plurality (most first places)? By a runoff between the top two? By Borda count (2 points for a first place, 1 for a second)? Is there a Condorcet winner, a candidate who beats each of the others head to head?
  6. The spoiler. In question 5, suppose C withdraws before the election and C's voters vote for their second choice. Who wins by plurality now? Which of Arrow's four conditions does plurality voting violate here, and what is the everyday name for this effect?

Stretch: the theorem that says you must choose

Hamilton's method always stays within quota: every state gets either the whole part of its quota or one more. Explain why that is built into the method. Jefferson's can leave quota, as you saw in question 3. Balinski and Young proved no method can satisfy quota and avoid the population paradox.

Now Arrow. Dictatorship satisfies conditions (1), (2) and (3): check each one. So the theorem is really saying that the only rules which respect (1) to (3) are dictatorships. Then explain why a Condorcet cycle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A by majority) makes "just use majority rule between each pair" fail condition (1), and why that is the seed of the whole problem.

Answers

  1. Total 1,800 thousand; 20 seats means 90 thousand per seat. Quotas 5.033, 4.078, 7.744, 3.144; whole parts 5, 4, 7, 3 (19 seats); the largest fraction is 0.744, so the third state gets the extra: 5, 4, 8, 3. For 21 seats the divisor is 85.71 thousand: quotas 5.285, 4.282, 8.132, 3.302; whole parts 5, 4, 8, 3 (20); largest fraction 0.302 goes to the fourth state: 5, 4, 8, 4. Nobody lost a seat this time.
  2. Ten seats: quotas 4.286, 4.286, 1.429; whole parts 4, 4, 1; the largest fraction (0.429) is the small state's, so 4, 4, 2. Eleven seats: quotas 4.714, 4.714, 1.571; whole parts 4, 4, 1; two seats left and the two largest fractions are now the big states' 0.714, so 5, 5, 1. The small state loses a seat as the house grows. Adding a seat multiplies every quota by 11/10, and the big states' fractional parts grew by more (0.429 each) than the small state's (0.143), overtaking it.
  3. Total 1,000 thousand; quotas 7.8, 1.2, 1.2, 1.2, 0.6. With \(D = 72\): \(650/72 = 9.03\), \(100/72 = 1.39\), \(50/72 = 0.69\); rounding down gives 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, which is 12 seats, so the divisor works. The big state gets 9 against a quota of 7.8: more than its quota rounded up, a quota violation. The smallest state gets nothing at all despite a quota of 0.6.
  4. With \(D = 80\): \(8.125, 1.25, 1.25, 1.25, 0.625\) rounds to 8, 1, 1, 1, 1, which is 12. Hamilton: whole parts 7, 1, 1, 1, 0 (10 seats); the two largest fractions are 0.8 and 0.6, so 8, 1, 1, 1, 1. Webster and Hamilton agree here, and both stay within quota; Jefferson was the odd one out.
  5. Plurality: A 5, B 4, C 3, A wins. Runoff between A and B: C's three voters prefer B, so B beats A 7 to 5, B wins. Borda: A gets \(5 \times 2 = 10\); B gets \(5 \times 1 + 4 \times 2 + 3 \times 1 = 16\); C gets \(4 \times 1 + 3 \times 2 = 10\); B wins. Head to head: B beats A 7–5 and B beats C 9–3, so B is the Condorcet winner, and plurality failed to elect it.
  6. Without C, the three C voters go to B: B 7, A 5, B wins. Whether A or B wins changed because of how voters felt about C, who was not going to win either way: a violation of independence of irrelevant alternatives. Everyday name: the spoiler effect (C "spoiled" the election for B).

Stretch. Hamilton starts every state at the whole part of its quota and adds at most one seat each, so each state ends at \(\lfloor q \rfloor\) or \(\lfloor q \rfloor + 1\): quota is automatic. Dictatorship: it produces a ranking for any input (1); if everyone, including the dictator, ranks A above B then so does the output (2); the output's A-versus-B order is the dictator's A-versus-B order, which depends on nothing else (3). It fails only (4). A Condorcet cycle means pairwise majority rule produces A > B > C > A, which is not a ranking at all, so the rule has no output for that profile and fails (1). Every repair of that failure, such as picking a winner by plurality or by Borda, lets a third candidate's position change the A-versus-B outcome, and that is the failure of (3) that Arrow proved is unavoidable.
