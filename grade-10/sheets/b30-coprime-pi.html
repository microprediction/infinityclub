Why π knows whether a fraction cancels

Pick two whole numbers at random. The chance that their fraction is already in lowest terms has π in it.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Choose two whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\), each from 1 to \(N\), every choice equally likely. The fraction \(\tfrac{a}{b}\) cancels exactly when \(a\) and \(b\) share a prime factor. Otherwise \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\) and the pair is called coprime. How likely is that?

Start with \(N = 6\). The 36 ordered pairs make a grid. Shade a cell when \(a\) and \(b\) share a factor.

Both even: \(3 \times 3 = 9\) cells. Both multiples of 3: \(2 \times 2 = 4\). Both multiples of 5: just \((5, 5)\). But \((6, 6)\) is both even and a multiple of 3, so it was shaded twice. Add it back once:

\(36 - 9 - 4 - 1 + 1 = 23\) coprime pairs, a proportion of \(\tfrac{23}{36} \approx 0.639\). Subtract, then add back what was subtracted twice: inclusion–exclusion, the same bookkeeping as in probability.

For a prime \(p\), about \(\tfrac1p\) of the numbers up to \(N\) are multiples of \(p\), so about \(\tfrac{1}{p^2}\) of the pairs share \(p\) and a share \(1 - \tfrac{1}{p^2}\) avoids it. On a big grid the primes act almost independently, which is where the stretch begins.

Watch one: bigger grids

Counting \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\) over every cell of an \(N \times N\) grid gives these exact totals:

\(N\)61020100
coprime ordered pairs23632556087
proportion0.63890.63000.63750.6087

Guess where the proportion is heading before you read on. It wobbles, since each new \(N\) adds a row and a column, but the wobbles shrink. As \(N\) grows without bound the proportion tends to a limit, and the limit is

\(\dfrac{6}{\pi^2} \approx 0.6079\). This is a claim about the limit of finite grids. For any particular \(N\) the proportion is an ordinary fraction, and there is no such thing as a uniformly random positive integer. The \(N = 1000\) grid gives 608 383 coprime pairs out of a million.

Your turn

  1. For \(N = 10\), count the pairs that share 2, share 3, share 5 and share 7. Then count the pairs that share 6 and the pairs that share 10. Use inclusion–exclusion to confirm the total of 63 coprime pairs.
  2. Explain why exactly \(\tfrac14\) of the 36 cells have both \(a\) and \(b\) even, but for \(N = 7\) the fraction of both-even cells is not exactly \(\tfrac14\). What does that fraction approach as \(N\) grows?
  3. The cell \((a, a)\) is coprime only when \(a = 1\), and the grid is symmetric about that diagonal. Show that the number of coprime ordered pairs is odd for every \(N\). Check against the table.
  4. Simulate it. With a spreadsheet, draw 200 pairs from 1 to 1000 and count those with \(\mathrm{GCD}(a, b) = 1\). Record \(N\), the sample size and the proportion. How far from 0.608 would be surprising?

Stretch: the product over primes

Multiply \(1 - \tfrac{1}{p^2}\) over the primes 2, 3, 5, 7. Then write each \(\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{p^2}\big)^{-1}\) as a geometric series \(1 + \tfrac{1}{p^2} + \tfrac{1}{p^4} + \cdots\) and multiply those out. Using unique factorisation, which sum from Corner 12 appears, and why is the limit \(6/\pi^2\)?

Answers

  1. Share 2: \(5 \times 5 = 25\). Share 3: \(3 \times 3 = 9\). Share 5: \(2 \times 2 = 4\). Share 7: 1. Share 6: only \((6, 6)\), 1 cell. Share 10: only \((10, 10)\), 1 cell. No pair shares 14 or 15. Total \(100 - 25 - 9 - 4 - 1 + 1 + 1 = 63\).
  2. Up to 6 there are exactly 3 even numbers, so \(3 \times 3 = 9 = \tfrac14 \times 36\). Up to 7 there are still 3 evens, giving \(\tfrac{9}{49}\), not \(\tfrac14\). The fraction approaches \(\tfrac14\) as \(N\) grows, since the number of evens is always within one of \(N/2\).
  3. Off the diagonal, coprime cells come in mirror pairs, \((a, b)\) with \((b, a)\): an even count. The diagonal contributes exactly one coprime cell, \((1, 1)\). Even plus one is odd. The table shows 23, 63, 255 and 6087, all odd.
  4. Answers vary. Each draw is coprime with probability about 0.608, so the count out of 200 has standard deviation \(\sqrt{200 \times 0.608 \times 0.392} \approx 6.9\), about 0.035 as a proportion. A result outside roughly 0.54 to 0.68 would be surprising.

Stretch. \(\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac89 \cdot \tfrac{24}{25} \cdot \tfrac{48}{49} \approx 0.627\); taking primes up to 50 brings it to 0.610. Multiplying the series out, every product \(\tfrac{1}{p^{2k} q^{2m} \cdots}\) is \(\tfrac{1}{n^2}\) for exactly one \(n\), because each \(n\) factorises in exactly one way. So the product of all the \(\dfrac{1}{1 - 1/p^2}\) is \(1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac19 + \cdots = \tfrac{\pi^2}{6}\), and the product of the \(1 - \tfrac{1}{p^2}\), the limiting coprime proportion, is its reciprocal \(\tfrac{6}{\pi^2}\). A rigorous version handles finitely many primes at a time and bounds what the rest can change.
