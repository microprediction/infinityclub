Bayes' theorem

Update a belief with evidence. Base rates, the prosecutor's fallacy, and a spam filter in four lines.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The probability of \(A\) given \(B\) is written \(P(A \mid B)\) and defined as the share of the \(B\)-cases that are also \(A\)-cases:

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}. \]

Swap the letters and the same definition gives \(P(B \mid A) = P(A \text{ and } B)/P(A)\). Both equations contain \(P(A \text{ and } B)\). Set the two expressions for it equal, \(P(A\mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)\), and divide by \(P(B)\):

\(\displaystyle P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}\). That is Bayes' theorem. It turns "how likely is the evidence if the hypothesis is true" into "how likely is the hypothesis now that we have seen the evidence".

The names: \(P(A)\) is the prior, what you believed before. \(P(B \mid A)\) is the likelihood. \(P(A \mid B)\) is the posterior. The denominator \(P(B)\) is the total probability of the evidence, adding up both ways it can happen: \(P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)P(\text{not } A)\).

Natural frequencies. The formula is easy to misapply and hard to feel. Imagining 10,000 people fixes both. Suppose 1% of people have a disease, the test catches 99% of cases (sensitivity) and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people (false positive rate). Out of 10,000 people: 100 are sick, and 99 of them test positive. The other 9,900 are healthy, and 5% of them, 495, also test positive. So 594 people test positive and only 99 are sick. \(P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive}) = 99/594 \approx 17\%\). A "99% accurate" test, and five out of six positives are false alarms. The base rate of 1% did that, and ignoring it is the most common probability mistake there is.

The odds form. Divide Bayes for \(A\) by Bayes for not-\(A\) and \(P(B)\) cancels: \(\dfrac{P(A \mid B)}{P(\text{not }A \mid B)} = \dfrac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid \text{not }A)} \times \dfrac{P(A)}{P(\text{not }A)}\). In words: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. Each new independent piece of evidence just multiplies the odds by its own ratio. Bookmakers and spam filters think this way.

The prosecutor's fallacy. "The chance that an innocent person's DNA matches is one in a million, so the chance this defendant is innocent is one in a million." No. The first number is \(P(\text{match} \mid \text{innocent})\); the court needs \(P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{match})\). In a city of 10 million, about 10 innocent people match. If the match is the only evidence, the defendant is one of 11 matching people and \(P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{match}) \approx 10/11\). Courts have convicted on exactly this confusion.

A spam filter in four lines. Suppose 40% of your mail is spam, the word "prize" appears in 60% of spam and 2% of real mail. A message contains "prize". Spam route: \(0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.24\). Real route: \(0.6 \times 0.02 = 0.012\). \(P(\text{spam} \mid \text{prize}) = 0.24/(0.24 + 0.012) \approx 0.95\). Real filters multiply the ratios for every word in the message (that is the "naive" in naive Bayes: they pretend the words are independent) and it works astonishingly well.

Watch one: the two coins

A bag holds two coins: one fair, one with heads on both sides. You pull one out without looking, flip it, and get heads. What is the probability you are holding the double-headed coin?

Formula. Prior \(P(\text{DH}) = \tfrac12\). Likelihoods: \(P(\text{H} \mid \text{DH}) = 1\), \(P(\text{H} \mid \text{fair}) = \tfrac12\). Evidence: \(P(\text{H}) = 1 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34\). Posterior: \(P(\text{DH} \mid \text{H}) = \dfrac{1 \cdot \tfrac12}{\tfrac34} = \tfrac23\).

Frequencies. Do it 400 times. In 200 you hold the double-header and see 200 heads. In 200 you hold the fair coin and see about 100 heads. Of the 300 heads, 200 came from the double-header: \(200/300 = \tfrac23\). Same answer, no formula.

Update again. Flip the same coin a second time: heads again. Now the posterior \(\tfrac23\) becomes the new prior. Odds form: prior odds \(2{:}1\), likelihood ratio \(1/\tfrac12 = 2\), posterior odds \(4{:}1\), so \(P(\text{DH}) = \tfrac45\). Each extra head multiplies the odds by 2. Ten heads in a row: odds \(1024{:}1\), still not certain, because a fair coin can do that too.

Your turn

  1. A disease affects 1 person in 1,000. The test has sensitivity 99% and specificity 99% (it wrongly flags 1% of healthy people). Imagine 100,000 people. How many are sick, how many of them test positive, how many healthy people test positive, and what is \(P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive})\)? Then say in one sentence why a "99% accurate" test is wrong most of the time here.
  2. Same test, but now the patient has symptoms and the doctor's prior is 20%. Redo the calculation with 10,000 people. What changed, and what does that say about ordering tests for people with no symptoms?
  3. Prosecutor's fallacy. A DNA profile is shared by 1 person in 100,000. A city has 2,000,000 people. A database search finds one match and there is no other evidence. About how many innocent people in the city share the profile, and what is \(P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{match})\)? Now suppose other evidence had already narrowed the suspects to 200 people before the DNA test. Recompute.
  4. Spam filter. 30% of your mail is spam. The word "free" appears in 50% of spam and 5% of real mail. A new message contains "free": \(P(\text{spam})\)? It also contains "meeting", which appears in 2% of spam and 20% of real mail. Treating the words as independent, multiply both likelihood ratios and find the new \(P(\text{spam})\).
  5. Monty Hall, properly. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is and always opens a goat door (choosing at random if he has two), opens door 3. Write down \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car behind 1})\), \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car behind 2})\) and \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car behind 3})\), then use Bayes to find \(P(\text{car behind 2} \mid \text{opens 3})\). (Grade 6 did this with a tree: Monty Hall. Tonight it is one line.)
  6. Three suspects. A factory has three machines. Machine 1 makes 50% of the widgets and 3% of its output is faulty; machine 2 makes 30% with 4% faulty; machine 3 makes 20% with 5% faulty. A widget is faulty. Find the probability it came from each machine. Check your three answers add to 1.

Stretch: the taxi problem

A taxi was involved in a hit-and-run at night. In this city 85% of taxis are green and 15% are blue. A witness says the taxi was blue. Tested under the same night conditions, the witness identifies colours correctly 80% of the time. What is the probability the taxi was actually blue? Most people say 80%. Use the odds form: prior odds blue to green, times the likelihood ratio.

Now a second, independent witness also says blue. Update again. Then find how reliable a single witness would have to be for "blue" to be more likely than not. (Set posterior odds equal to 1 and solve for the reliability \(w\).)

Answers

  1. 100 sick, 99 test positive. 99,900 healthy, 999 test positive. \(P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive}) = 99/1098 \approx 9.0\%\). The 1% error rate is applied to a huge healthy population, so false positives (999) swamp true positives (99): the base rate matters more than the accuracy.
  2. 2,000 sick, 1,980 positive. 8,000 healthy, 80 positive. \(1980/2060 \approx 96.1\%\). Only the prior changed. The same test is nearly useless for screening everyone and nearly decisive for someone with symptoms, which is why screening programmes for rare conditions are controversial.
  3. Innocent matches expected: \(2{,}000{,}000 \times 1/100{,}000 = 20\). The culprit is one of about 21 matching people, so \(P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{match}) \approx 1/21 \approx 4.8\%\). With 200 suspects: expected innocent matches \(199/100{,}000 \approx 0.002\), so \(P(\text{guilty}) \approx 1/1.002 \approx 99.8\%\). Same test, same match; the prior is everything.
  4. Spam route \(0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15\); real route \(0.7 \times 0.05 = 0.035\); \(P(\text{spam}) = 0.15/0.185 \approx 0.81\). Add "meeting": spam route \(0.15 \times 0.02 = 0.003\); real route \(0.035 \times 0.2 = 0.007\); \(P(\text{spam}) = 0.003/0.010 = 0.30\). In odds: prior \(3{:}7\), times 10 for "free", times \(0.1\) for "meeting", back to \(3{:}7\).
  5. \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car 1}) = \tfrac12\) (he could open 2 or 3), \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car 2}) = 1\) (door 2 is off limits), \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car 3}) = 0\). Priors all \(\tfrac13\). \(P(\text{car 2} \mid \text{opens 3}) = \dfrac{1 \cdot \tfrac13}{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13 + 1\cdot\tfrac13 + 0} = \dfrac{\tfrac13}{\tfrac12} = \tfrac23\). Switch.
  6. Routes: \(0.5 \times 0.03 = 0.015\), \(0.3 \times 0.04 = 0.012\), \(0.2 \times 0.05 = 0.010\); total \(0.037\). Machine 1: \(0.015/0.037 \approx 0.405\). Machine 2: \(0.012/0.037 \approx 0.324\). Machine 3: \(0.010/0.037 \approx 0.270\). Sum \(= 1\). Machine 3 is the least reliable but still the least likely culprit, because it makes so few widgets.

Stretch. Prior odds blue:green \(= 15{:}85 = 3{:}17\). Likelihood ratio \(= 0.8/0.2 = 4\). Posterior odds \(12{:}17\), so \(P(\text{blue}) = 12/29 \approx 0.41\). Despite an 80% reliable witness, green is still more likely. Second witness: odds \(48{:}17\), \(P(\text{blue}) = 48/65 \approx 0.74\). For one witness to make blue more likely than not: \(\dfrac{3}{17} \times \dfrac{w}{1-w} = 1\), so \(\dfrac{w}{1-w} = \dfrac{17}{3}\), \(w = 0.85\). The witness must be exactly as reliable as the base rate is lopsided. (This is Kahneman and Tversky's taxi problem; most people answer 80% and never think about the 85%.)
