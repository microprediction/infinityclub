Bayes' theorem

Update a belief with evidence. Base rates, the prosecutor's fallacy, and a spam filter in four lines.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The probability of \(A\) given \(B\), written \(P(A \mid B)\), is the share of the \(B\)-cases that are also \(A\)-cases: \(P(A \mid B) = P(A \text{ and } B)/P(B)\). Swap the letters: \(P(B \mid A) = P(A \text{ and } B)/P(A)\). Equate the two expressions for \(P(A \text{ and } B)\) and divide by \(P(B)\):

\(\displaystyle P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}\). Bayes' theorem turns "how likely is the evidence if the hypothesis is true" into "how likely is the hypothesis now that we have the evidence".

\(P(A)\) is the prior, \(P(B \mid A)\) the likelihood, \(P(A \mid B)\) the posterior. The denominator adds up both routes to the evidence: \(P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)P(\text{not } A)\).

Natural frequencies. The formula is easy to misapply; imagining 10,000 people fixes that. Say 1% have a disease, the test catches 99% of cases and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people. Of 10,000: 100 are sick and 99 test positive; 9,900 are healthy and 495 test positive. So \(P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive}) = 99/594 \approx 17\%\). A "99% accurate" test, and five out of six positives are false alarms. That is the base rate at work.

Odds form. Divide Bayes for \(A\) by Bayes for not-\(A\) and \(P(B)\) cancels: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. Each independent piece of evidence multiplies the odds by its own ratio.

Watch one: the two coins

A bag holds a fair coin and a double-headed coin. You pull one out, flip it, and get heads. Which coin is it?

Formula. Prior \(\tfrac12\). Likelihoods \(P(\text{H} \mid \text{DH}) = 1\), \(P(\text{H} \mid \text{fair}) = \tfrac12\). \(P(\text{H}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\). Posterior \(P(\text{DH} \mid \text{H}) = \tfrac12 / \tfrac34 = \tfrac23\).

Frequencies. Do it 400 times: 200 double-headers give 200 heads, 200 fair coins give about 100. Of 300 heads, 200 came from the double-header: \(\tfrac23\).

Update again. A second flip is also heads. Odds form: prior odds \(2{:}1\), likelihood ratio 2, posterior odds \(4{:}1\), so \(P(\text{DH}) = \tfrac45\). Ten heads in a row: odds \(1024{:}1\), still not certain.

Your turn

  1. A disease affects 1 person in 1,000. The test has sensitivity 99% and wrongly flags 1% of healthy people. Imagine 100,000 people: how many are sick, how many of them test positive, how many healthy people test positive, and what is \(P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive})\)? (Demo: infinityclub.net/demos/bayes.html.)
  2. Prosecutor's fallacy. A DNA profile is shared by 1 person in 100,000. A city has 2,000,000 people, a database search finds one match, and there is no other evidence. About how many innocent people share the profile, and what is \(P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{match})\)?
  3. Spam filter. 30% of your mail is spam. "free" appears in 50% of spam and 5% of real mail. A message contains "free": \(P(\text{spam})\)? It also contains "meeting" (2% of spam, 20% of real mail). Multiply both likelihood ratios and find the new \(P(\text{spam})\).
  4. Monty Hall in one line. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is and always opens a goat door (at random if he has two), opens door 3. Write down \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car behind } 1)\), \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car behind } 2)\), \(P(\text{opens 3} \mid \text{car behind } 3)\), and use Bayes to find \(P(\text{car behind } 2 \mid \text{opens 3})\).

Stretch: the taxi problem

A taxi was in a hit-and-run at night. In this city 85% of taxis are green and 15% blue. A witness says blue, and under night conditions the witness gets colours right 80% of the time. Most people answer 80%. Use the odds form. Then a second, independent witness also says blue: update again.

Answers

  1. 100 sick, 99 positive. 99,900 healthy, 999 positive. \(P(\text{sick} \mid \text{positive}) = 99/1098 \approx 9\%\). The 1% error rate acts on a huge healthy population, so false positives swamp true ones.
  2. Innocent matches: \(2{,}000{,}000 / 100{,}000 = 20\). The culprit is one of about 21 matching people, so \(P(\text{guilty} \mid \text{match}) \approx 1/21 \approx 5\%\), not \(99.999\%\). The "one in 100,000" is \(P(\text{match} \mid \text{innocent})\), the wrong way round.
  3. Spam route \(0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.15\); real route \(0.7 \times 0.05 = 0.035\); \(P(\text{spam}) = 0.15/0.185 \approx 0.81\). Add "meeting": \(0.15 \times 0.02 = 0.003\) against \(0.035 \times 0.2 = 0.007\); \(P(\text{spam}) = 0.3\). In odds: \(3{:}7\), times 10, times \(0.1\), back to \(3{:}7\).
  4. \(\tfrac12\) (he could open 2 or 3), \(1\) (door 2 is off limits), \(0\). Priors \(\tfrac13\) each. \(P(\text{car } 2 \mid \text{opens 3}) = \dfrac{1 \cdot \tfrac13}{\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13 + 1 \cdot \tfrac13 + 0} = \tfrac23\). Switch.

Stretch. Prior odds blue:green \(= 15{:}85 = 3{:}17\). Likelihood ratio \(0.8/0.2 = 4\). Posterior odds \(12{:}17\), so \(P(\text{blue}) = 12/29 \approx 0.41\): green is still more likely. Second witness: odds \(48{:}17\), \(P(\text{blue}) = 48/65 \approx 0.74\). (Kahneman and Tversky's taxi problem; most people answer 80% and never think about the 85%.)
