Inequalities, absolute value, and sign charts

Absolute value as distance, quadratic inequalities by sign chart, and the AM–GM inequality.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

An equation has a handful of solutions. An inequality has a whole region of them, and the skill is describing that region without missing a piece or including a wrong one. Three tools do almost everything.

1. Absolute value is distance. \(|x - a|\) is the distance from \(x\) to \(a\) on the number line. So \(|x - 3| \lt 5\) says "within 5 of 3": \(-2 \lt x \lt 8\). And \(|x - 3| \gt 5\) says "further than 5 from 3": \(x \lt -2\) or \(x \gt 8\), two separate pieces. Read it as distance and you will never confuse the two.

2. Multiplying by a negative flips the sign. \(2 \lt 3\), but \(-2 \gt -3\). So \(-3x \gt 6\) becomes \(x \lt -2\). The common trap is multiplying through by something whose sign you do not know, like \(x - 3\): it is positive for some \(x\) and negative for others, so the inequality would flip for some solutions and not others. Do not do it. Move everything to one side instead.

3. Sign charts. A product or quotient changes sign only where a factor is zero (or undefined). Mark those points on a number line, test one value in each interval, and read off where the expression is positive or negative. For \((x-2)(x+3) \gt 0\): the critical points are \(-3\) and \(2\); test \(x = -4\) (positive), \(x = 0\) (negative), \(x = 3\) (positive). Solution: \(x \lt -3\) or \(x \gt 2\).

And one inequality that is a theorem rather than a problem. For any non-negative \(a, b\),

\(\dfrac{a + b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}\), with equality exactly when \(a = b\).

The arithmetic mean is at least the geometric mean, AM–GM. The proof is one line: \((\sqrt a - \sqrt b)^2 \ge 0\) because squares are never negative; expand to get \(a - 2\sqrt{ab} + b \ge 0\), and rearrange. It answers questions like "what is the biggest rectangle you can fence with 40 m of wire?" without calculus.

Watch one: three kinds

\(|2x - 1| \le 7\). Within 7 of 0: \(-7 \le 2x - 1 \le 7\). Add 1 everywhere: \(-6 \le 2x \le 8\). Halve: \(-3 \le x \le 4\).

\(x^2 - x - 6 \lt 0\). Factor: \((x - 3)(x + 2) \lt 0\). Critical points \(-2\) and \(3\). Test \(x = 0\): \((-3)(2) = -6\), negative, so the middle interval works; the outer intervals give positive products. Solution \(-2 \lt x \lt 3\). (Check: a parabola opening upward is below the axis between its roots.)

\(\dfrac{x - 1}{x + 2} \ge 0\). Critical points \(1\) (numerator zero, allowed, since \(\ge\)) and \(-2\) (denominator zero, never allowed). Test \(x = -3\): \(\tfrac{-4}{-1} = 4 \gt 0\), yes. \(x = 0\): \(\tfrac{-1}{2}\), no. \(x = 2\): \(\tfrac14\), yes. Solution: \(x \lt -2\) or \(x \ge 1\). Note the mixed brackets: open at \(-2\), closed at \(1\).

Your turn

  1. Solve, and say each answer in words as a distance: \(|x + 4| \lt 3\); \(|3 - x| \ge 2\); \(|2x + 5| \gt -1\).
  2. Solve \(-3(x - 2) \ge 12\). Then solve the double inequality \(2 \lt 5 - x \le 9\), keeping all three parts together, and say at which step the direction flips.
  3. Solve \(x^2 \ge 16\) (many people write only half the answer; draw the parabola to see why there are two pieces). Then \(x^2 - 5x \le 0\) and \(2x^2 - 7x + 3 \gt 0\) by sign chart.
  4. Solve \(\dfrac{x + 1}{x - 3} \lt 2\). Do not multiply both sides by \(x - 3\); move the 2 across, combine into one fraction, and make a sign chart. Then explain what goes wrong if you do multiply through.
  5. Use AM–GM. (a) A rectangle has perimeter 40 m. Show its area is at most 100 m² and say which rectangle achieves it. (b) Show \(x + \dfrac1x \ge 2\) for every \(x \gt 0\). (c) Find the smallest value of \(x + \dfrac9x\) for \(x \gt 0\) and the \(x\) that gives it.
  6. Solve \(|x - 1| + |x + 2| \le 5\) by splitting into the three cases \(x \ge 1\), \(-2 \le x \lt 1\), \(x \lt -2\). Then describe the solution geometrically: the sum of the distances from \(x\) to \(1\) and to \(-2\) is at most 5.

Stretch: the triangle inequality

Prove that for all real \(a, b\), \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\). Hint: both sides are non-negative, so it is enough to compare their squares. Expand \((a+b)^2\) and \((|a| + |b|)^2\), and notice that \(ab \le |a||b|\) always.

Then use it, with a clever choice of \(a\) and \(b\), to show \(|x| - |y| \le |x - y|\). Why is it called the triangle inequality? Draw \(a\) and \(b\) as arrows in the plane rather than numbers on a line, and look at the three lengths.

Answers

  1. \(|x + 4| \lt 3\): within 3 of \(-4\), so \(-7 \lt x \lt -1\). \(|3 - x| = |x - 3| \ge 2\): at least 2 away from 3, so \(x \le 1\) or \(x \ge 5\). \(|2x + 5| \gt -1\): an absolute value is never negative, so this holds for every real \(x\).
  2. \(-3(x-2) \ge 12 \Rightarrow x - 2 \le -4\) (dividing by \(-3\) flips) \(\Rightarrow x \le -2\). Double inequality: subtract 5, \(-3 \lt -x \le 4\); multiply by \(-1\) and flip both signs, \(3 \gt x \ge -4\), that is \(-4 \le x \lt 3\). The flip happens at the multiplication by \(-1\).
  3. \(x^2 \ge 16 \Leftrightarrow x^2 - 16 \ge 0 \Leftrightarrow (x-4)(x+4) \ge 0\): \(x \le -4\) or \(x \ge 4\). The parabola \(y = x^2\) is above 16 on both sides. \(x^2 - 5x = x(x - 5) \le 0\): between the roots, \(0 \le x \le 5\). \(2x^2 - 7x + 3 = (2x - 1)(x - 3) \gt 0\): outside the roots, \(x \lt \tfrac12\) or \(x \gt 3\).
  4. \(\dfrac{x+1}{x-3} - 2 \lt 0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{x + 1 - 2(x - 3)}{x - 3} = \dfrac{7 - x}{x - 3} \lt 0\). Critical points 3 (excluded) and 7. Test 0: \(\tfrac{7}{-3} \lt 0\) yes; 5: \(\tfrac22 \gt 0\) no; 8: \(\tfrac{-1}{5} \lt 0\) yes. Solution \(x \lt 3\) or \(x \gt 7\). Multiplying through by \(x - 3\) treats it as positive and gives \(x + 1 \lt 2x - 6\), i.e. \(x \gt 7\), silently losing every solution below 3, where \(x - 3\) is negative and the inequality should have flipped.
  5. (a) Sides \(x, y\) with \(x + y = 20\). AM–GM: \(\sqrt{xy} \le \tfrac{x+y}{2} = 10\), so \(xy \le 100\), with equality when \(x = y = 10\): the square. (b) AM–GM on \(x\) and \(\tfrac1x\): \(\tfrac{x + 1/x}{2} \ge \sqrt{x \cdot \tfrac1x} = 1\). (c) \(\tfrac{x + 9/x}{2} \ge \sqrt{9} = 3\), so \(x + \tfrac9x \ge 6\), with equality when \(x = \tfrac9x\), i.e. \(x = 3\). Minimum 6.
  6. Case \(x \ge 1\): \((x-1) + (x+2) = 2x + 1 \le 5 \Rightarrow x \le 2\), giving \(1 \le x \le 2\). Case \(-2 \le x \lt 1\): \((1-x) + (x+2) = 3 \le 5\), always true, giving the whole interval. Case \(x \lt -2\): \((1-x) + (-x-2) = -2x - 1 \le 5 \Rightarrow x \ge -3\), giving \(-3 \le x \lt -2\). Union: \(-3 \le x \le 2\). Geometrically, the two points are 3 apart, so anywhere between them the distances sum to exactly 3; outside, each unit you move adds 2 to the sum, and you may move 1 unit past either end.

Stretch. \((a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\) and \((|a|+|b|)^2 = a^2 + 2|a||b| + b^2\). Since \(ab \le |ab| = |a||b|\), the first is at most the second; both bases are non-negative, so \(|a+b| \le |a|+|b|\). For the second claim put \(a = x - y\), \(b = y\): \(|x| = |(x-y) + y| \le |x-y| + |y|\), so \(|x| - |y| \le |x-y|\). With arrows, \(a\) and \(b\) placed head to tail form two sides of a triangle and \(a + b\) is the third: one side of a triangle is never longer than the other two together.
