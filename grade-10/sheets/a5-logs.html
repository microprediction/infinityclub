A logarithm is an exponent

The log laws are the exponent laws in disguise. Solve 2ˣ = 10, and find out how many digits 2¹⁰⁰⁰ has.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

\(\log_2 8\) is a question: what power of 2 gives 8? The answer is 3. That is the whole definition:

\(\log_b x = y\) means exactly \(b^y = x\). A logarithm is an exponent.

So \(\log_{10} 1000 = 3\), \(\log_5 \tfrac{1}{25} = -2\), \(\log_9 3 = \tfrac12\), and \(\log_b 1 = 0\) for every base, because \(b^0 = 1\). Two bases get special buttons: \(\log\) with no base means base 10, and \(\ln\) means base \(e \approx 2.718\), which you will meet properly in The number e.

Every log law is an exponent law read backwards. Let \(x = b^m\) and \(y = b^n\), so \(m = \log_b x\) and \(n = \log_b y\).

  • \(xy = b^m b^n = b^{m+n}\), so \(\log_b(xy) = m + n = \log_b x + \log_b y\). Multiplying numbers adds their logs.
  • \(x/y = b^{m-n}\), so \(\log_b(x/y) = \log_b x - \log_b y\).
  • \(x^k = (b^m)^k = b^{mk}\), so \(\log_b(x^k) = k\log_b x\). Powers come down as multipliers. This one solves equations.

Change of base. Your calculator only has base 10 and base \(e\). To get \(\log_2 10\): let \(y = \log_2 10\), so \(2^y = 10\). Take base-10 logs of both sides: \(y \log 2 = \log 10 = 1\), so \(y = \dfrac{1}{\log 2} \approx 3.32\). In general \(\log_b x = \dfrac{\log x}{\log b}\), any base on the right as long as it is the same twice.

Counting digits. A number \(N\) has \(d\) digits when \(10^{d-1} \leq N \lt 10^d\), which is \(d - 1 \leq \log N \lt d\). So the number of digits is \(\lfloor \log_{10} N \rfloor + 1\). For \(2^{1000}\): \(\log_{10} 2^{1000} = 1000 \log_{10} 2 \approx 1000 \times 0.30103 = 301.03\), so \(2^{1000}\) has \(301 + 1 = 302\) digits. No computer needed.

Watch one: three equations

\(5 \cdot 3^x = 200\). Isolate the power: \(3^x = 40\). Take logs: \(x \ln 3 = \ln 40\), so \(x = \dfrac{\ln 40}{\ln 3} \approx 3.358\). (Sanity: \(3^3 = 27\) and \(3^4 = 81\), so a bit over 3.)

\(\log_2 x + \log_2(x - 2) = 3\). Combine: \(\log_2\big(x(x-2)\big) = 3\), so \(x(x - 2) = 2^3 = 8\), so \(x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0\), so \(x = 4\) or \(x = -2\). Reject \(-2\): you cannot take the log of a negative number. Answer \(x = 4\). Always check log equations against the domain.

\(\log_4 32\) without a calculator: \(4 = 2^2\) and \(32 = 2^5\), so \(4^y = 32\) means \(2^{2y} = 2^5\), \(y = \tfrac52\).

Your turn

  1. Evaluate without a calculator: \(\log_2 64\), \(\;\log_3 \tfrac{1}{27}\), \(\;\log_{10} 0.001\), \(\;\log_5 \sqrt5\), \(\;\log_8 4\), \(\;\ln e^3\).
  2. Write \(2\log x + \log y - 3\log z\) as a single logarithm. Then expand \(\log\dfrac{\sqrt a}{b^2 c}\) into separate logs.
  3. Solve, exactly where possible and to three decimal places otherwise: \(3^{2x} = 81\); \(\;7^x = 50\); \(\;2^{x+1} = 3^x\).
  4. How many digits are in \(3^{500}\)? In \(7^{77}\)? Use \(\log_{10} 3 \approx 0.47712\) and \(\log_{10} 7 \approx 0.84510\).
  5. Logarithmic scales. The Richter magnitude is \(\log_{10}\) of the shaking amplitude, so each step of 1 is ten times the amplitude; the energy released grows by a factor of about \(10^{1.5}\) per step. Compare a magnitude 7 earthquake to a magnitude 5: how many times the amplitude, and roughly how many times the energy? Decibels are \(10\log_{10}\) of sound intensity: how many times more intense is 80 dB than 60 dB? pH is \(-\log_{10}\) of hydrogen ion concentration: how does pH 3 compare with pH 6?
  6. Money grows at 6% per year, so it multiplies by 1.06 annually. How many years until it doubles? Bankers use the "rule of 72": doubling time \(\approx 72 / (\text{percent rate})\). Check the rule at 6%, 3% and 12% against the exact answers.

Stretch: a log you can prove is irrational

First a warm-up: show that \(\log_a b \cdot \log_b a = 1\) using change of base.

Now prove \(\log_2 3\) is irrational. Suppose it were a fraction \(p/q\) with positive whole numbers \(p, q\). Then \(2^{p/q} = 3\), so \(2^p = 3^q\). Why is that impossible? Then adapt the argument to prove \(\log_{10} 2\) is irrational, which means the decimal \(0.30102999566\ldots\) never repeats.

Last: estimate the number of digits in \(100! = 1 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 100\) without a computer. The log of a product is the sum of the logs, so you need \(\log 1 + \log 2 + \cdots + \log 100\). Group the terms (all two-digit numbers have a log between 1 and 2, for instance) to trap the sum between two whole numbers, and then sharpen the estimate as much as you can. The true value is on the answers page.

Answers

  1. \(6\); \(\;-3\); \(\;-3\); \(\;\tfrac12\); \(\;\tfrac23\) (since \(8^{2/3} = 4\)); \(\;3\).
  2. \(\log\dfrac{x^2 y}{z^3}\). \(\;\tfrac12\log a - 2\log b - \log c\).
  3. \(3^{2x} = 3^4\), so \(x = 2\). \(\;x = \dfrac{\ln 50}{\ln 7} \approx 2.010\). \(\;(x+1)\ln 2 = x\ln 3\), so \(x(\ln 3 - \ln 2) = \ln 2\), \(x = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.5} \approx 1.710\).
  4. \(500 \times 0.47712 = 238.56\), so \(239\) digits. \(77 \times 0.84510 = 65.07\), so \(66\) digits.
  5. Amplitude \(10^2 = 100\) times; energy about \(10^3 = 1000\) times (\(10^{1.5} \approx 31.6\) per step, squared). 80 dB is \(10^{2} = 100\) times as intense as 60 dB. pH 3 has \(10^3 = 1000\) times the hydrogen ion concentration of pH 6.
  6. \(1.06^n = 2\), so \(n = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.06} \approx 11.9\) years; rule of 72 says 12. At 3%: exact 23.4, rule says 24. At 12%: exact 6.1, rule says 6. The rule is good to within a few percent for ordinary rates, because \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\) and \(\ln(1 + r) \approx r\) for small \(r\), giving \(0.693/r\), and 72 is a nearby number with many divisors.

Stretch. \(\log_a b \cdot \log_b a = \dfrac{\log b}{\log a} \cdot \dfrac{\log a}{\log b} = 1\). If \(2^p = 3^q\) with \(p, q \geq 1\), the left side is even and the right side is odd: impossible. So no such fraction exists and \(\log_2 3\) is irrational. For \(\log_{10} 2 = p/q\): \(10^p = 2^q\), but the left side is divisible by 5 and the right side is not. For \(100!\): the nine numbers 1 to 9 have logs between 0 and 1 (sum less than 9), and the ninety numbers 10 to 99 have logs between 1 and 2, with \(\log 100 = 2\). So the total is between 90 + 2 = 92 and 9 + 180 + 2 = 191, meaning somewhere between 93 and 192 digits. Sharpen by splitting 10–99 further: for example the fifty numbers 50–99 have logs above \(\log 50 \approx 1.7\), adding 85 on their own. The exact sum is \(\log_{10}(100!) \approx 157.97\), so \(100!\) has \(158\) digits. (A famous formula, Stirling's, gives \(157.97\) directly; you have just reinvented the reason it exists.)
