Groups: the mathematics of symmetry

Symmetries compose, every element has an inverse, and Lagrange's theorem says subgroups divide.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Label the corners of an equilateral triangle A, B, C. There are exactly six ways to put it back in its outline: do nothing (\(e\)); rotate by 120° (\(r\)) or 240° (\(r^2\)); or flip it about the axis through corner A (\(a\)), B (\(b\)) or C (\(c\)). These are the symmetries of the triangle.

Flip about A, then rotate by 120°: track the corners in the figure and the result is the single flip \(c\). We write \(r \circ a = c\), reading right to left. The other order gives \(a \circ r = b\). Order matters.

In the table, the entry in row \(g\), column \(h\) is \(g \circ h\): do \(h\) first, then \(g\). So row \(r\), column \(a\) gives \(c\).

Four things to notice. (1) Combining two symmetries gives a symmetry: no gaps. (2) There is a do-nothing element \(e\). (3) Every element can be undone: every row contains \(e\). (4) \((g \circ h) \circ k = g \circ (h \circ k)\). Any set with an operation obeying these four rules is a group. This one is \(D_3\), of order 6. The numbers \(\{0, \ldots, 5\}\) under addition mod 6 are another group, \(\mathbb{Z}_6\). A theorem proved once applies to all of them.

Lagrange's theorem. If a group has \(n\) elements and a subgroup (a subset that is itself a group) has \(m\) elements, then \(m\) divides \(n\).

Watch one: why Lagrange is true

Take the subgroup \(H = \{e, a\}\) inside \(D_3\). For each element \(g\), form the coset \(gH = \{g \circ e,\ g \circ a\}\) from the table:

\(eH = aH = \{e, a\}\), \(rH = cH = \{r, c\}\), \(r^2H = bH = \{r^2, b\}\).

Only three different cosets appear. They do not overlap, each has exactly 2 elements, and together they cover all 6. So \(6 = 3 \times 2\). Cosets always cut a group into pieces the size of the subgroup: that is the proof. One consequence: the order of an element \(g\), the smallest \(k\) with \(g^k = e\), is the size of the subgroup of its powers, so it divides the order of the group. In \(D_3\) the orders are 1, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2.

Your turn

  1. Use the table to compute \(r \circ b\), \(b \circ r\), and \((r \circ a)^2\). Find the inverse of every element.
  2. The symmetries of a non-square rectangle: list them and write the 4 × 4 Cayley table. Could you relabel its elements to make its table the table of \(\mathbb{Z}_4\) (addition mod 4)? Compare the orders of the elements before you decide.
  3. In \(\mathbb{Z}_{12}\) (addition mod 12), find the order of 8, of 9 and of 5. Then list every subgroup of \(\mathbb{Z}_{12}\), using Lagrange to limit the search.
  4. The numbers \(1, 2, \ldots, p - 1\), multiplied mod a prime \(p\), form a group of order \(p - 1\). Use "the order of an element divides the order of the group" to prove Fermat's little theorem, \(a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p\), in three lines. (Compare the Fermat sheet.)

Stretch: the rotations of a cube

The top face of a cube can land in 6 positions and then be spun 4 ways, so the cube has \(6 \times 4 = 24\) rotations. The rotations that keep one particular corner fixed form a subgroup. How many are there? How many cosets does Lagrange predict, and what do they mean geometrically?

Answers

  1. \(r \circ b = a\), \(b \circ r = c\) (row \(b\), column \(r\)). \((r \circ a)^2 = c \circ c = e\). Inverses: \(e^{-1} = e\), \(r^{-1} = r^2\), \((r^2)^{-1} = r\), and \(a, b, c\) are each their own inverse.
  2. Four symmetries: \(e\), the half-turn \(h\), the horizontal flip \(x\), the vertical flip \(y\). Every element squared is \(e\), and the product of any two different non-identity elements is the third. It is not \(\mathbb{Z}_4\): there the element 1 has order 4, while here every element has order 1 or 2. This is the Klein four-group.
  3. Order of 8: \(8, 4, 0\), so 3. Order of 9: \(9, 6, 3, 0\), so 4. Order of 5: 12. Subgroup sizes divide 12, and there is one of each size: \(\{0\}\), \(\{0, 6\}\), \(\{0, 4, 8\}\), \(\{0, 3, 6, 9\}\), \(\{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}\), \(\mathbb{Z}_{12}\). Six subgroups.
  4. Let \(k\) be the order of \(a\). By Lagrange, \(k\) divides \(p - 1\), say \(p - 1 = km\). Then \(a^{p-1} = (a^k)^m = 1^m = 1\). Fermat's theorem is Lagrange's theorem applied to one group.

Stretch. The rotations fixing a corner are the identity and the rotations by 120° and 240° about the long diagonal through it: a subgroup of order 3. Lagrange gives \(24/3 = 8\) cosets, one for each of the 8 corners the chosen corner can be sent to. Each coset is "all the rotations that send our corner there".
