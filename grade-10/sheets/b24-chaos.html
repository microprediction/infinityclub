The logistic map and chaos

x → r x (1 − x). Steady, then periodic, then chaos, and a constant that shows up everywhere.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A model of a population. Let \(x_n\) be this year's population as a fraction of the most the habitat can hold, so \(0 \le x_n \le 1\). Next year's is

\(x_{n+1} = r\,x_n\,(1 - x_n)\). The \(r x_n\) part is growth; the \((1 - x_n)\) part is crowding.

Fixed points. A population that stays put satisfies \(x = r x (1 - x)\): either \(x = 0\) or \(x^* = 1 - \tfrac1r\).

Stable or not? Near \(x^*\) the parabola \(f(x) = rx(1-x)\) is nearly a straight line with some steepness \(m\). Start a small distance \(\varepsilon\) from \(x^*\) and one step later you are about \(m\varepsilon\) away, so errors shrink when \(|m| \lt 1\) and grow when \(|m| \gt 1\). The steepness at \(x\) is \(r(1 - 2x)\), and at \(x^*\) that is \(m = 2 - r\). So the fixed point is stable exactly when \(1 \lt r \lt 3\). Negative \(m\) means the population overshoots, landing alternately above and below \(x^*\).

Past 3: period doubling. At \(r = 3.2\) the fixed point is unstable and the population bounces forever between \(0.513\) and \(0.799\): a 2-cycle. At \(r = 3.5\) a 4-cycle takes over; then 8, 16, 32, faster and faster, piling up at \(r_\infty \approx 3.5699\). Beyond that, chaos: at \(r = 3.9\) the sequence never repeats, and two starts that differ by \(0.001\) are unrelated within ten steps.

The universal constant. Successive gaps between doublings shrink by a factor tending to Feigenbaum's \(\delta = 4.6692\ldots\), the same number for dripping taps, circuits and lasers. Play at infinityclub.net/demos/logistic.html.

Watch one: \(r = 3.2\) by hand, and the 2-cycle exactly

Start at \(x_0 = 0.2\): \(x_1 = 3.2 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.512\), \(x_2 = 0.7995\), \(x_3 = 0.5129\), \(x_4 = 0.7995\), \(\ldots\) A 2-cycle by the fourth step.

Exactly: the two values are fixed points of the double step \(f(f(x)) = x\). Dividing out the roots \(0\) and \(x^*\) leaves a quadratic, whose roots are

\[ x = \frac{(r + 1) \pm \sqrt{(r + 1)(r - 3)}}{2r}. \]

For \(r = 3.2\): \(\dfrac{4.2 \pm 0.9165}{6.4}\), giving \(0.7995\) and \(0.5130\). The square root shows the 2-cycle is born exactly at \(r = 3\), when the fixed point loses stability.

Your turn

  1. Take \(r = 2.5\) and \(x_0 = 0.2\). Compute \(x_1\) to \(x_5\) (three decimals). Where is the population heading? Check against \(1 - 1/r\).

Stretch: \(r = 4\) is just doubling

Write \(x = \sin^2\theta\). Show that \(4x(1 - x) = \sin^2(2\theta)\), so the map is simply \(\theta \to 2\theta\). Now take \(\theta = \pi/7\): doubling gives \(2\pi/7\), \(4\pi/7\), then \(8\pi/7\), which is \(\pi/7\) again under \(\sin^2\). Compute the three \(x\) values and check on a calculator that \(4x(1-x)\) really cycles through them.

Answers

  1. \(0.400, 0.600, 0.600, 0.600, 0.600\): it lands on \(0.6 = 1 - 1/2.5\) in two steps and stays.
  2. \(x^* = 0.6, 0.6875, 0.7143, 0.7436\); \(m = -0.5, -1.2, -1.5, -1.9\). Only \(r = 2.5\) is stable. Negative \(m\) means each error flips sign: the sequence alternates above and below \(0.6\) while halving.
  3. From 0.2: \(0.303, 0.824, 0.566, 0.958, 0.156, 0.514\). From 0.201: \(0.311, 0.835, 0.537, 0.970, 0.115, 0.397\). The difference first exceeds 0.1 at step 8. A 0.5% error in the start becomes a 100% error within ten generations.
  4. Gaps \(0.4495\) and \(0.0946\); ratio \(4.75\), approaching \(4.669\) from above. \(r_4 \approx 3.5441 + 0.0946/4.669 = 3.5644\). ✓

Stretch. \(4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = (2\sin\theta\cos\theta)^2 = \sin^2 2\theta\). Values: \(\sin^2(\pi/7) = 0.1883\), \(\sin^2(2\pi/7) = 0.6113\), \(\sin^2(4\pi/7) = 0.9505\). Check: \(4(0.1883)(0.8117) = 0.6113\), then \(0.9505\), then back to \(0.1883\). A 3-cycle. Doubling an angle shifts its binary digits one place, so after 50 steps the 50th digit of the start is in charge: chaos is reading out the initial condition, one digit per step.
