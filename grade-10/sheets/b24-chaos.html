The logistic map and chaos

x → r x (1 − x). Steady, then periodic, then chaos, and a constant that shows up everywhere.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Here is a model of a population. Let \(x_n\) be this year's population as a fraction of the most the habitat could hold, so \(0 \le x_n \le 1\). Next year's is

\(x_{n+1} = r\,x_n\,(1 - x_n)\). The \(r x_n\) part is growth; the \((1 - x_n)\) part is crowding: the fuller the habitat, the harder next year is.

That is the whole rule. One multiplication, one subtraction. You can run it on a calculator, and tonight you will. What it does depends entirely on \(r\), and what it does is one of the great surprises of twentieth-century mathematics.

Fixed points. A population that stays put satisfies \(x = r x (1 - x)\). Either \(x = 0\) (extinct) or, dividing by \(x\), \(1 = r(1 - x)\), so \(x^* = 1 - \tfrac1r\). For \(r = 2.5\), \(x^* = 0.6\).

Stable or not? Being a fixed point is not enough; the question is whether nearby values are pulled in or pushed away. Picture the graph of \(f(x) = r x (1 - x)\), an upside-down parabola. Near \(x^*\) the graph is nearly a straight line with some steepness \(m\) (the slope of the curve at that point; sheet b10 makes this precise, but all you need tonight is "steepness"). If you start a small distance \(\varepsilon\) from \(x^*\), one step later you are about \(m\varepsilon\) away. So errors shrink when \(|m| \lt 1\) and grow when \(|m| \gt 1\). For the parabola the steepness at \(x\) is \(r(1 - 2x)\), and at \(x^* = 1 - 1/r\) that works out to

\[ m = r\left(1 - 2 + \tfrac{2}{r}\right) = 2 - r. \]

So the fixed point is stable exactly when \(|2 - r| \lt 1\), that is, \(1 \lt r \lt 3\). A negative \(m\) means the population overshoots, landing alternately above and below \(x^*\) while it closes in.

Past 3: period doubling. At \(r = 3.2\) the fixed point \(0.6875\) is there but unstable (\(m = -1.2\)). The population settles instead into a 2-cycle, bouncing forever between \(0.513\) and \(0.799\). At \(r = 3.5\) that 2-cycle has gone unstable too and a 4-cycle takes over: \(0.383, 0.827, 0.501, 0.875\). Then 8, 16, 32, each doubling arriving faster than the last: at \(r = 3, 3.4495, 3.5441, 3.5644, \ldots\), piling up at \(r_\infty \approx 3.5699\). Beyond that: chaos. At \(r = 3.9\) the sequence never repeats and looks random, though every number is produced by that one deterministic rule.

Sensitive dependence. Start at \(0.2\) and at \(0.201\) with \(r = 3.9\). For a few steps they stay close. By step 8 one is at \(0.514\) and the other at \(0.397\); soon after they are unrelated. A tiny error in the starting value grows exponentially until it swamps everything. This is why weather forecasts die after about ten days however good the computers get: the atmosphere obeys rules like this one. Edward Lorenz called it the butterfly effect.

The universal constant. Look at the gaps between successive doublings: \(3.4495 - 3 = 0.4495\), then \(0.0946\), then \(0.0203\). Each is about 4.67 times smaller than the last. Mitchell Feigenbaum showed in 1975 that the ratio tends to \(\delta = 4.6692\ldots\), and, astonishingly, that the same number appears for any hump-shaped map: dripping taps, electronic circuits, animal populations, lasers. A new constant of nature, found with a pocket calculator.

Watch one: r = 3.2 by hand, and the 2-cycle exactly

Start at \(x_0 = 0.2\). \(x_1 = 3.2 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.512\). \(x_2 = 3.2 \times 0.512 \times 0.488 = 0.7995\). \(x_3 = 3.2 \times 0.7995 \times 0.2005 = 0.5129\). \(x_4 = 0.7995\), \(x_5 = 0.5130\), \(x_6 = 0.7995\), \(\ldots\) Locked into a 2-cycle by the fourth step.

Can we find the two values exactly? They are fixed points of the double step, \(f(f(x)) = x\). That is a quartic, but it contains the ordinary fixed points \(0\) and \(x^*\) as roots, and dividing those out leaves a quadratic: \(r^2 x^2 - r(r + 1)x + (r + 1) = 0\), so

\[ x = \frac{(r + 1) \pm \sqrt{(r + 1)(r - 3)}}{2r}. \]

For \(r = 3.2\): \(\dfrac{4.2 \pm \sqrt{4.2 \times 0.2}}{6.4} = \dfrac{4.2 \pm 0.9165}{6.4}\), giving \(0.7995\) and \(0.5130\). Exactly what the calculator found.

Notice the square root: \((r + 1)(r - 3)\) is negative for \(r \lt 3\), so the 2-cycle does not exist until \(r = 3\). It is born at the precise moment the fixed point loses stability (\(m = 2 - r = -1\)). That is no coincidence, and it is the mechanism behind every doubling in the cascade.

Stretch: r = 4, and why chaos is just digit-shifting

At \(r = 4\) there is a trick. Write \(x = \sin^2\theta\). Then \(4x(1 - x) = 4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = (2\sin\theta\cos\theta)^2 = \sin^2(2\theta)\). So the complicated map on \(x\) is simply \(\theta \to 2\theta\) on the angle: doubling.

Now write \(\theta\) as a fraction of \(\pi\) in binary, say \(\theta = 0.b_1 b_2 b_3 \ldots \times \pi\). Doubling shifts every digit one place left and drops the first (angles repeat every \(\pi\) under \(\sin^2\)). After 50 steps, the 50th binary digit of your starting value is in charge. That is sensitive dependence made exact: chaos is reading out the digits of the initial condition, one per step.

Try \(\theta = \pi/7\): doubling gives \(2\pi/7\), \(4\pi/7\), \(8\pi/7 \equiv \pi/7\). A 3-cycle. Compute the three \(x\) values \(\sin^2(\pi/7), \sin^2(2\pi/7), \sin^2(4\pi/7)\) and check with the calculator that \(4x(1 - x)\) really cycles through them. Then find the 2-cycle hiding at \(\theta = \pi/5\). Finally: why does a rational multiple of \(\pi\) always give a repeating orbit, and an irrational one never? (A 1975 paper proved that any map with a 3-cycle has cycles of every length and chaotic orbits too. Its title: "Period three implies chaos".)

Stretch. \(\sin^2(\pi/7) = 0.1883\), \(\sin^2(2\pi/7) = 0.6113\), \(\sin^2(4\pi/7) = 0.9505\). Check: \(4(0.1883)(0.8117) = 0.6113\), then \(0.9505\), then back to \(0.1883\): a 3-cycle. At \(\theta = \pi/5\): \(\sin^2(\pi/5) = 0.3455 \to \sin^2(2\pi/5) = 0.9045 \to \sin^2(4\pi/5) = \sin^2(\pi/5) = 0.3455\): a 2-cycle. A rational \(\theta/\pi = p/q\) has only \(q\) possible values under doubling mod 1 (its binary expansion repeats), so the orbit must eventually repeat; an irrational angle has a non-repeating expansion, so the orbit never repeats. Almost every number is irrational (sheet b13), so almost every start is chaotic. The 1975 paper is by Li and Yorke.
