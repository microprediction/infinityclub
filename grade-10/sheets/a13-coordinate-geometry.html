Geometry with coordinates

Distance, midpoint, perpendicular slopes, the equation of a circle, and proving a shape is a rhombus by algebra.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Descartes' big idea was that a point is a pair of numbers. After that, every geometry statement becomes an algebra statement, and algebra can be checked without a ruler. Four tools do almost all the work.

Distance. From \((x_1, y_1)\) to \((x_2, y_2)\), go across \(x_2 - x_1\) and up \(y_2 - y_1\). Those are the legs of a right triangle, so the distance is the hypotenuse: \(d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\). Pythagoras, nothing more.

Midpoint. Average the coordinates: \(\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)\).

Slope. \(m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\), rise over run. Parallel lines have equal slopes. Perpendicular lines have slopes that multiply to \(-1\). Why: a line of slope \(m\) goes across 1 and up \(m\). Rotate that little triangle a quarter turn and it goes across \(-m\) and up 1, so the perpendicular slope is \(-\dfrac1m\).

Circle. The points at distance \(r\) from the centre \((h, k)\). Write that with the distance formula and square both sides:

\((x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2\)

Handed a circle in expanded form, complete the square in \(x\) and in \(y\) separately to read off the centre and radius. To prove a shape has a property: put coordinates on it, compute, conclude. Choose coordinates to make the arithmetic kind: a vertex at the origin, a side along an axis.

Watch one: is there a right angle at \(B\)?

\(A(1, 2)\), \(B(4, 6)\), \(C(8, 3)\). Slope of \(AB\): \(\frac{6 - 2}{4 - 1} = \frac43\). Slope of \(BC\): \(\frac{3 - 6}{8 - 4} = -\frac34\). Product: \(-1\). So \(AB \perp BC\): a right angle at \(B\). Check with lengths: \(AB = 5\), \(BC = 5\), and the hypotenuse should be \(5\sqrt2\). Indeed \(AC = \sqrt{7^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2\). Everything agrees, which is how you know you have not made a slip.

Your turn

  1. \(P(-3, 4)\) and \(Q(5, -2)\). Find the length \(PQ\), the midpoint \(M\), the slope of \(PQ\), and the equation of the perpendicular bisector of \(PQ\) in the form \(ax + by + c = 0\).
  2. The circle \(x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0\). Complete the square to find its centre and radius. Show that \((7, 1)\) lies on it, then find the tangent line at \((7, 1)\). (Hint: the tangent is perpendicular to the radius.)
  3. Find the point on the \(x\)-axis that is the same distance from \((2, 5)\) as from \((6, -1)\). Check your answer by computing both distances.
  4. Triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((6,0)\), \((0,8)\). Find the centre and radius of the circle through all three vertices, and write its equation. Which circle theorem told you where to look?

Stretch: a theorem for the price of a good coordinate choice

Prove that the diagonals of any parallelogram bisect each other. Place it as \(A(0,0)\), \(B(a, 0)\), \(D(b, c)\), work out where \(C\) must be, and compare the midpoints of \(AC\) and \(BD\).

Answers

  1. \(PQ = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10\). \(M = (1, 1)\). Slope \(= \frac{-6}{8} = -\frac34\). Perpendicular slope \(\frac43\), through \((1,1)\): \(y - 1 = \frac43(x - 1)\), so \(4x - 3y - 1 = 0\).
  2. \((x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) = 12 + 9 + 4\), so \((x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25\): centre \((3, -2)\), radius 5. At \((7, 1)\): \(4^2 + 3^2 = 25\). Yes. Radius slope \(\frac{1 - (-2)}{7 - 3} = \frac34\), so tangent slope \(-\frac43\): \(y - 1 = -\frac43(x - 7)\), or \(4x + 3y = 31\).
  3. Call it \((x, 0)\). \((x - 2)^2 + 25 = (x - 6)^2 + 1\). Expand: \(x^2 - 4x + 29 = x^2 - 12x + 37\), so \(8x = 8\), \(x = 1\). The point is \((1, 0)\). Check: distances squared are \(1 + 25 = 26\) and \(25 + 1 = 26\).
  4. The right angle is at the origin, so the hypotenuse from \((6,0)\) to \((0,8)\) is a diameter (converse of Thales). Centre is its midpoint \((3, 4)\), radius half its length \(= 5\). Equation \((x - 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 25\). Check \((0,0)\): \(9 + 16 = 25\).

Stretch. \(C\) is reached from \(B\) by the same move that takes \(A\) to \(D\), so \(C = (a + b, c)\). Midpoint of \(AC\): \(\left(\frac{a + b}{2}, \frac c2\right)\). Midpoint of \(BD\): \(\left(\frac{a + b}{2}, \frac c2\right)\). Same point, so each diagonal passes through the other's midpoint: they bisect each other.
