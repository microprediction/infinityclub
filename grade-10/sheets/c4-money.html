Compound interest, mortgages and why the casino wins

e in your savings account, the mortgage formula, expected value, and the house edge made visible.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Three pieces of mathematics decide more of your financial life than anything else you will learn: a geometric series, a limit, and a weighted average. Tonight you meet all three in the places they actually live.

Compounding. Put \(P\) in an account paying annual rate \(r\), compounded \(n\) times a year. Each period multiplies the balance by \(1 + r/n\), so after \(t\) years \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\). Compound more often and you earn a little more, but not without limit: as \(n \to \infty\) the bracket tends to \(e^{rt}\), where \(e = 2.71828\ldots\) is the number from the sheet The number e. Continuous compounding gives \(A = Pe^{rt}\), and for ordinary rates the difference between monthly and continuous is a few parts in a thousand. A quick mental tool: money doubles in about \(72/\text{(rate in \%)}\) years, the rule of 72. You will check how good it is.

The mortgage formula. Borrow \(P\) at monthly rate \(r\) (annual rate divided by 12), repay a fixed \(M\) every month for \(n\) months. Each month the balance grows by the factor \((1 + r)\) and then drops by \(M\). After \(n\) months it must be zero:

\[P(1+r)^n - M\left[(1+r)^{n-1} + (1+r)^{n-2} + \cdots + 1\right] = 0.\]

The bracket is a geometric series with sum \(\dfrac{(1+r)^n - 1}{r}\). Solve for \(M\):

\(M = P \cdot \dfrac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n - 1}\). Every mortgage, car loan and student loan in the world is this one line.

Expected value. A bet pays amounts \(x_i\) with probabilities \(p_i\). Its expected value is \(E = \sum p_i x_i\), the average winnings per play over the long run. Casinos are built on bets with \(E\) slightly negative for you. A European roulette wheel has 37 pockets (0 to 36); betting on red wins on 18 of them and pays even money. American wheels add a 00, making 38 pockets, same payouts. Small changes in the denominator, large changes in the casino's profit.

The Benford demo is a reminder that financial numbers have structure that fraud forgets; tonight's structure is the kind the house never forgets.

Watch one: a $300,000 mortgage at 6% over 30 years

Monthly rate \(r = 0.06/12 = 0.005\), \(n = 360\) months. \((1.005)^{360} = 6.0226\). So

\[M = 300{,}000 \times \frac{0.005 \times 6.0226}{6.0226 - 1} = 300{,}000 \times \frac{0.030113}{5.0226} = \$1{,}798.65 \text{ per month.}\]

Total paid: \(360 \times 1798.65 = \$647{,}515\). Interest: \(\$347{,}515\), more than the house. That is not a scandal, it is \((1.005)^{360}\): six doublings' worth of compounding spread over thirty years.

And one spin of American roulette, $1 on a single number, paying 35 to 1: \(E = \frac{1}{38}(35) + \frac{37}{38}(-1) = \frac{35 - 37}{38} = -\frac{2}{38} = -5.26\%\). On a European wheel: \(\frac{35 - 36}{37} = -\frac{1}{37} = -2.70\%\). Every bet on the table has the same edge (bar one American exception that is worse). You can win tonight; you cannot win on average.

Your turn

  1. $1,000 at 5% for 10 years. Find the balance with yearly, monthly and continuous compounding (\(e^{0.5}\)). How much does "compounding forever" add over monthly?
  2. Rule of 72: at 6% it predicts doubling in 12 years; at 9%, in 8. The exact doubling time is \(\ln 2 / \ln(1 + r)\). Compute both exact times. Is the rule good to a month?
  3. $200,000 at 4.5% annual. Find the monthly payment and total interest over 15 years (\(n = 180\)) and over 30 years (\(n = 360\)). Comment on the trade.
  4. A $2 lottery ticket wins $5,000,000 with probability 1 in 10,000,000 and $5 with probability 1 in 100. Find the expected value of the ticket and the expected loss per ticket. If 50 million tickets are sold, how much does the lottery keep on average?
  5. Find the expected value of a $10 bet on red on a European wheel (18 red, 18 black, 1 green zero; even money), and of a $10 bet on a single number on an American wheel (35 to 1, 38 pockets). Which is the better bet for the player, and by how much per spin?
  6. The doubling system. Bet $1 on red. If you lose, bet $2, then $4, and so on, until you win, which puts you $1 ahead; then start again. With $255 you can afford eight losses in a row (\(1 + 2 + \cdots + 128 = 255\)). On a European wheel the chance of losing a single spin is 19/37. Find the probability of losing eight in a row, and the expected value of one complete round of the system (win $1, or lose $255). Why does the "sure thing" fail?

Stretch: a pension is a mortgage run backwards

You retire at 65 with a pot of money earning 4% a year and want to withdraw $40,000 at the end of every year for 25 years, leaving nothing. The pot is the present value of those withdrawals: each \(\$40{,}000\) due in \(k\) years is worth \(40{,}000/1.04^k\) today. Sum the geometric series to show the pot needed is

\[PV = 40{,}000 \times \frac{1 - 1.04^{-25}}{0.04},\]

and compute it. Then let the 25 become infinity: how much would let you withdraw $40,000 a year forever (a perpetuity)? Finally, show that the mortgage formula is this formula solved the other way round: the lender's "pot" is the loan, and your payments are the withdrawals.

Answers

  1. Yearly: \(1000 \times 1.05^{10} = \$1{,}628.89\). Monthly: \(1000 \times (1 + 0.05/12)^{120} = \$1{,}647.01\). Continuous: \(1000e^{0.5} = \$1{,}648.72\). Continuous adds only $1.71 over monthly.
  2. \(\ln 2/\ln 1.06 = 11.90\) years (rule: 12); \(\ln 2/\ln 1.09 = 8.04\) years (rule: 8). Off by about a month at 6% and half a month at 9%. The rule is good for rates between about 4% and 12%.
  3. \(r = 0.00375\). 15 years: \(M = \$1{,}529.99\), total interest \(\$75{,}398\). 30 years: \(M = \$1{,}013.37\), total interest \(\$164{,}813\). The 30-year loan costs $517 less per month and $89,000 more in total. Which is "better" depends on what else you could do with $517 a month, which is the real financial question.
  4. \(E = 5{,}000{,}000/10^7 + 5/100 = 0.50 + 0.05 = \$0.55\). Expected loss \(= 2 - 0.55 = \$1.45\) per ticket. 50 million tickets: \(\$72.5\) million kept on average.
  5. Red, European: \(10 \times \frac{18 - 19}{37} = -\$0.27\) per spin. Single number, American: \(10 \times \frac{35 - 37}{38} = -\$0.53\) per spin. Red on the European wheel loses about half as fast; it is the better of two bad bets.
  6. \((19/37)^8 = 0.00484\), about 1 in 207. Expected value per round: \(0.99516 \times 1 - 0.00484 \times 255 = -\$0.24\). Each spin still has the house edge; the doubling system only rearranges when the losses arrive, trading many small wins for a rare catastrophe. With a bigger bankroll the catastrophe is rarer and larger, and the expected value stays negative. Casinos also impose table limits, which cap the doubling.

Stretch. \(PV = \sum_{k=1}^{25} \frac{40000}{1.04^k} = 40000 \cdot \frac{1}{1.04} \cdot \frac{1 - 1.04^{-25}}{1 - 1/1.04} = 40000 \cdot \frac{1 - 1.04^{-25}}{0.04} = \$624{,}883\). As the term goes to infinity, \(1.04^{-n} \to 0\) and \(PV \to 40000/0.04 = \$1{,}000{,}000\): a million dollars at 4% pays $40,000 a year forever. In the mortgage, the bank pays out \(P\) today and receives \(M\) a month: \(P = M \cdot \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r}\), and multiplying top and bottom by \((1+r)^n\) gives exactly \(M = P \frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n - 1}\).
