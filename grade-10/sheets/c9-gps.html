How GPS knows where you are

Spheres intersecting, four satellites, clocks, and the relativity correction that keeps you on the road.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A GPS satellite does one thing: it broadcasts the time and its own position, over and over. Your phone receives the message and compares the broadcast time with its own clock. The delay, multiplied by the speed of light \(c = 299{,}792.458\) km/s, is the distance to the satellite. A signal from a satellite overhead at 20,200 km altitude takes about 67 milliseconds to arrive.

Knowing you are 20,176 km from one satellite puts you somewhere on a sphere of that radius centred on it. A second satellite gives a second sphere; two spheres meet in a circle. A third sphere cuts that circle in two points, and one of the two is usually thousands of kilometres out in space or moving at absurd speed, so it is thrown away. Three distances, one position. This is trilateration (not triangulation, which uses angles).

There is a catch, and it is the clock. The satellites carry atomic clocks accurate to nanoseconds. Your phone does not. If your clock is off by one microsecond, every distance is off by \(10^{-6} \times 299{,}792 \approx 0.3\) km, and your position is wrong by hundreds of metres. The fix is beautiful: treat your clock error \(t\) as a fourth unknown alongside \(x, y, z\), and use a fourth satellite. Four equations, four unknowns. Every GPS fix is also, as a by-product, a clock synchronised to the atomic standard, which is why financial exchanges and power grids time themselves off GPS.

And then relativity shows up, in a device in your pocket. The satellites orbit at 3.87 km/s, and special relativity says a moving clock runs slow, by about 7 microseconds a day. They also sit higher in Earth's gravity, where general relativity says clocks run fast, by about 46 microseconds a day. The net effect is that satellite clocks gain about 38 microseconds a day on ground clocks. Left uncorrected, at 0.3 km per microsecond, that is a position error growing by more than 11 km every day. The satellite clocks are deliberately built to tick slightly slow before launch so that, in orbit, they keep ground time.

Finally, once the phone knows two positions on the globe, how far apart are they? Not by Pythagoras: the Earth is round. For two points at latitudes \(\varphi_1, \varphi_2\) and longitudes \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\), the angle \(\sigma\) between them at the centre of the Earth satisfies

\[ \cos\sigma = \sin\varphi_1 \sin\varphi_2 + \cos\varphi_1 \cos\varphi_2 \cos(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2), \] and the great-circle distance is \(R\,\sigma\) with \(\sigma\) in radians and \(R \approx 6{,}371\) km. (Why great circles are the shortest routes was the subject of Why Greenland looks as big as Africa.)

Watch one: trilateration in two dimensions

Three radio towers at \(A = (0, 0)\), \(B = (10, 0)\) and \(C = (0, 10)\), in km. Your phone measures its distance to each: \(5\), \(\sqrt{65} \approx 8.06\) and \(\sqrt{45} \approx 6.71\). Where are you?

The three circles are \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\), \((x - 10)^2 + y^2 = 65\) and \(x^2 + (y - 10)^2 = 45\). Subtract the first from the second: \((x - 10)^2 - x^2 = 40\), so \(-20x + 100 = 40\) and \(x = 3\). Subtract the first from the third: \(-20y + 100 = 20\), so \(y = 4\). Check: \(3^2 + 4^2 = 25\). You are at \((3, 4)\).

Subtracting turned two quadratic equations into a linear one, because the \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) terms cancelled. That is the trick that makes the real three-dimensional problem solvable in a fraction of a second.

Your turn

  1. A signal arrives 67.3 ms after it was sent. How far away is the satellite? If your clock is wrong by 1 microsecond, by how much is that distance wrong? By 1 nanosecond? What timing accuracy do you need for a 3 m position fix?
  2. Towers at \(A = (0, 0)\), \(B = (8, 0)\), \(C = (0, 6)\). Your measured distances are 5, 5 and 5. Find your position by the subtraction trick. Then: if you only had towers \(A\) and \(B\), what two positions would be possible?
  3. The clock problem. Same towers, but your clock is slow, so every distance you measure is too long by the same unknown amount \(e\). You measure 6, 6 and 6. Find \(x\), \(y\) and \(e\). Then explain why, in three dimensions, you need four satellites rather than three.
  4. Satellite clocks gain 38.5 microseconds a day. Convert that to a distance error per day, and per hour. How many minutes until an uncorrected clock has accumulated a 1 microsecond error?
  5. Use the great-circle formula for London (51.5° N, 0.1° W) to New York (40.7° N, 74.0° W). Work in degrees, convert \(\sigma\) to radians at the end. Then do an easy check: two points on the same meridian 10° of latitude apart.
  6. GPS satellites orbit at radius 26,560 km (about 20,200 km above the surface) with a period of 11 h 58 min. Compute their speed in km/s. Why is the period half a day rather than a day?

Stretch: where the 7 microseconds comes from

Special relativity says a clock moving at speed \(v\) ticks slower by the factor \(\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\). For small \(x\), \(\sqrt{1 - x} \approx 1 - x/2\) (check it: square the right-hand side). So the fractional slowing is about \(\dfrac{v^2}{2c^2}\). Put in \(v = 3.874\) km/s and \(c = 299{,}792\) km/s, multiply by the 86,400 seconds in a day, and see whether you get the 7 microseconds.

The gravitational part is \(\dfrac{GM}{c^2}\left(\dfrac{1}{R_E} - \dfrac{1}{r}\right)\) with \(GM = 3.986 \times 10^{14}\) m³/s², \(R_E = 6.371 \times 10^6\) m and \(r = 2.656 \times 10^7\) m. Check that one too. Which effect wins, and what would happen to a satellite in a very low orbit, where \(r\) is barely bigger than \(R_E\) but \(v\) is larger?

Answers

  1. \(0.0673 \times 299{,}792 \approx 20{,}176\) km. One microsecond: \(0.30\) km (300 m). One nanosecond: 30 cm. For 3 m you need the time right to \(3 / (3 \times 10^8) = 10\) nanoseconds.
  2. Circles \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\), \((x-8)^2 + y^2 = 25\), \(x^2 + (y-6)^2 = 25\). First minus second: \(-16x + 64 = 0\), \(x = 4\). First minus third: \(-12y + 36 = 0\), \(y = 3\). Position \((4, 3)\); check \(16 + 9 = 25\). With only \(A\) and \(B\): \(x = 4\) and \(y^2 = 9\), so \((4, 3)\) or \((4, -3)\). The third circle picks one.
  3. True distance is \(6 - e\) to each tower. The subtractions are unchanged (the \((6-e)^2\) cancels), so \(x = 4\), \(y = 3\) again, and then \(4^2 + 3^2 = (6 - e)^2\) gives \(6 - e = 5\), \(e = 1\) (the other root, \(e = 11\), would make the distances negative). Three towers fixed three unknowns \((x, y, e)\). In three dimensions the unknowns are \((x, y, z, e)\), four of them, so four satellites are needed.
  4. \(38.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 299{,}792 \approx 11.5\) km per day, about 480 m per hour. \(1/38.5\) of a day is about 37 minutes.
  5. \(\cos\sigma = \sin 51.5° \sin 40.7° + \cos 51.5° \cos 40.7° \cos 73.9° = 0.7826 \times 0.6521 + 0.6225 \times 0.7581 \times 0.2773 \approx 0.5103 + 0.1309 = 0.6412\), so \(\sigma \approx 50.1° = 0.875\) rad and \(d \approx 6{,}371 \times 0.875 \approx 5{,}570\) km. Same meridian, 10° apart: \(\sigma = 10° = 0.1745\) rad, \(d \approx 1{,}112\) km, about 111 km per degree, as it should be.
  6. Period \(= 43{,}080\) s, circumference \(2\pi \times 26{,}560 \approx 166{,}880\) km, speed \(\approx 3.87\) km/s. Half a (sidereal) day means each satellite passes over the same ground track twice a day, which makes the constellation's coverage repeat predictably; it is a design choice, not a law.

Stretch. \((1 - x/2)^2 = 1 - x + x^2/4 \approx 1 - x\) for small \(x\). \(v^2/(2c^2) = (3.874/299{,}792)^2/2 \approx 8.35 \times 10^{-11}\); times 86,400 s gives \(7.2 \times 10^{-6}\) s, about 7 microseconds slow per day. Gravity: \(\dfrac{3.986 \times 10^{14}}{(2.998 \times 10^8)^2}\left(\dfrac{1}{6.371 \times 10^6} - \dfrac{1}{2.656 \times 10^7}\right) \approx 5.29 \times 10^{-10}\); times 86,400 gives \(45.7 \times 10^{-6}\) s, about 46 microseconds fast per day. Gravity wins by 38.5 microseconds. For a very low orbit the gravitational term shrinks toward zero (the clock is at nearly the same height as the ground) while \(v\) is larger, about 7.8 km/s, so the velocity term wins and the satellite clock runs slow overall. Astronauts on the space station age very slightly less than we do; GPS satellites age very slightly more.
