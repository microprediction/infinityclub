How GPS knows where you are

Spheres intersecting, four satellites, clocks, and the relativity correction that keeps you on the road.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A GPS satellite does one thing: it broadcasts the time and its own position, over and over. Your phone compares the broadcast time with its own clock. The delay, multiplied by the speed of light \(c = 299{,}792.458\) km/s, is the distance to the satellite. From 20,200 km up, the signal takes about 67 milliseconds.

Knowing you are 20,176 km from one satellite puts you on a sphere of that radius centred on it. Two spheres meet in a circle; a third cuts that circle in two points, one of which is out in space and thrown away. Three distances, one position. This is trilateration (not triangulation, which uses angles).

The catch is the clock. The satellites carry atomic clocks; your phone does not. If your clock is off by one microsecond, every distance is off by \(10^{-6} \times 299{,}792 \approx 0.3\) km. The fix: treat your clock error \(e\) as a fourth unknown alongside \(x, y, z\), and use a fourth satellite. Four equations, four unknowns. Every GPS fix is also a clock synchronised to the atomic standard.

Relativity matters here too. The satellites move at 3.87 km/s and sit higher in Earth's gravity, and each changes how fast their clocks tick:

effectper day
motion (special relativity)−7 µs, slow
weaker gravity (general relativity)+46 µs, fast
net+38 µs ≈ 11 km of position

So the satellite clocks are built to tick slightly slow before launch and keep ground time in orbit.

Watch one: trilateration in two dimensions

Three radio towers at \(A = (0, 0)\), \(B = (10, 0)\) and \(C = (0, 10)\), in km. Your phone measures its distance to each: \(5\), \(\sqrt{65} \approx 8.06\) and \(\sqrt{45} \approx 6.71\). Where are you?

The three circles are \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\), \((x - 10)^2 + y^2 = 65\) and \(x^2 + (y - 10)^2 = 45\). Subtract the first from the second: \(-20x + 100 = 40\), so \(x = 3\). Subtract the first from the third: \(-20y + 100 = 20\), so \(y = 4\). Check: \(3^2 + 4^2 = 25\). You are at \((3, 4)\).

Subtracting turned two quadratic equations into a linear one, because the \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) terms cancelled.

Your turn

  1. (a) A signal arrives 67.3 ms after it was sent. How far away is the satellite? (b) If your clock is wrong by 1 microsecond, by how much is that distance wrong? By 1 nanosecond? (c) What timing accuracy do you need for a 3 m position fix?
  2. Towers at \(A = (0, 0)\), \(B = (8, 0)\), \(C = (0, 6)\). Your measured distances are 5, 5 and 5. Find your position by the subtraction trick. Then: if you only had towers \(A\) and \(B\), what two positions would be possible?
  3. The clock problem. Same towers, but your clock is slow, so every distance you measure is too long by the same unknown amount \(e\). You measure 6, 6 and 6. Find \(x\), \(y\) and \(e\). Then explain why, in three dimensions, you need four satellites rather than three.
  4. Satellite clocks gain 38.5 microseconds a day. Convert that to a distance error per day, and per hour. How many minutes until an uncorrected clock has accumulated a 1 microsecond error?

Stretch: where the 7 microseconds comes from

A clock moving at speed \(v\) ticks slower by the factor \(\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\) (special relativity's time-dilation factor; take it on trust tonight). For small \(x\), \(\sqrt{1 - x} \approx 1 - x/2\) (check by squaring), so the fractional slowing is about \(\dfrac{v^2}{2c^2}\). Put in \(v = 3.874\) km/s and \(c = 299{,}792\) km/s, multiply by the 86,400 seconds in a day, and see whether you get 7 microseconds.

Answers

  1. (a) \(0.0673 \times 299{,}792 \approx 20{,}176\) km. (b) One microsecond: \(0.30\) km (300 m). One nanosecond: 30 cm. (c) For 3 m you need the time right to \(3 / (3 \times 10^8) = 10\) nanoseconds.
  2. Circles \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\), \((x-8)^2 + y^2 = 25\), \(x^2 + (y-6)^2 = 25\). First minus second: \(-16x + 64 = 0\), \(x = 4\). First minus third: \(-12y + 36 = 0\), \(y = 3\). Position \((4, 3)\); check \(16 + 9 = 25\). With only \(A\) and \(B\): \(x = 4\) and \(y^2 = 9\), so \((4, 3)\) or \((4, -3)\). The third circle picks one.
  3. True distance is \(6 - e\) to each tower. The subtractions are unchanged (the \((6-e)^2\) cancels), so \(x = 4\), \(y = 3\) again, and then \(4^2 + 3^2 = (6 - e)^2\) gives \(6 - e = 5\), \(e = 1\) (the other root, \(e = 11\), would make the distances negative). Three towers fixed three unknowns \((x, y, e)\). In three dimensions the unknowns are \((x, y, z, e)\), four of them, so four satellites are needed.
  4. \(38.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 299{,}792 \approx 11.5\) km per day, about 480 m per hour. \(1/38.5\) of a day is about 37 minutes.

Stretch. \((1 - x/2)^2 = 1 - x + x^2/4 \approx 1 - x\) for small \(x\). \(v^2/(2c^2) = (3.874/299{,}792)^2/2 \approx 8.35 \times 10^{-11}\); times 86,400 s gives \(7.2 \times 10^{-6}\) s, about 7 microseconds slow per day. The gravitational 46 microseconds needs general relativity, and wins; a satellite in a very low orbit moves faster and sits barely higher, so there the velocity term wins and the clock runs slow overall. Astronauts on the space station age very slightly less than we do; GPS satellites age very slightly more.
