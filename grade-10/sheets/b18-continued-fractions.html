Continued fractions and the golden ratio

The best fractions for π and √2, why 355/113 is so good, and why φ is the hardest number to approximate.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Decimals are one way to write a number as a list of digits. There is an older, stranger way that turns out to be better at one thing: finding the best fractions for a number. Take \(415/93\). It is \(4\) and a bit: \(415 = 4 \times 93 + 43\), so \(415/93 = 4 + 43/93\). Now flip the leftover: \(43/93 = 1/(93/43)\), and \(93/43 = 2 + 7/43\). Flip again: \(43/7 = 6 + 1/7\). Flip once more: \(7/1 = 7\). Done:

\[\frac{415}{93} = 4 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \cfrac{1}{6 + \cfrac{1}{7}}}.\]

That is a continued fraction, written for short as \([4; 2, 6, 7]\). The steps are exactly Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common divisor, which is why it always finishes for a fraction. For an irrational number it never finishes, and the pattern of terms is often far more revealing than the decimal digits.

\(\sqrt2 = [1; 2, 2, 2, 2, \ldots]\), \(\qquad \varphi = \dfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} = [1; 1, 1, 1, 1, \ldots]\), \(\qquad \pi = [3; 7, 15, 1, 292, 1, 1, 1, \ldots]\).

Stop a continued fraction early and tidy it into an ordinary fraction. These stopping points are the convergents, and they have a remarkable property: each convergent \(p/q\) is closer to the number than any fraction with a smaller denominator. They are the champion approximations. For \(\pi\) the convergents are \(3\), \(\tfrac{22}{7}\), \(\tfrac{333}{106}\), \(\tfrac{355}{113}\), and every one of them is a famous approximation for a reason.

How good is a convergent? If the next term in the continued fraction is \(a\), the error is less than \(1/(a q^2)\). So a big term coming up means the convergent just before it is unusually good. In \(\pi\) the term after \(355/113\) is \(292\), huge, which is why \(355/113 = 3.14159292\ldots\) matches \(\pi\) to six decimal places with a three-digit denominator. The Chinese astronomer Zu Chongzhi found it in the fifth century.

And the opposite. The golden ratio's terms are all 1s, the smallest possible, so its convergents are the worst that convergents can be. Those convergents are \(\tfrac11, \tfrac21, \tfrac32, \tfrac53, \tfrac85, \tfrac{13}{8}, \ldots\), ratios of Fibonacci numbers. In a precise sense \(\varphi\) is the most irrational number: the one that fractions approach most reluctantly. That is why sunflowers use it (see the grade 6 Fibonacci sheet): seeds placed by a golden-angle turn never line up into spokes, because no fraction gets close enough to make them.

Watch one: the continued fraction of \(\sqrt2\)

\(\sqrt2 = 1.414\ldots\), so the first term is \(1\) and the leftover is \(\sqrt2 - 1\). Flip it. The trick is to multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt2 + 1\):

\[\frac{1}{\sqrt2 - 1} = \frac{\sqrt2 + 1}{(\sqrt2 - 1)(\sqrt2 + 1)} = \frac{\sqrt2 + 1}{2 - 1} = \sqrt2 + 1 = 2.414\ldots\]

So the next term is \(2\), and the leftover is \(\sqrt2 + 1 - 2 = \sqrt2 - 1\): the same leftover as before. Flipping it gives \(2.414\ldots\) again, and again, forever. \(\sqrt2 = [1; 2, 2, 2, \ldots]\).

Now the convergents. \([1] = 1\). \([1; 2] = 1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac32\). \([1; 2, 2] = 1 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \frac12} = 1 + \tfrac25 = \tfrac75\). \([1; 2, 2, 2] = 1 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \frac25} = 1 + \tfrac{5}{12} = \tfrac{17}{12}\). There is a faster way: each convergent's top and bottom are (term) × (previous) + (one before that). With terms \(2, 2, 2, \ldots\): tops \(1, 3, 7, 17, 41, 99\); bottoms \(1, 2, 5, 12, 29, 70\). And \(\tfrac{99}{70} = 1.4142857\), against \(\sqrt2 = 1.4142136\): correct to four decimals with a two-digit denominator.

Notice the errors alternate: \(1 \lt \sqrt2\), \(\tfrac32 > \sqrt2\), \(\tfrac75 \lt \sqrt2\), \(\tfrac{17}{12} > \sqrt2\). Convergents always close in from both sides.

Your turn

  1. Find the continued fraction of \(649/200\) and write out its convergents. (You should get four terms; the last convergent is the fraction itself.)
  2. Use the "term × previous + one before" rule with the terms of \(\pi\), \([3; 7, 15, 1, 292]\), to produce the convergents \(3, \tfrac{22}{7}, \tfrac{333}{106}, \tfrac{355}{113}\), and the one after. Then compute how far each of the first four is from \(\pi = 3.14159265\ldots\) and say which error is the surprise.
  3. Run the \(\sqrt2\) trick on \(\sqrt3\): start with \(1\), leftover \(\sqrt3 - 1\), and rationalise each flip. You should find the terms repeat with period 2. Write down the first six convergents and check that \(\tfrac{97}{56}\) is within \(0.0001\) of \(\sqrt3 = 1.7320508\).
  4. The convergents of \(\varphi\) are ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Compute \(\tfrac{13}{8}\), \(\tfrac{21}{13}\) and \(\tfrac{34}{21}\) as decimals, and the error of each against \(\varphi = 1.6180340\). Compare with the errors for \(\sqrt2\)'s convergents \(\tfrac{17}{12}\) and \(\tfrac{41}{29}\), whose denominators are similar in size. Which number is harder to approximate?
  5. Prove that \([1; 1, 1, 1, \ldots] = \varphi\). Call the whole thing \(x\). Explain why \(x = 1 + \tfrac1x\), turn that into a quadratic, and solve it. Then do the same for \([2; 2, 2, 2, \ldots]\) and \([1; 2, 2, 2, \ldots]\) and check it agrees with the worked example.
  6. A year is \(365.2422\) days. Write \(0.2422\) as a continued fraction (four terms is plenty) and find its convergents. Which convergent is the Julian calendar's rule of one leap day every 4 years? Which one would be a better rule, and what does it suggest about leap years every 33 years?

Stretch: why convergents are champions

Here is the key inequality, and it is worth proving once. Consecutive convergents \(\tfrac{p_n}{q_n}\) and \(\tfrac{p_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\) satisfy \(p_{n+1}q_n - p_nq_{n+1} = \pm 1\). Check it on \(\tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{7}{5}\) and on \(\tfrac{22}{7}, \tfrac{333}{106}\). Now, the number \(x\) lies between consecutive convergents, so

\[\left|x - \frac{p_n}{q_n}\right| \lt \left|\frac{p_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}} - \frac{p_n}{q_n}\right| = \frac{1}{q_n q_{n+1}} \lt \frac{1}{q_n^2}.\]

Every convergent is within \(1/q^2\) of the target. A random fraction with denominator \(q\) is only guaranteed to be within \(1/(2q)\). For \(q = 113\) that is the difference between an error of \(0.0044\) and \(0.00008\). Now use the recurrence \(q_{n+1} = a_{n+1}q_n + q_{n-1}\) to explain why a big next term \(a_{n+1}\) makes the bound even better, and why all-ones terms (the golden ratio) make it as weak as it can be.

Answers

  1. \(649 = 3 \times 200 + 49\); \(200 = 4 \times 49 + 4\); \(49 = 12 \times 4 + 1\); \(4 = 4 \times 1\). So \(649/200 = [3; 4, 12, 4]\). Convergents \(\tfrac31, \tfrac{13}{4}, \tfrac{159}{49}, \tfrac{649}{200}\).
  2. Tops: \(3, 22, 15 \times 22 + 3 = 333, 333 + 22 = 355, 292 \times 355 + 333 = 103993\). Bottoms: \(1, 7, 106, 113, 33102\). Errors: \(3\) is off by \(0.1416\); \(\tfrac{22}{7} = 3.142857\), off by \(0.00126\); \(\tfrac{333}{106} = 3.141509\), off by \(0.000083\); \(\tfrac{355}{113} = 3.14159292\), off by \(0.00000027\). The surprise is \(355/113\): a jump of 300 times in accuracy for a slightly bigger denominator, because the next term is 292.
  3. \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3 - 1} = \tfrac{\sqrt3 + 1}{2} = 1.366\ldots\), term \(1\), leftover \(\tfrac{\sqrt3 - 1}{2}\). Flip: \(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3 - 1} = \sqrt3 + 1 = 2.732\ldots\), term \(2\), leftover \(\sqrt3 - 1\), back to the start. \(\sqrt3 = [1; 1, 2, 1, 2, \ldots]\). Convergents \(\tfrac11, \tfrac21, \tfrac53, \tfrac74, \tfrac{19}{11}, \tfrac{26}{15}, \tfrac{71}{41}, \tfrac{97}{56}\); \(\tfrac{97}{56} = 1.732143\), within \(0.0001\) of \(1.7320508\).
  4. \(\tfrac{13}{8} = 1.625\) (error \(0.0070\)), \(\tfrac{21}{13} = 1.61538\) (error \(0.0026\)), \(\tfrac{34}{21} = 1.61905\) (error \(0.0010\)). For \(\sqrt2\): \(\tfrac{17}{12} = 1.41667\) (error \(0.0025\)) and \(\tfrac{41}{29} = 1.41379\) (error \(0.00042\)). Raw errors are not a fair comparison because the denominators differ, so multiply each error by \(q^2\): for \(\varphi\) you get \(0.446, 0.448, 0.447\), for \(\sqrt2\) you get \(0.353, 0.354\). The \(\sqrt2\) convergents are consistently closer for their size, and the golden ratio's figure \(0.447 \approx 1/\sqrt5\) is the worst any number can have (that is Hurwitz's theorem). The golden ratio is harder, and it is the hardest of all.
  5. Everything after the first \(1 +\) is the whole fraction again, so \(x = 1 + 1/x\), \(x^2 - x - 1 = 0\), \(x = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2}\) (the positive root). For \([2; 2, 2, \ldots]\): \(y = 2 + 1/y\), \(y^2 - 2y - 1 = 0\), \(y = 1 + \sqrt2\). Then \([1; 2, 2, \ldots] = 1 + 1/y = 1 + \tfrac{1}{1 + \sqrt2} = 1 + (\sqrt2 - 1) = \sqrt2\), as the worked example found.
  6. \(0.2422 = \tfrac{2422}{10000} = \tfrac{1211}{5000}\). \(5000 = 4 \times 1211 + 156\); \(1211 = 7 \times 156 + 119\); \(156 = 1 \times 119 + 37\); \(119 = 3 \times 37 + 8\). So \(0.2422 = [0; 4, 7, 1, 3, \ldots]\), convergents \(\tfrac14, \tfrac{7}{29}, \tfrac{8}{33}, \tfrac{31}{128}\). \(\tfrac14\) is the Julian rule, one leap day in 4 years (too many: it drifts a day every 128 years). \(\tfrac{8}{33}\) says 8 leap years in every 33, which is more accurate than our Gregorian 97 in 400 and was proposed by Omar Khayyam's calendar committee in 1079.

Stretch. \(7 \times 2 - 3 \times 5 = -1\); \(333 \times 7 - 22 \times 106 = 2331 - 2332 = -1\). The identity holds in general because each step of the recurrence changes the determinant \(p_{n+1}q_n - p_nq_{n+1}\) only by a sign. Since \(q_{n+1} = a_{n+1}q_n + q_{n-1} > a_{n+1}q_n\), the error is less than \(\tfrac{1}{q_n q_{n+1}} \lt \tfrac{1}{a_{n+1}q_n^2}\): a big next term shrinks the error by that factor, which is the 292 effect for \(\pi\). With every \(a = 1\) the denominators grow as slowly as the recurrence allows (Fibonacci numbers, each only about \(1.618\) times the last), so \(q_n q_{n+1}\) is as small as it can be and the convergents are as weak as convergents ever are. That is the precise meaning of "most irrational".
