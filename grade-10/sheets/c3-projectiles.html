Parabolas in flight

Why a thrown ball traces a parabola, the 45° myth, and the pendulum's √(L/g).

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

You have factored a hundred quadratics this year. Here is where one of them lives. Throw a ball with speed \(v\) at angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal. Gravity pulls down with constant acceleration \(g \approx 9.81\) m/s², and nothing pulls sideways (we ignore air for now). Split the motion into two independent problems.

Sideways: no acceleration, so constant speed \(v\cos\theta\). After time \(t\), \(x = v\cos\theta \cdot t\).

Up and down: starting speed \(v\sin\theta\), acceleration \(-g\). The standard constant-acceleration result \(s = ut + \tfrac12 a t^2\) gives \(y = v\sin\theta \cdot t - \tfrac12 g t^2\).

Time is the thing we cannot see. Eliminate it: from the first equation \(t = x/(v\cos\theta)\). Substitute into the second:

\(y = x\tan\theta - \dfrac{g}{2v^2\cos^2\theta}\,x^2.\) A quadratic in \(x\) with a negative leading coefficient: an upside-down parabola. Every thrown, kicked, struck or fired object follows one, to the extent air can be ignored.

Where does it land, if the ground is flat and level with the launch? Set \(y = 0\): either \(x = 0\) (the start) or \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{g x}{2v^2\cos^2\theta}\), giving \(x = \dfrac{2v^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g}\). With the identity \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta\):

Range \(R = \dfrac{v^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}\). Maximum when \(\sin 2\theta = 1\), that is \(\theta = 45°\). And since \(\sin 2\theta = \sin(180° - 2\theta)\), the angles \(\theta\) and \(90° - \theta\) give the same range.

So why does no shot-putter throw at 45°, and no long-jumper take off at 45°? Because the formula's assumptions fail in instructive ways. A shot leaves the hand two metres up, so the ball has extra time to travel and a flatter throw pays: the optimum drops to about 42°. A long-jumper cannot convert sprinting speed into vertical speed without losing most of it, so the best real take-off is around 20°. And air drag, which grows with speed, punishes high slow arcs more than low fast ones. The pure parabola is the first approximation; the corrections are where the engineering lives.

Watch one: 20 m/s at 30°

Range: \(R = \dfrac{20^2 \sin 60°}{9.81} = \dfrac{400 \times 0.866}{9.81} = 35.3\) m.

Maximum height: the vertical speed \(v\sin\theta = 10\) m/s is used up when \(v_y^2 = u_y^2 - 2gh\) hits zero, so \(h = \dfrac{(v\sin\theta)^2}{2g} = \dfrac{100}{19.62} = 5.10\) m.

Time of flight: up and down symmetric, \(t = \dfrac{2v\sin\theta}{g} = \dfrac{20}{9.81} = 2.04\) s. Check: \(x = v\cos\theta \cdot t = 17.32 \times 2.04 = 35.3\) m. Same answer two ways, which is how you catch mistakes.

And the pendulum, the other piece of school physics with a square root in it: a small swing of a pendulum of length \(L\) has period \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\), independent of how heavy the bob is and, for small swings, of how wide it swings. For \(L = 1\) m: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{1/9.81} = 2.006\) s. Galileo noticed the independence from amplitude by timing a swinging lamp against his pulse, and that observation became the pendulum clock.

Your turn

  1. A football is kicked at 25 m/s at 40° above the horizontal. Find the range, the maximum height and the time of flight. Take \(g = 9.81\) m/s².
  2. The same ball is kicked at 25 m/s at 50°. Without recalculating, state the range and explain why. Which kick stays in the air longer, and by how much?
  3. A hose held 1.2 m above the ground squirts water horizontally at 8 m/s. How long does the water take to reach the ground, and how far away does it land? (Horizontal launch means \(\theta = 0\): the vertical motion is a pure drop.)
  4. A ball is launched at 45° from ground level and must clear a 3 m wall that is 20 m away. Use the trajectory equation (at 45°, \(\tan\theta = 1\) and \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12\)) to find the minimum launch speed.
  5. On the Moon, \(g = 1.62\) m/s². How far would the kick in question 1 travel there? Why does a lower \(g\) help the range exactly in inverse proportion?
  6. Pendulums. What length gives a period of exactly 1 second? A grandfather clock "ticks" every second, which is a half-swing, so its period is 2 s: what length is its pendulum? Explain why pendulum clocks had to be re-adjusted when shipped from Paris to the equator, where \(g\) is about 0.5% smaller.

Stretch: the real shot put

Launch from height \(h\) above the landing level. The landing point satisfies \(0 = h + x\tan\theta - \dfrac{g x^2}{2v^2\cos^2\theta}\), a quadratic in \(x\) whose positive root is

\[R = \frac{v\cos\theta}{g}\left(v\sin\theta + \sqrt{v^2\sin^2\theta + 2gh}\right).\]

Check that \(h = 0\) gives back \(v^2\sin 2\theta/g\). It can be shown (calculus, next year) that the best angle satisfies \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{v}{\sqrt{v^2 + 2gh}}\), which is below 45° whenever \(h > 0\). A good shot-putter releases at about \(v = 14\) m/s from \(h = 2.1\) m. Find the optimal angle, the range at that angle, the range at 45° from the same height, and the range the flat-ground formula would predict. How much does the release height buy, and how much does the angle correction add on top?

Answers

  1. \(R = 625 \sin 80° / 9.81 = 62.7\) m. Height \(= (25\sin 40°)^2/(2 \times 9.81) = 13.2\) m. Time \(= 2 \times 25 \sin 40° / 9.81 = 3.28\) s.
  2. Same range, 62.7 m, because \(\sin 100° = \sin 80°\): complementary angles share a range. The 50° kick is in the air longer: \(2 \times 25\sin 50°/9.81 = 3.90\) s against 3.28 s, about 0.63 s more, because more of its speed is vertical.
  3. Drop time from \(1.2 = \tfrac12 g t^2\): \(t = \sqrt{2.4/9.81} = 0.495\) s. Distance \(= 8 \times 0.495 = 3.96\) m.
  4. At 45° the trajectory is \(y = x - \dfrac{g x^2}{v^2}\). Require \(y \ge 3\) at \(x = 20\): \(3 = 20 - \dfrac{9.81 \times 400}{v^2}\), so \(v^2 = 3924/17 = 230.8\) and \(v = 15.2\) m/s.
  5. \(R = 62.7 \times 9.81/1.62 = 380\) m. \(R = v^2\sin 2\theta/g\) has \(g\) in the denominator and nowhere else, so dividing \(g\) by 6.06 multiplies \(R\) by 6.06.
  6. From \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\), \(L = gT^2/(4\pi^2)\). \(T = 1\) s: \(L = 0.248\) m. \(T = 2\) s: \(L = 0.994\) m, almost exactly a metre (this is not a coincidence: the metre was once proposed to be defined this way). Smaller \(g\) means a longer period, so the clock runs slow near the equator; the pendulum must be shortened by about 0.5% to compensate. This is how Jean Richer discovered in 1672 that the Earth is not a perfect sphere.

Stretch. With \(h = 0\) the square root is \(v\sin\theta\), so \(R = \frac{v\cos\theta}{g} \cdot 2v\sin\theta = v^2 \sin 2\theta/g\). For \(v = 14\), \(h = 2.1\): \(\tan\theta = 14/\sqrt{196 + 41.2} = 0.909\), \(\theta = 42.3°\). Range at 42.3°: 21.98 m. Range at 45° from 2.1 m: 21.90 m. Flat-ground formula: \(196/9.81 = 19.98\) m. The release height buys about 1.9 m; the angle correction adds only another 8 cm. Real athletes release even lower, around 37–40°, because they can generate more speed at flatter angles, and speed enters squared.
