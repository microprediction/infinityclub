Kepler's laws and measuring the universe

T² ∝ a³, parallax, standard candles, and a cosmic distance ladder built out of logarithms.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Nobody has ever laid a tape measure to a star. Every distance in astronomy is an inference, and the inferences stack: each rung of the ladder is calibrated by the one below.

Rung 1: the Solar System, with Kepler. In 1619 Kepler found that for every planet the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbit's size. In years and astronomical units (1 AU is the Earth–Sun distance) the constant is 1:

\(T^2 = a^3\). Mars: \(a = 1.524\) AU, \(T = 1.881\) yr. \(1.524^3 = 3.540\), \(1.881^2 = 3.538\).

Kepler had no idea why; Newton showed it is the inverse-square law in disguise (tonight's stretch). The law turns time into distance, and radar off Venus pins down the AU itself: 149.6 million km.

Rung 2: nearby stars, with parallax. Hold a finger at arm's length and blink one eye then the other: it jumps. The Earth does the same six months apart. Half a nearby star's total shift is its parallax \(p\); a star with \(p = 1\) arcsecond is 1 parsec away, 3.26 light-years, and in general \(d = 1/p\) parsecs. Proxima Centauri has \(p = 0.768''\), so \(d = 1.30\) pc \(= 4.25\) light-years.

Rung 3: everything else, with standard candles. A source of luminosity \(L\) at distance \(d\) has brightness \(b = L/(4\pi d^2)\). Know \(L\), because the object is a type calibrated on nearby examples by parallax, and \(b\) gives \(d\). Cepheid variables and type Ia supernovae are the famous candles.

Watch one: Jupiter, Ceres, and a twin star

Jupiter: \(a = 5.203\) AU, \(T = 11.86\) yr. \(5.203^3 = 140.85\) and \(11.86^2 = 140.66\). Ceres orbits at \(a = 2.77\) AU, so predict \(T = a^{3/2} = 2.77^{1.5} = 4.61\) yr; observed, 4.60 yr. This is how new asteroids get orbits after a fraction of a lap.

Now a distance from a brightness. Two stars are identical twins. Star A is 10 pc away by parallax; star B appears 25 times fainter. Brightness falls as \(1/d^2\), so B is \(\sqrt{25} = 5\) times farther: 50 pc. No parallax needed.

Your turn

  1. Check Kepler's third law for Venus (\(a = 0.723\) AU, \(T = 0.615\) yr) and Saturn (\(a = 9.54\) AU, \(T = 29.46\) yr). Give \(a^3\) and \(T^2\) to four significant figures.
  2. An asteroid orbits the Sun every 8 years. How far from the Sun is it, on average? Halley's comet has period 75.3 years and comes within 0.586 AU of the Sun. Find its semi-major axis, then its farthest distance (the two extremes add to \(2a\)). Which planet does that take it beyond?
  3. A star has parallax \(0.1''\). How far away is it in parsecs and in light-years? Barnard's Star has \(p = 0.547''\): its distance?
  4. A type Ia supernova at 20 million light-years calibrates the candle. Another is seen 10,000 times fainter. How far away is it? And one seen a million times fainter?

Stretch: where Kepler's law comes from, and weighing the Sun

For a circular orbit gravity is the centripetal force: \(\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\), with \(v = 2\pi r/T\). Show that \(T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM} r^3\). Then turn it round and weigh the Sun from Earth's orbit: \(r = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\) m, \(T = 3.156 \times 10^{7}\) s, \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\) SI.

Answers

  1. Venus: \(a^3 = 0.3779\), \(T^2 = 0.3782\). Saturn: \(a^3 = 868.3\), \(T^2 = 867.9\). Both agree to better than one part in a thousand.
  2. \(a = T^{2/3} = 8^{2/3} = 4\) AU exactly. Halley: \(a = 75.3^{2/3} = 17.83\) AU; farthest distance \(2 \times 17.83 - 0.586 = 35.1\) AU, beyond Neptune (30.1 AU).
  3. \(p = 0.1''\): 10 pc \(= 32.6\) ly. Barnard's Star: \(1/0.547 = 1.83\) pc \(= 5.96\) ly.
  4. 10,000 times fainter means \(\sqrt{10000} = 100\) times farther: 2 billion light-years. A million times fainter: 1,000 times farther, 20 billion light-years.

Stretch. Cancel \(m\) and one \(r\): \(\frac{GM}{r} = v^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}\), so \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM} r^3\): \(T^2 \propto r^3\) with a constant depending only on the central mass. Then \(M = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 (1.496\times10^{11})^3}{6.674\times10^{-11} \times (3.156\times10^7)^2} = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\) kg. The same trick on Io's orbit weighs Jupiter, which is how planets with moons were first weighed.
