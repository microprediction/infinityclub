Imaginary numbers are real

There is no ordinary number whose square is −1. So mathematicians invented one, called it i, and it turned out to be the key to electricity, waves and quantum physics. Tonight you learn to calculate with it.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Five hundred years ago, negative numbers were called "absurd" and "fictitious". How can you have less than nothing? Then people found that if you simply allow them and follow the rules, debts, temperatures and directions all become easy. The numbers were strange only until they were useful.

The same thing happened again. Nothing squared gives a negative, because positive times positive and negative times negative are both positive. So define a new number \(i\) with the single property \(i^2 = -1\), and see what happens if you follow the ordinary rules of algebra.

Numbers like \(3 + 2i\) are called complex numbers: an ordinary part and an \(i\) part. Adding is easy, just add the parts: \((3 + 2i) + (1 + 5i) = 4 + 7i\). Multiplying uses the distributive law and \(i^2 = -1\) whenever it appears.

The picture is the real surprise. Plot \(a + bi\) as the point \((a, b)\): ordinary numbers along the horizontal axis, \(i\) one step up. Now multiply any point by \(i\) and plot the result. It has rotated a quarter turn anticlockwise. That explains \(i^2 = -1\) perfectly: two quarter turns make a half turn, and a half turn takes 1 to \(-1\). There was never anything imaginary about it. It was a rotation all along. Try it at infinityclub.net/demos/complex.

Watch one: multiply (2 + 3i)(1 − i)

Expand like any pair of brackets: \(2 \times 1 + 2 \times (-i) + 3i \times 1 + 3i \times (-i) = 2 - 2i + 3i - 3i^2\).

Now use \(i^2 = -1\): \(-3i^2 = +3\). So the total is \(2 + 3 + (-2i + 3i) = 5 + i\).

Your turn

  1. Work out \(i^1, i^2, i^3, i^4, i^5\). You should see a cycle. Use it to find \(i^7\) and \(i^{100}\).
  2. Compute \((2 + 3i) + (1 - i)\) and \((2 + 3i) - (1 - i)\).
  3. Compute \((1 + 2i)(3 + i)\). Show the \(i^2\) step.
  4. Compute \((3 + 2i)(3 - 2i)\). The \(i\) parts should vanish. Then compute \((a + bi)(a - bi)\) in general. This is why the number 13 can be split into two squares.
  5. On a grid, plot \(2 + i\). Multiply it by \(i\) and plot the result. Multiply by \(i\) again, and again, and again, plotting each time. Describe the shape your four points make and say why the fifth point is back where you started.
  6. Solve \(x^2 = -9\) and \(x^2 = -2\). (Each has two answers.)

Stretch: an equation with no ordinary solution

The equation \(x^2 - 2x + 5 = 0\) has no ordinary solution. Try a few values of \(x\) and notice the left side never reaches zero. Now rewrite it: \(x^2 - 2x + 5 = (x - 1)^2 + 4\). (Check that by expanding.) So the equation says \((x - 1)^2 = -4\). Finish it off with \(i\). Then check one of your answers by substituting it back in.

And a picture question: \(1 + i\) sits at 45° from the real axis, at distance \(\sqrt{2}\) from the centre. Compute \((1 + i)^2\). Where does it land, at what angle and what distance? Can you guess the rule for what squaring does to the angle and to the distance?

Answers

  1. \(i, -1, -i, 1, i\): a cycle of length 4. \(i^7 = i^3 = -i\) (since \(7 = 4 + 3\)). \(i^{100} = 1\) (100 is a multiple of 4).
  2. \(3 + 2i\) and \(1 + 4i\).
  3. \(3 + i + 6i + 2i^2 = 3 + 7i - 2 = 1 + 7i\).
  4. \(9 - 6i + 6i - 4i^2 = 9 + 4 = 13\). In general \((a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2\). So \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\).
  5. \(2 + i \to -1 + 2i \to -2 - i \to 1 - 2i \to 2 + i\). The four points are the corners of a square centred at 0, each a quarter turn from the last. Four quarter turns make a full turn, which is also the statement \(i^4 = 1\).
  6. \(x = 3i\) or \(x = -3i\) (check: \((3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9\)). \(x = i\sqrt{2}\) or \(x = -i\sqrt{2}\).

Stretch. \((x - 1)^2 = -4\) means \(x - 1 = 2i\) or \(x - 1 = -2i\), so \(x = 1 + 2i\) or \(x = 1 - 2i\). Check with \(x = 1 + 2i\): \(x^2 = 1 + 4i + 4i^2 = -3 + 4i\), then \(x^2 - 2x + 5 = (-3 + 4i) - (2 + 4i) + 5 = 0\). Picture: \((1 + i)^2 = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 2i\), which sits straight up the \(i\) axis at 90°, at distance 2. The angle doubled (45° to 90°) and the distance squared (\(\sqrt{2}\) to 2). That is the general rule: multiplying complex numbers adds their angles and multiplies their distances. It is the reason they are so good at describing anything that rotates or oscillates.
