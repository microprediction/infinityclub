How many ways: factorials and the birthday problem

Every time you shuffle a deck of cards properly, you almost certainly produce an order that has never existed before in the history of the universe. And in a class of 23, it is more likely than not that two people share a birthday. Both facts come from the same kind of counting.

Tonight's idea

Multiply the choices. Four shirts and three pairs of trousers make \(4 \times 3 = 12\) outfits, because each shirt goes with each pair. Add two pairs of shoes and it is \(4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24\). Whenever choices are made one after another and do not interfere, the number of combinations is the product.

Arrangements. In how many orders can five books stand on a shelf? Five choices for the first slot, then four left for the second, three, two, one: \(5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120\). This product has a name, five factorial, written \(5!\). In general \(n!\) is the number of ways to put \(n\) different things in a row.

Factorials grow absurdly fast. \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\). \(20!\) is about \(2 \times 10^{18}\). And \(52!\), the number of orders for a deck of cards, is about \(8 \times 10^{67}\). For comparison, the universe is about \(4 \times 10^{17}\) seconds old, and there are perhaps \(10^{50}\) atoms in the whole Earth. If every atom on Earth had been shuffling a deck once a second since the Big Bang, together they would have produced about \(4 \times 10^{67}\) orders, still fewer than \(52!\). So a well-shuffled deck is, with near certainty, an order nobody has ever held.

Choosing without order is different: picking 2 books from 5 to take on holiday is \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\), the Pascal's triangle number, because the order you pick them in does not matter. Arranging cares about order. Choosing does not.

Watch one: the birthday problem

How likely is it that in a group of 23 people, at least two share a birthday? Counting the ways it can happen is a nightmare. Counting the ways it cannot is easy, so do that and subtract from 1.

Line the people up. The first can have any birthday: \(\tfrac{365}{365}\). The second must avoid one day: \(\tfrac{364}{365}\). The third must avoid two: \(\tfrac{363}{365}\). Keep going to the 23rd person, who must avoid 22 days: \(\tfrac{343}{365}\). Multiply all 23 fractions together and you get \(0.493\). That is the chance of no shared birthday. So the chance of at least one match is \(1 - 0.493 = 0.507\), just over a half.

Why so few? Because what matters is not the number of people but the number of pairs, and 23 people make \(\binom{23}{2} = 253\) pairs, each with a 1 in 365 chance.

Your turn

  1. A café offers 3 breads, 4 fillings and 2 sauces. How many different sandwiches? If they add a fifth filling, how many more sandwiches does that create?
  2. How many ways can 6 friends line up for a photo? How many if the two tallest insist on standing at the two ends? (Count the two ways round.)
  3. A 4-digit PIN uses digits 0 to 9. How many PINs are there? How many have no repeated digit? What fraction of all PINs is that?
  4. Six people sit around a round table. Two seatings count as the same if everyone has the same left and right neighbours (so rotating the whole table changes nothing). Explain why the answer is \(5!\) and not \(6!\).
  5. Five people in a room. Compute the chance that they all have different birthdays, as a product of five fractions, to the nearest percent. (Use a calculator.) So what is the chance of a match?
  6. How many pairs of people are there in a room of 10? Of 23? Of 50? Use this to explain in a sentence why the table in the example climbs so quickly.

Stretch: 99% sure

How many people do you need before a shared birthday is 99% likely? Keep multiplying fractions (a spreadsheet helps) until the no-match product drops below 0.01.

Most people, asked this cold, say something like 183, half of 365. Explain what question 183 would be the answer to. (Hint: it is about matching your birthday, not any pair's.) Then estimate how many people are needed for a 50% chance that someone shares your birthday, and notice how different it is from 23.

Answers

  1. \(3 \times 4 \times 2 = 24\). With five fillings, \(3 \times 5 \times 2 = 30\), so 6 more.
  2. \(6! = 720\). With the tallest two fixed at the ends: 2 ways to place them, then \(4! = 24\) ways for the middle, total 48.
  3. \(10^4 = 10{,}000\) PINs. No repeats: \(10 \times 9 \times 8 \times 7 = 5{,}040\). That is \(50.4\%\), just over half.
  4. Sit the first person anywhere; rotating the table makes every seat the same for them, so that choice does not count. The remaining five fill the other seats in order: \(5! = 120\). Equivalently, \(6!\) counts each arrangement 6 times, once per rotation, and \(720 / 6 = 120\).
  5. \(\tfrac{365}{365} \times \tfrac{364}{365} \times \tfrac{363}{365} \times \tfrac{362}{365} \times \tfrac{361}{365} \approx 0.973\), so about 97% all different and about 3% chance of a match.
  6. \(\binom{10}{2} = 45\), \(\binom{23}{2} = 253\), \(\binom{50}{2} = 1{,}225\). Pairs grow roughly with the square of the number of people, and each pair is a fresh chance at a match, so the probability climbs much faster than the headcount.

Stretch. 57 people: the no-match product is about 0.0099, so a match is 99.0% likely. (56 people gives 98.8%.) The number 183 answers a different question: roughly how many people until one of them shares your birthday with better than even odds. Even that intuition is off. For someone to match you specifically, each person has only a 1 in 365 chance, and the no-match probability is \((364/365)^n\), which drops below one half at \(n = 253\). Matching a fixed date is hard; matching any pair is easy, because there are so many pairs.
