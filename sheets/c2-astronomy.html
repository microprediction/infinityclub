The size of everything

If the Sun were a basketball, Earth would be a peppercorn 26 metres away and the nearest star another basketball in a different country. Ratios make the universe fit in your head.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Nothing in space is the size you think it is, and nothing is as close as the pictures suggest. The best tool for seeing how big and how far is not a telescope. It is a ratio.

Start with light, the fastest thing there is, about 300,000 kilometres every second. Light from the Moon takes 1.3 seconds to reach us. Light from the Sun takes 8.3 minutes, so if the Sun vanished you would carry on seeing it for eight minutes. Light from the next nearest star, Proxima Centauri, takes 4.2 years. When you look at it tonight, you are seeing it as it was when you were about seven. A light-year is simply the distance light covers in a year, about \(9.46 \times 10^{12}\) km. It is a distance, not a time, even though it has "year" in the name.

Numbers that size mean nothing to a human brain, so astronomers shrink everything by the same factor and build a scale model. Suppose the Sun is a basketball, 24 cm across. The real Sun is \(1.39 \times 10^6\) km across, so our model shrinks everything by the ratio \(24 \text{ cm} : 1{,}390{,}000 \text{ km}\). Apply that same ratio to the Earth, which is 12,742 km across, and you get about 2 mm: a peppercorn. Apply it to the Earth–Sun distance of \(1.496 \times 10^8\) km and the peppercorn sits 26 metres from the basketball. Across the width of a sports field, a basketball and a peppercorn, and nothing else. Jupiter is a large marble 134 m away. Neptune is a small pea three quarters of a kilometre off.

And Proxima Centauri, the next basketball? Keep the same ratio and it lands roughly 6,900 km away: put the Sun in New York and the nearest star is in Berlin. Space is mostly space.

Here is the deeper connection. In 1619 Johannes Kepler found that a planet's year \(T\) and its distance from the Sun \(a\) obey one rule: \(T^2 = a^3\), when \(T\) is measured in Earth years and \(a\) in Earth–Sun distances (an astronomical unit, AU). Far planets have long years, and the rule says exactly how long. Newton later showed it falls out of gravity. You can check it tonight with a table and a pencil.

Watch one: Mars by Kepler's rule

Mars orbits at 1.524 AU. Kepler predicts \(T^2 = 1.524^3\). Compute: \(1.524^2 = 2.323\), and \(2.323 \times 1.524 = 3.540\). So \(T = \sqrt{3.540}\). Squeeze it: \(1.8^2 = 3.24\) is too small, \(1.9^2 = 3.61\) is too big, \(1.88^2 = 3.534\) is very close. Mars's year should be about 1.88 Earth years.

The measured value is 1.881 years. A rule written down four centuries ago, from naked-eye observations, predicts the Martian year to three decimal places. That is what a good law of nature feels like.

Stretch: predicting and signalling

The asteroid belt sits about 2.8 AU from the Sun. Predict the length of a year for an asteroid there. You will need the square root of \(2.8^3\); squeeze it between two whole numbers first, then between two numbers with one decimal place.

Second problem. The Voyager 1 probe is about 20 light-hours away (truly: it left in 1977 and has been coasting outward ever since). Engineers send it a command. How long before they could possibly hear back that it worked? If a mistake is in the command, how long is the earliest the fix could arrive?

Answers

  1. A light-second is 300,000 km; a light-minute is 18,000,000 km (\(1.8 \times 10^7\)). The Sun: \(1.496 \times 10^8 / 1.8 \times 10^7 \approx 8.3\) light-minutes.
  2. Moon distance: \(384{,}400 \times 24 / 1{,}390{,}000 \approx 6.6\) cm from the peppercorn. Moon size: \(3{,}474 \times 24 / 1{,}390{,}000 \approx 0.06\) cm, a grain of fine sand. The whole Earth–Moon system fits in your palm; the Sun is 26 m away.
  3. Mercury: 0.058 and 0.058. Venus: 0.378 and 0.378. Earth: 1 and 1. Jupiter: 140.7 and 140.9. Saturn: 867.9 and 867.4. They agree to within rounding every time.
  4. \(T^2 = 64 = a^3\), so \(a = 4\) AU.

Stretch. \(2.8^3 = 21.952\). \(4^2 = 16\) and \(5^2 = 25\), so \(T\) is between 4 and 5. \(4.6^2 = 21.16\), \(4.7^2 = 22.09\), so \(T \approx 4.7\) years (more precisely 4.69). The dwarf planet Ceres, at 2.77 AU, takes 4.6 years. For Voyager: the command takes 20 hours to arrive and the reply takes 20 hours to return, so 40 hours minimum before confirmation. A fix sent the moment the mistake is noticed also takes 20 hours to arrive, so engineers check such commands very carefully before pressing send.
