Why the best team doesn't always win

Suppose one team really is better: it wins six games in ten against its rival. Play a best-of-seven final and the better team still loses nearly three times in ten. Tonight, the maths underneath the sports pages.

Tonight's idea

Say team A beats team B with probability 0.6 in any single game. That is a big edge in real sport. Now ask how often A wins a series.

One game: 60%. Best of three: A needs two wins. The ways are WW, WLW, LWW. Each has probability \(0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.36\), \(0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.144\), \(0.4 \times 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.144\). Total 0.648, so 64.8%. Best of five: 68.3%. Best of seven: 71.0%. Longer series help the better team, but slowly, and never to certainty. Even a best-of-seven leaves the worse team winning 29 times in 100. That is not a scandal. That is what "probability 0.6" means (see Dice don't lie).

Tournament shape matters too. In a knockout (single elimination), one bad day and you are gone, so upsets pile up. In a round robin, where everyone plays everyone, luck has many games to average out, which is why league champions are usually stronger than cup winners. A round robin of \(n\) teams needs \(\binom{n}{2}\) games (see How many ways), which gets expensive fast.

Then there are streaks. A player scores five in a row and the commentator says they are "hot". But flip a fair coin 20 times and the chance of seeing at least one run of four heads or four tails is about 77%. Runs are what randomness looks like. Researchers have argued for forty years about whether the hot hand in basketball exists at all, and the honest answer is: if it does, it is small.

Finally, averages. A batter can have a higher average than a rival in the first half of the season and in the second half, and still a lower average for the whole season. It depends on how many times each batted in each half. This is Simpson's paradox, from Lying with averages, and it bites sports statistics constantly.

Watch one: best of seven, sweep

What is the chance the 0.6 team wins a best-of-seven four games to nothing? Four wins in a row: \(0.6 \times 0.6 \times 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.6^4 = 0.1296\), about 13%. A sweep by the worse team is \(0.4^4 = 0.0256\), about 2.6%. So sweeps by the underdog happen roughly one series in forty. Over decades of finals, you will see several.

Your turn

  1. Team A wins each game with probability 0.7. Compute the chance A wins a best-of-three by listing the cases WW, WLW, LWW. Compare with the single-game chance.
  2. Eight teams play a round robin (everyone plays everyone once). How many games is that? Explain where the number comes from, either by counting pairs or using Pascal's triangle.
  3. A knockout cup starts with 64 teams. How many matches are played in total? Give a one-sentence reason that works without drawing the bracket.
  4. In 20 coin flips there is roughly a 77% chance of a run of at least four the same. A coach sees a player miss four shots in a row and benches them "because they are cold". Write two sentences the team's statistician might say to the coach.

Stretch: how often does it go to game seven?

Two perfectly equal teams play a best-of-seven. The series lasts exactly seven games when, after six games, it is 3–3. How many of the \(2^6 = 64\) possible six-game sequences have exactly three wins for each side? (Pascal's triangle, row 6.) So what fraction of evenly matched series go the distance?

Real data from several sports show more game-sevens than this. Suggest two explanations that are not "the teams are equal", and one way you might test which is right.

Answers

  1. WW: \(0.49\). WLW: \(0.7 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.147\). LWW: \(0.147\). Total \(0.784\), so 78.4%, up from 70% for a single game.
  2. \(\binom{8}{2} = 28\) games. Each of 8 teams plays 7 others, \(8 \times 7 = 56\), but that counts every game twice, so 28. It is the third entry of row 8 of Pascal's triangle.
  3. 63 matches. Every match eliminates exactly one team, and 63 teams must be eliminated to leave one champion.
  4. Something like: "Four misses in a row happens by chance to any shooter many times a season; it is not evidence of anything. If you bench everyone after four misses you will bench your best players as often as your worst."

Stretch. Row 6 of Pascal's triangle is 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1, so 20 of the 64 sequences are 3–3: \(20/64 = 31.25\%\). Possible reasons for more game-sevens in reality: home advantage alternates and the team behind is often at home in game six; a team that is behind tries harder or changes tactics; coaches rest players when ahead. To test: compare how often the trailing team wins game six at home versus away, or check whether the pattern persists in sports with no home advantage.
