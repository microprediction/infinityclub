Calculating with beads

In practised hands a Japanese soroban adds faster than most people can type. Each rod is a digit, and carrying is something you can feel.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A soroban is a Japanese abacus: a frame of rods with sliding beads. A bar crosses the frame. On every rod, one bead sits above the bar and four sit below.

A bead counts only when it is pushed against the bar. The bead above is worth 5. Each bead below is worth 1. So one rod can show any digit from 0 to 9. For 7, bring the five-bead down and push two one-beads up.

Each rod is one digit of a number: ones on the right, then tens, then hundreds, just as in writing.

Adding is moving beads, and sometimes there are not enough. A rod shows 3 and you want to add 4, but only one one-bead is left. So add 5 and take 1 back: \(3 + 5 - 1 = 7\).

When a rod cannot hold the answer, use the rod to its left. To add 8, add 10 there and take 2 away here. That is a carry, and your fingers do it.

Watch one: 27 + 8

Set 27. The tens rod has two one-beads up. The ones rod has the five-bead down and two one-beads up.

Add 8 to the ones rod. It would pass 9, so add 10 and take away 2 instead, since \(8 = 10 - 2\).

Take 2 here: push the two one-beads on the ones rod back down. It now shows 5.

Add 10 there: push one more one-bead up on the tens rod. It now shows 3.

The soroban reads 35, and \(27 + 8 = 35\).

Your turn

  1. Read the number on each soroban.
  2. Draw the beads to show (a) 261 and (b) 509. Shade the beads that count.
  3. Describe the bead moves on a single rod, and say what the rod shows afterwards. (a) The rod shows 2; add 4. (b) The rod shows 4; add 3. (c) The rod shows 7; take away 4.
  4. (a) A soroban shows 38. Describe the bead moves that add 4, and say what it shows now. (b) A soroban shows 199. Add 1. Describe what happens on each rod, starting from the ones.

Stretch: a different abacus

A traditional Chinese abacus, the suanpan, has two beads worth 5 above the bar and five beads worth 1 below. What is the largest value one rod can show? Which values can one rod show in more than one way, and why does the soroban never have that problem?

Answers

  1. (a) 152. (b) 608. (c) 947.
  2. (a) Hundreds rod: two one-beads up. Tens rod: five-bead down and one one-bead up. Ones rod: one one-bead up. (b) Hundreds rod: five-bead down, no one-beads. Tens rod: nothing touches the bar. Ones rod: five-bead down and four one-beads up.
  3. (a) Only two one-beads are left, so add 5 and take 1: five-bead down, one one-bead back. The rod shows 6. (b) No one-beads are left, so add 5 and take 2: five-bead down, two one-beads back. It shows 7. (c) 7 is the five-bead and two one-beads, so there are not four ones to remove. Take 5 and give 1: five-bead up, one more one-bead up. It shows 3.
  4. (a) \(8 + 4\) passes 9, so add 10 and take 6. Ones rod: lift the five-bead away and push one one-bead back, leaving 2. Tens rod: push one more one-bead up, making 4. It shows 42. (b) Ones rod: \(9 + 1\) is 10, so clear the rod to 0 and carry. Tens rod: \(9 + 1\) is 10 again, so clear it and carry again. Hundreds rod: one more one-bead up, making 2. It shows 200. One small push runs along three rods.

Stretch. The largest is \(5 + 5 + 5 = 15\). Two values have two settings: 5 is one five-bead or five one-beads, and 10 is two five-beads or one five-bead with five one-beads. On a soroban the four one-beads reach only 4, so they can never stand in for the five-bead. Every digit from 0 to 9 has exactly one setting.
