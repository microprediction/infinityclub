Digits that double

Guess the square root of 10 as 3. Average 3 with 10 divided by 3. Do it again. In two steps you have 3.1623, and the number of correct digits roughly doubles each time.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

You want the positive number \(x\) with \(x \times x = 10\). That is \(\sqrt{10}\). Guess 3. Since \(3 \times 3 = 9\), your guess is too small. Now work out \(10 \div 3 = 3.33\ldots\) Multiply the two and you get exactly 10, so if 3 is too small, \(10 \div 3\) must be too big. The root is trapped between them.

So take their average as your new guess. Then do the same thing to the new guess, and again. A recipe that feeds each answer back in is called an iteration. You will want a calculator tonight.

To find \(\sqrt{N}\) for a positive number \(N\): start with any positive guess \(g\). The new guess is \(\dfrac{g + N \div g}{2}\). Repeat.

The surprise is the speed. Once a guess is close, the number of correct digits roughly doubles at every step. It is not exact doubling, only a rule of thumb, but it means four or five steps give more digits than most calculators can show.

In each row the root lies between the guess and \(10 \div g\). Row 3 already pins it between 3.1622746 and 3.1622807.

The last column compares the new guess with the true value, \(\sqrt{10} = 3.1622776601683\ldots\), counting the digits that agree, reading from the left. So 3.1666667 starts 3.16 and then goes wrong: 3 correct digits. Next, 3.1622807 starts 3.1622: 5 correct digits. The new guess in row 3, kept as an exact fraction, is \(3.1622776601698\ldots\) It agrees as far as 3.16227766016: 12 correct digits.

The starting guess 3 had 1 correct digit, so the count goes 1, 3, 5, 12. That is roughly doubling, not exactly. One more step gives 25.

Stretch: cube roots

The cube root of 10 is the number \(x\) with \(x \times x \times x = 10\). Try this recipe: the new guess is \(\dfrac{2x + 10 \div x^2}{3}\). (a) Start from \(x = 2\) and do two steps. (b) Cube your answer to check it. (c) The square root recipe averages two numbers whose product is \(N\). Which three numbers does this recipe average, and what is their product?

Answers

  1. Step 1: \(2 \div 1 = 2\), and the average of 1 and 2 is 1.5. Step 2: \(2 \div 1.5 = 1.3333333\), and the average is 1.4166667. Step 3: \(2 \div 1.4166667 = 1.4117647\), and the average is 1.4142157. As fractions the guesses are \(\frac{3}{2}\), \(\frac{17}{12}\) and \(\frac{577}{408}\). Your last digit may differ by one, depending on how your calculator rounds.
  2. (a) The guess 1 has 1 correct digit. Then 1.5 has 1 (only the 1 before the point), 1.4166667 has 3 (it starts 1.41) and 1.4142157 has 6 (it starts 1.41421). (b) It agrees as far as 1.41421356237, which is 12 correct digits. (c) No. The count goes 1, 1, 3, 6, 12. The first step does not double it at all, and the next one triples it. After that, with the guess close, it doubles: 3, 6, 12. For \(\sqrt{10}\) the count went 1, 3, 5, 12. "Roughly doubles, once you are close" is the honest rule.
  3. (a) Step 1: \(20 \div 4 = 5\), and the average of 4 and 5 is 4.5. Step 2: \(20 \div 4.5 = 4.4444444\), and the average is 4.4722222 (exactly \(\frac{161}{36}\)). (b) \(4.4722222 \times 4.4722222\) is about 20.00077, too big by less than 0.001. (c) \(20 \div 4.4722222 = 4.4720497\). The root lies between 4.4720497 and 4.4722222, so it certainly starts 4.472. You know that from the trap alone. In fact \(\sqrt{20} = 4.4721359\ldots\), and one more step gives 4.4721360.
  4. (a) From 2: \(10 \div 2 = 5\), and the average of 2 and 5 is 3.5. From 5: \(10 \div 5 = 2\), and the average of 5 and 2 is 3.5 again. The step averages the pair \(g\) and \(10 \div g\), and starting from either number of the pair gives the same pair. (b) The product is 10, so \(m \times m - d \times d = 10\), which says \(m \times m = 10 + d \times d\). A square such as \(d \times d\) is never negative, so \(m \times m\) is at least 10. Since \(m\) is positive, \(m\) is at least \(\sqrt{10}\). Check with \(g = 3\): \(m = \frac{19}{6}\), \(d = \frac{1}{6}\), and \(m \times m = \frac{361}{36} = 10 + \frac{1}{36}\). (c) Only when \(d = 0\). Then \(g\) and \(10 \div g\) are equal, so \(g \times g = 10\): the guess was already the exact root. So after the first step, every guess is at or above the root, whatever positive guess you started from. Nothing here is special to 10. On a calculator, rounding can blur the very last digit.

Stretch. (a) Step 1: \(10 \div 2^2 = 2.5\), so the new guess is \((4 + 2.5) \div 3 = 2.1666667\). Step 2: \(10 \div 2.1666667^2 = 2.1301775\), so the new guess is \((4.3333333 + 2.1301775) \div 3 = 2.1545036\). (b) \(2.1545036^3\) is about 10.00096. The true cube root is \(2.15443469\ldots\), so you have 4 correct digits, and a third step gives 2.1544347. (c) It averages \(x\), \(x\) and \(10 \div x^2\). Their product is \(x \times x \times 10 \div x^2 = 10\). If all three were equal, each would be the cube root of 10. When \(x\) is too small, \(10 \div x^2\) is too big, and the average pulls the guess towards the root, just as before.
