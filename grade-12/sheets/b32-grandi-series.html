What is 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + …?

The partial sums of 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + … go 1, 0, 1, 0 for ever, so the series has no sum. Yet two famous methods, averaging and Abel's trick, both assign it the value 1/2.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Add up \(1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots\) one term at a time. The partial sums go 1, 0, 1, 0, for ever. They never settle, so the series diverges: it has no sum. Still, they seem to hover around \(\tfrac12\). A summability method is a precise rule that turns such a hunch into a value. Here are two, for a series \(a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots\) with partial sums \(s_1, s_2, \dots\)

Cesàro (averaging). Let \(\sigma_n = \dfrac{s_1 + s_2 + \cdots + s_n}{n}\). If \(\sigma_n\) approaches \(S\), the series is Cesàro summable to \(S\).
Abel. Let \(A(x) = a_1 + a_2 x + a_3 x^2 + \cdots\). If this converges for \(0 \le x \lt 1\) and \(A(x)\) approaches \(S\) as \(x\) climbs to 1, the series is Abel summable to \(S\).

Both rules are honest: a series that already converges to \(S\) gets the value \(S\) from each (two theorems; exercise 4 tests one case). Some divergent series get a value too, but not all. And "summable to \(\tfrac12\)" does not mean "equals \(\tfrac12\)". The series still diverges.

Watch one: averaging 1, 0, 1, 0, …

For \(1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots\) the partial sums are \(s_n = 1\) for odd \(n\) and \(s_n = 0\) for even \(n\). Average the first \(n\) of them: \(\sigma_1 = 1\), \(\sigma_2 = \tfrac12\), \(\sigma_3 = \tfrac23\), \(\sigma_4 = \tfrac12\), \(\sigma_5 = \tfrac35\), \(\sigma_6 = \tfrac12\), and so on.

Among the first \(n\) partial sums, the ones equal to 1 are those with odd index. For even \(n\) that is exactly half of them, so \(\sigma_n = \tfrac12\). For odd \(n\) it is \(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\) of them, so \(\sigma_n = \tfrac{n+1}{2n} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{2n}\).

The extra \(\tfrac{1}{2n}\) shrinks towards 0 (\(\sigma_{99} = \tfrac{50}{99} \approx 0.505\)), so \(\sigma_n\) approaches \(\tfrac12\): the series is Cesàro summable to \(\tfrac12\). The partial sums themselves have not moved. They are still 1, 0, 1, 0.

Your turn

  1. Abel's method on the same series. Here \(A(x) = 1 - x + x^2 - x^3 + \cdots\), a geometric series with ratio \(-x\). (a) Write its sum as a formula, valid for \(0 \le x \lt 1\). (b) Evaluate it at \(x = 0.9\), \(0.99\) and \(0.999\). What value does Abel's method assign? (c) Why are you not allowed to skip the limit and put \(x = 1\) into the series?
  2. Now take \(1 - 2 + 3 - 4 + \cdots\). (a) Write down the first eight partial sums and the first eight averages \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_8\). (b) Find \(\sigma_n\) for even \(n\), and for odd \(n\). (c) Is this series Cesàro summable?
  3. Abel's method on \(1 - 2 + 3 - 4 + \cdots\). Let \(B(x) = 1 - 2x + 3x^2 - 4x^3 + \cdots\), which does converge for \(0 \le x \lt 1\) (take that on trust). (a) Work out \((1 + x)B(x) = B(x) + xB(x)\) by collecting powers of \(x\). Which series appears? Deduce that \(B(x) = \tfrac{1}{(1+x)^2}\). (b) Evaluate \(B(0.9)\) and \(B(0.99)\). What value does Abel's method assign? (c) Which of the two methods reaches further?
  4. An honesty check on a series that converges: \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots = 1\), with \(s_n = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2^n}\). (a) Show that \(\sigma_n = 1 - \dfrac{1}{n}\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{2^n}\right)\). Compute \(\sigma_4\) and \(\sigma_{10}\) and compare \(\sigma_{10}\) with \(s_{10}\). What does \(\sigma_n\) approach? (b) Find \(A(x) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 x + \tfrac18 x^2 + \cdots\) as a formula. What does it approach as \(x\) climbs to 1?

Stretch: average again, and a series nobody rescues

(a) Averaging failed in exercise 2. So average the averages: let \(\tau_n\) be the mean of \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_n\) for \(1 - 2 + 3 - 4 + \cdots\). Compute \(\tau_2, \tau_4, \tau_6\). Where do the \(\tau_n\) seem to be heading, and why is that plausible? (b) Try both methods on \(1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots\). (c) "\(1 - 1 + 1 - \cdots\) diverges" and "\(1 - 1 + 1 - \cdots\) is summable to \(\tfrac12\)" are both true. Why is that not a contradiction?

Answers

  1. (a) First term 1, ratio \(-x\), and \(|-x| \lt 1\), so \(A(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x}\). (b) \(A(0.9) = \tfrac{1}{1.9} \approx 0.5263\), \(A(0.99) \approx 0.5025\), \(A(0.999) \approx 0.50025\). As \(x\) climbs to 1, \(\tfrac{1}{1+x}\) approaches \(\tfrac12\), so the series is Abel summable to \(\tfrac12\), the same value averaging gave. (c) The formula was proved only where the geometric series converges, \(|x| \lt 1\). At \(x = 1\) the series is \(1 - 1 + 1 - \cdots\) itself, which diverges, so it has no value to compare with. Abel's method is the limit, never the substitution.
  2. (a) Partial sums: \(1, -1, 2, -2, 3, -3, 4, -4\). Averages: \(1, 0, \tfrac23, 0, \tfrac35, 0, \tfrac47, 0\). (b) The partial sums cancel in pairs, so for even \(n\) their total is 0 and \(\sigma_n = 0\). For odd \(n = 2k - 1\) the total is the last one left over, \(s_n = k\), so \(\sigma_n = \tfrac{k}{2k-1} = \tfrac{n+1}{2n}\), which approaches \(\tfrac12\). (c) No. The averages at even \(n\) sit at 0 while those at odd \(n\) head for \(\tfrac12\), so \(\sigma_n\) approaches no single number.
  3. (a) The coefficient of \(x^m\) in \(B(x)\) is \((-1)^m (m+1)\), and in \(xB(x)\) it is \((-1)^{m-1} m\). Add them: \((-1)^m (m + 1 - m) = (-1)^m\). So \((1+x)B(x) = 1 - x + x^2 - x^3 + \cdots = \dfrac{1}{1+x}\) by exercise 1, and dividing by \(1 + x\) gives \(B(x) = \dfrac{1}{(1+x)^2}\). (b) \(B(0.9) = \tfrac{1}{3.61} \approx 0.2770\) and \(B(0.99) = \tfrac{1}{3.9601} \approx 0.2525\). As \(x\) climbs to 1 this approaches \(\tfrac14\): the series is Abel summable to \(\tfrac14\). (c) Abel's method: it gives a value here, where averaging gave none. It is also a theorem (not proved here) that whenever averaging gives a value, Abel's method gives the same one.
  4. (a) \(s_1 + \cdots + s_n = n - \left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{2^n}\right) = n - \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{2^n}\right)\). Divide by \(n\) to get the formula. \(\sigma_4 = 1 - \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{15}{16} = \tfrac{49}{64} \approx 0.766\) and \(\sigma_{10} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{10} \cdot \tfrac{1023}{1024} \approx 0.9001\), while \(s_{10} = \tfrac{1023}{1024} \approx 0.9990\). The bracket stays below 1, so \(\sigma_n\) is within \(\tfrac1n\) of 1 and approaches 1, the true sum. Averaging is slower than the series itself, but it arrives at the same place. (b) Geometric with first term \(\tfrac12\) and ratio \(\tfrac{x}{2}\): \(A(x) = \dfrac{1/2}{1 - x/2} = \dfrac{1}{2 - x}\), which approaches 1 as \(x\) climbs to 1. Both methods return the ordinary sum.

Stretch. (a) \(\tau_2 = \tfrac12(1 + 0) = \tfrac12\), \(\tau_4 = \tfrac14\left(1 + \tfrac23\right) = \tfrac{5}{12} \approx 0.417\), \(\tau_6 = \tfrac16\left(1 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac35\right) = \tfrac{17}{45} \approx 0.378\). They head, slowly, for \(\tfrac14\) (\(\tau_{1000} \approx 0.252\)). That is plausible because half of the \(\sigma\)'s are 0 and the other half are close to \(\tfrac12\), so their mean is close to \(\tfrac14\). Averaging twice gives the value Abel's method gave. (b) The partial sums are \(s_n = n\), so \(\sigma_n = \tfrac{1 + 2 + \cdots + n}{n} = \tfrac{n+1}{2}\), which grows without limit. And \(A(x) = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots = \tfrac{1}{1-x}\) also grows without limit as \(x\) climbs to 1: it is 1000 at \(x = 0.999\). Neither method assigns a value, and neither rescues \(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots\) either (see 1 + 2 + 3 + … = −1/12?). (c) The two sentences are about different lists of numbers. "Diverges" says the partial sums \(s_n\) approach no limit. "Cesàro summable to \(\tfrac12\)" says the averages \(\sigma_n\) do approach \(\tfrac12\). "Abel summable to \(\tfrac12\)" says the function \(A(x)\) approaches \(\tfrac12\). A list can jump about for ever while its running average settles. A summability method is a new definition that agrees with the old one wherever the old one applies. It does not make the series converge.
