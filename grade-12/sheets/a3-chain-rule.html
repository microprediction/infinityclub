The chain rule: gears

If y turns three times for each turn of u, and u twice for each turn of x, y turns six times for x. That is the chain rule, and why it multiplies.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Three gears in a row. The big one, \(x\), has 36 teeth. It drives \(u\), with 18 teeth, so \(u\) turns twice for each turn of \(x\). Gear \(u\) drives \(y\), with 6 teeth, so \(y\) turns three times for each turn of \(u\). So \(y\) turns six times for each turn of \(x\): \(3 \times 2\). Rates through a chain multiply, because the middle gear's output is the last gear's input.

Functions chain the same way. Suppose \(y = f(u)\) and \(u = g(x)\), so \(y = f(g(x))\): \(x\) goes into \(g\), and what comes out goes into \(f\). Nudge \(x\) by \(\Delta x\). Then \(u\) changes by \(\Delta u\) and \(y\) by \(\Delta y\), and as long as \(\Delta u \ne 0\),

\[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u} \cdot \frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}.\]

Just arithmetic: the \(\Delta u\) cancels. Let \(\Delta x \to 0\). Then \(\Delta u \to 0\) too, because \(g\) is smooth, and each ratio becomes a derivative.

\(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}\), or \(\big(f(g(x))\big)' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\): differentiate the outer function at the inner value, then multiply by the derivative of the inner.

Gears have constant rates. Functions do not, so the rule is read at a point: \(dy/du\) is evaluated at the current \(u = g(x)\), not at \(x\). That is what \(f'(g(x))\) says.

Watch one: \(y = (x^2 + 1)^5\)

Expanding is possible but miserable. Instead name the inside: \(u = x^2 + 1\), so \(y = u^5\). Then \(dy/du = 5u^4\) and \(du/dx = 2x\), and

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = 5u^4 \cdot 2x = 10x\,(x^2 + 1)^4.\]

Check at \(x = 1\): slope \(10 \cdot 16 = 160\). Numerically, \(y(1.001) - y(1) = 0.1604\), so the secant slope is about 160.4. Good. The order of the pieces matters: the outer derivative \(5u^4\) is evaluated at \(u = x^2 + 1\), never at \(x\). Writing \(5x^4 \cdot 2x\) is the classic mistake.

Your turn

  1. Name the inner function, then differentiate: (a) \(y = (3x + 1)^4\); (b) \(y = (2x^3 - 1)^4\).
  2. Differentiate \(y = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}\) by writing it as \(u^{-1}\) with \(u = x^2 + 1\), using \((u^{-1})' = -u^{-2}\). Where is the slope zero, and what does the graph do there?
  3. Given \(g(1) = 2\), \(g'(1) = 4\), \(f(2) = 7\), \(f'(2) = 5\), \(f(1) = 3\), \(f'(1) = -2\), \(g(3) = 0\), \(g'(3) = 6\), find (a) the derivative of \(f(g(x))\) at \(x = 1\); (b) the derivative of \(g(f(x))\) at \(x = 1\). Why are they different?
  4. (a) Differentiate \(y = \big((x^2 + 1)^3 + x\big)^2\), a chain of three gears, and evaluate at \(x = 1\). (b) Let \(s(x) = \sqrt{x}\), so \(s(x)^2 = x\). Differentiate both sides with the chain rule and solve for \(s'(x)\). Then find the derivative of \(\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\).

Stretch: running the gears backwards

Let \(g\) be the inverse of \(f\), so \(f(g(x)) = x\) for all \(x\). Differentiate both sides and solve for \(g'(x)\). Use your formula with \(f(x) = x^3\) to find the derivative of the cube root, and check it matches the power rule with \(n = \tfrac13\).

Answers

  1. (a) \(u = 3x + 1\): \(y' = 4u^3 \cdot 3 = 12(3x + 1)^3\). (b) \(u = 2x^3 - 1\): \(y' = 4u^3 \cdot 6x^2 = 24x^2 (2x^3 - 1)^3\).
  2. \(y' = -u^{-2} \cdot 2x = -\dfrac{2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2}\). Zero at \(x = 0\), where the graph has its peak, \(y = 1\); it falls away on both sides.
  3. (a) \(f'(g(1))\,g'(1) = f'(2) \cdot 4 = 20\). (b) \(g'(f(1))\,f'(1) = g'(3) \cdot (-2) = -12\). The outer derivative is read at the inner value, and the inner values differ: \(g(1) = 2\) for one, \(f(1) = 3\) for the other. Composition is not commutative, so neither is its derivative.
  4. (a) \(y' = 2\big((x^2 + 1)^3 + x\big)\big(3(x^2 + 1)^2 \cdot 2x + 1\big)\). At \(x = 1\): \(2 \cdot 9 \cdot 25 = 450\). (b) \(2s(x)\,s'(x) = 1\), so \(s'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\). Then \(\big(\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\big)' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2 + 1}} \cdot 2x = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}\).

Stretch. \(f'(g(x))\,g'(x) = 1\), so \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))}\): the inverse's slope is the reciprocal of the original's slope, read at the matching point. With \(f(x) = x^3\), \(f'(x) = 3x^2\) and \(g(x) = x^{1/3}\), so \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{3(x^{1/3})^2} = \dfrac{1}{3x^{2/3}} = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3}\), exactly \(n x^{n-1}\) with \(n = \tfrac13\). Sheet 4 uses this to differentiate \(\ln x\).
