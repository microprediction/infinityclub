Which primes are sums of two squares

5, 13, 17, 29 are; 3, 7, 11, 19 are not. The rule is the remainder mod 4, and the reason runs through Gaussian integers.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Every integer is 0, 1, 2 or 3 mod 4, and squaring those gives 0, 1, 0, 1. So every square is 0 or 1 mod 4. (\(a \equiv b \pmod 4\): same remainder on division by 4.) Two squares add to 0, 1 or 2 mod 4, never 3. So nothing that is 3 mod 4, prime or not, is a sum of two squares. That is the easy half, fully proved.

The hard half is the converse: every prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) is a sum of two squares. Fermat stated it; Euler proved it. The reason lives in the Gaussian integers \(a + bi\) (\(a, b\) integers), whose norm \(N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2\) multiplies: \(N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)\). So \(p = a^2 + b^2\) says exactly that \(p = (a + bi)(a - bi)\) splits.

Two facts make it split. When \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) there is an \(x\) with \(x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p\) (the stretch), so \(p\) divides \((x + i)(x - i)\) without dividing either factor. And Gaussian integers have division with remainder, so Euclid's lemma (a prime dividing a product divides a factor) holds for them too. So \(p\) is not a Gaussian prime: \(p = \alpha\beta\), and taking norms, \(N(\alpha) = p\).

Watch one: 13 from \(5^2 \equiv -1\)

\(5^2 = 25 = 2 \cdot 13 - 1\), so 13 divides \(5^2 + 1 = (5 + i)(5 - i)\). Divide 13 by \(5 + i\) as Gaussian integers:

\[\frac{13}{5 + i} = \frac{13(5 - i)}{26} = \frac{5 - i}{2} = 2.5 - 0.5i.\]

Round to a nearest Gaussian integer, say \(2 - i\); the remainder is \(13 - (5 + i)(2 - i) = 13 - (11 - 3i) = 2 + 3i\), of norm \(4 + 9 = 13\). Euclid's algorithm continues with \(5 + i\) and \(2 + 3i\), and stops at once:

\[\frac{5 + i}{2 + 3i} = \frac{(5 + i)(2 - 3i)}{13} = \frac{13 - 13i}{13} = 1 - i.\]

So \(2 + 3i\) is a common divisor of 13 and \(5 + i\), and \(13 = (2 + 3i)(2 - 3i) = 2^2 + 3^2\). No searching was needed.

Your turn

  1. Write 17, 37, 41 and 53 as sums of two squares. Check that each is \(1 \pmod 4\).
  2. (a) Explain, without searching, why 2023 is not a sum of two squares. (b) \(21 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). Is 21 a sum of two squares? What does that say about the rule?
  3. Multiply out \((1 + 2i)(2 + 3i)\) and \((1 + 2i)(2 - 3i)\) and take norms. Hence write \(65 = 5 \times 13\) as a sum of two squares in two ways. Then expand \((a + bi)(c + di)\) and take norms: what identity appears?
  4. \(12^2 = 144 \equiv -1 \pmod{29}\). Divide 29 by \(12 + i\), round to a nearest Gaussian integer, find the remainder and its norm, and read off 29 as a sum of two squares. Why did one step suffice?

Stretch: the missing fact

Wilson's theorem says \((p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p\) for every prime \(p\); take it as given. Pair each \(k \le \tfrac{p-1}{2}\) with \(p - k \equiv -k\) to show that when \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(x = \left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)!\) satisfies \(x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p\). Check \(p = 13\).

Answers

  1. \(17 = 16 + 1\), \(37 = 36 + 1\), \(41 = 25 + 16\), \(53 = 49 + 4\). Remainders mod 4: all 1.
  2. (a) \(2023 = 4 \cdot 505 + 3\), so \(2023 \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) and no sum of two squares can equal it. (b) \(21 - 1\), \(21 - 4\), \(21 - 9\), \(21 - 16\) are 20, 17, 12, 5, none a square, so no. The mod-4 test is a theorem about primes. For composites the full rule is: \(n\) is a sum of two squares exactly when every prime \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\) in its factorisation appears to an even power. \(21 = 3 \cdot 7\) fails; \(9 = 3^2 = 9 + 0\) and \(45 = 3^2 \cdot 5 = 36 + 9\) pass.
  3. \((1 + 2i)(2 + 3i) = -4 + 7i\) and \((1 + 2i)(2 - 3i) = 8 + i\). Norms: \(16 + 49 = 65 = 64 + 1\). In general \((a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i\), and taking norms gives \((a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2\): the right side is visibly a sum of two squares.
  4. \(\dfrac{29}{12 + i} = \dfrac{29(12 - i)}{145} = \dfrac{12 - i}{5} = 2.4 - 0.2i\), nearest Gaussian integer 2. Remainder \(29 - 2(12 + i) = 5 - 2i\), norm \(25 + 4 = 29\). So \(29 = 5^2 + 2^2\). The remainder already has norm 29, a prime, so it cannot be split further; the algorithm has found the Gaussian prime factor of 29 in one step.

Stretch. Group the factors of \((p-1)!\) in pairs \(k\) and \(p - k\) for \(k = 1, \dots, \tfrac{p-1}{2}\). Since \(p - k \equiv -k\), each pair contributes \(k \cdot (-k) = -k^2\), and \((p - 1)! \equiv (-1)^{(p-1)/2}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)!^2\). When \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\) is even, the sign is \(+\), and Wilson gives \(x^2 \equiv -1\). For \(p = 13\): \(6! = 720 = 55 \cdot 13 + 5\), so \(x \equiv 5\), and \(25 \equiv -1 \pmod{13}\), the very number the example began with.
