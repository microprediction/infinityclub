Least squares is a projection

The best-fit line is the shadow of the data on a plane. Derive the normal equations from "the error is perpendicular to the fit".

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

Three data points \((x_i, y_i)\), and you want the line \(y = a + bx\) that fits them best. Least squares defines best: make the residuals \(r_i = y_i - (a + bx_i)\), the vertical misses, have the smallest possible sum of squares.

Now stack the \(y\)-values into one vector \(\mathbf y = (y_1, y_2, y_3)\). The fitted values are a vector too: \(\hat{\mathbf y} = a\mathbf 1 + b\mathbf x\), with \(\mathbf 1 = (1, 1, 1)\) and \(\mathbf x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)\). As \(a, b\) vary, \(\hat{\mathbf y}\) sweeps out a plane through the origin, and \(\sum r_i^2 = |\mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}|^2\) is the squared distance from \(\mathbf y\) to it.

The closest point of a plane is the foot of the perpendicular: for any other point \(\mathbf p\) of the plane, Pythagoras gives \(|\mathbf y - \mathbf p|^2 = |\mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}|^2 + |\hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf p|^2\). So the residual \(\mathbf r = \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf 1\) and to \(\mathbf x\) (dot products zero): \(\mathbf 1 \cdot \mathbf r = 0\), \(\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf r = 0\). Written out, these are the normal equations (normal means perpendicular):

\(\sum y_i = na + b\sum x_i, \qquad \sum x_i y_i = a\sum x_i + b\sum x_i^2.\)

Watch one: \((1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2)\)

\(n = 3\), \(\sum x = 6\), \(\sum y = 5\), \(\sum xy = 11\), \(\sum x^2 = 14\). The normal equations:

\[5 = 3a + 6b, \qquad 11 = 6a + 14b.\]

Double the first and subtract: \(2b = 1\), so \(b = \tfrac12\) and \(a = \tfrac23\). The line is \(y = \tfrac23 + \tfrac12 x\), with residuals \(\mathbf r = (-\tfrac16, \tfrac13, -\tfrac16)\). Check: \(\mathbf 1 \cdot \mathbf r = 0\) and \(\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf r = -\tfrac16 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac12 = 0\). Sum of squares \(\tfrac{1}{36} + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{36} = \tfrac16\). Any other line does worse: the one through the end points has sum of squares \(\tfrac14\).

Your turn

  1. Points \((0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 3)\). Write the two normal equations, solve them, and check that \(\sum r_i = 0\) and \(\sum x_i r_i = 0\).
  2. (a) Divide the first normal equation by \(n\). Which point must the line pass through? (b) Fit a constant \(y = a\) by least squares (the only direction is \(\mathbf 1\)). Show \(a = \bar y\).
  3. Points \((1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 6)\). Find the line, the four residuals and the sum of squares. What does the line predict at \(x = 6\)?
  4. A line through the origin, \(y = bx\), has one direction only, \(\mathbf x\). Use \(\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf r = 0\) to find \(b\), and compare with grade 10's projection formula \(\dfrac{\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf y}{\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf x}\,\mathbf x\). Apply it to the example's points and compare the sum of squares with \(\tfrac16\). Why can it not be smaller?

Stretch: a parabola is still "linear"

\(y = a + bx + cx^2\) is still linear in the unknowns \(a, b, c\), so least squares is still a projection, onto the flat spanned by \(\mathbf 1\), \(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf x^2 = (x_1^2, \dots, x_n^2)\). Write the three normal equations. For the example's three points, what are the residuals, and why?

Answers

  1. \(\sum x = 3\), \(\sum y = 4\), \(\sum xy = 7\), \(\sum x^2 = 5\). \(4 = 3a + 3b\) and \(7 = 3a + 5b\), so \(b = \tfrac32\), \(a = -\tfrac16\): \(y = -\tfrac16 + \tfrac32 x\). Fitted \(-\tfrac16, \tfrac43, \tfrac{17}{6}\); residuals \(\tfrac16, -\tfrac13, \tfrac16\). \(\sum r_i = 0\) and \(\sum x_i r_i = 0 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = 0\). Sum of squares \(\tfrac16\).
  2. (a) \(\bar y = a + b\bar x\), which says \((\bar x, \bar y)\) is on the line. (b) Only \(\mathbf 1 \cdot \mathbf r = 0\) survives: \(\sum (y_i - a) = 0\), so \(a = \bar y\). The mean is the projection of \(\mathbf y\) onto the line through \(\mathbf 1\).
  3. \(\sum x = 10\), \(\sum y = 16\), \(\sum xy = 47\), \(\sum x^2 = 30\). \(16 = 4a + 10b\), \(47 = 10a + 30b\), so \(b = 1.4\), \(a = 0.5\). Fitted 1.9, 3.3, 4.7, 6.1; residuals \(0.1, -0.3, 0.3, -0.1\); sum of squares 0.2. At \(x = 6\) the line gives 8.9, a prediction outside the range of the data, so trust it less.
  4. \(\mathbf x \cdot (\mathbf y - b\mathbf x) = 0\) gives \(b = \dfrac{\sum x_i y_i}{\sum x_i^2} = \dfrac{\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf y}{\mathbf x \cdot \mathbf x}\), exactly the projection coefficient. For the example, \(b = \tfrac{11}{14}\), residuals \(\tfrac{3}{14}, \tfrac{6}{14}, -\tfrac{5}{14}\), sum of squares \(\tfrac{70}{196} = \tfrac{5}{14} \approx 0.357\), larger than \(\tfrac16\). The line through the origin is one of the candidates in the two-direction plane (the case \(a = 0\)), so the best over the whole plane cannot do worse.

Stretch. \(\mathbf r\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf 1\), \(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf x^2\): \(\sum y_i = na + b\sum x_i + c\sum x_i^2\), \(\sum x_i y_i = a\sum x_i + b\sum x_i^2 + c\sum x_i^3\), \(\sum x_i^2 y_i = a\sum x_i^2 + b\sum x_i^3 + c\sum x_i^4\). With three points and three unknowns, the vectors \(\mathbf 1, \mathbf x, \mathbf x^2\) span all of three-dimensional space (the \(x_i\) are distinct), so \(\mathbf y\) already lies in the flat: every residual is zero. The parabola \(y = -1 + \tfrac52 x - \tfrac12 x^2\) passes through all three points. A perfect fit with as many parameters as points has learned nothing.
