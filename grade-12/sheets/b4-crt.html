The Chinese remainder theorem

A number that leaves remainder 2 mod 3, 3 mod 5 and 2 mod 7. Why there is exactly one answer mod 105, and how to find it.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

A problem from Sunzi's Mathematical Classic, written in China some sixteen centuries ago: find a number leaving remainder \(2\) when divided by \(3\), \(3\) when divided by \(5\), and \(2\) when divided by \(7\). In the notation of clock arithmetic, where \(a \equiv b \pmod m\) means \(m\) divides \(a - b\):

\[x \equiv 2 \pmod 3, \qquad x \equiv 3 \pmod 5, \qquad x \equiv 2 \pmod 7.\]

If the moduli are pairwise coprime (no two share a factor), there is exactly one solution modulo their product, here \(3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 105\).

Why unique. If \(x\) and \(y\) both work, \(x - y\) is divisible by \(3\), by \(5\) and by \(7\). Coprime divisors multiply: \(105\) divides \(x - y\). So any two solutions agree mod \(105\).

Why one exists. Each residue \(0, \ldots, 104\) gives a triple of remainders \((x \bmod 3,\ x \bmod 5,\ x \bmod 7)\). By uniqueness, no two residues give the same triple. There are \(3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 105\) possible triples. So \(105\) residues land on \(105\) triples without repeats: every triple, including \((2, 3, 2)\), is hit exactly once. The figure shows two moduli.

Coprimality matters: \(x \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod 6\) has no solution, since the first makes \(x\) odd and the second even.

Watch one: building the answer from switches

For each modulus, multiply the other two: \(35\), \(21\), \(15\). Now \(35\) is \(0\) mod \(5\) and mod \(7\); mod \(3\) it is \(2\), and \(2 \cdot 2 = 4 \equiv 1\), so \(e_1 = 2 \cdot 35 = 70\) has remainders \((1, 0, 0)\). Already \(21 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\) and \(15 \equiv 1 \pmod 7\), so \(e_2 = 21\) gives \((0, 1, 0)\) and \(e_3 = 15\) gives \((0, 0, 1)\). These are switches. Scale by the wanted remainders and add:

\[x = 2 \cdot 70 + 3 \cdot 21 + 2 \cdot 15 = 233 \equiv 23 \pmod{105}.\]

Check: \(23 = 7 \cdot 3 + 2 = 4 \cdot 5 + 3 = 3 \cdot 7 + 2\). Every solution is \(23 + 105k\).

RSA works mod \(pq\); computers use this theorem to work mod \(p\) and mod \(q\) separately, then recombine: infinityclub.net/demos/rsa.html.

Your turn

  1. Find every \(x\) with \(x \equiv 1 \pmod 3\) and \(x \equiv 2 \pmod 4\).
  2. Solve \(x \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod 5\), \(x \equiv 3 \pmod 9\) by the switch method. Give the switches and the answer mod \(180\).
  3. \(4\) and \(6\) are not coprime. (a) Show that \(x \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(x \equiv 3 \pmod 6\) does have solutions, and find them all. (b) For which pairs of remainders \(a, b\) is \(x \equiv a \pmod 4\), \(x \equiv b \pmod 6\) solvable?
  4. A basket of eggs. Counted in twos, threes, fours, fives or sixes, one is always left over; counted in sevens, none. Smallest possible number of eggs? First collapse the five "one left over" conditions into one congruence.

Stretch: square roots of 1

Modulo a prime \(p\), \(x^2 \equiv 1\) has exactly two solutions, \(\pm 1\), because \(p\) must divide \((x - 1)(x + 1)\). How many solutions does \(x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{105}\) have? Count them with the theorem, then list them.

Answers

  1. Numbers that are \(2 \pmod 4\): \(2, 6, 10, \ldots\); the first that is \(1 \pmod 3\) is \(10\). So \(x \equiv 10 \pmod{12}\): \(10, 22, 34, \ldots\)
  2. \(M = 180\); \(M_1 = 45\), \(M_2 = 36\), \(M_3 = 20\). \(45 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) so \(e_1 = 45\); \(36 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\) so \(e_2 = 36\); \(20 \equiv 2 \pmod 9\) and \(2 \cdot 5 = 10 \equiv 1\), so \(e_3 = 5 \cdot 20 = 100\). Then \(x = 1 \cdot 45 + 2 \cdot 36 + 3 \cdot 100 = 417 \equiv 57 \pmod{180}\). Check: \(57 = 14 \cdot 4 + 1 = 11 \cdot 5 + 2 = 6 \cdot 9 + 3\).
  3. (a) \(1 \pmod 4\): \(1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21, \ldots\); of these, \(9\) and \(21\) are \(3 \pmod 6\). The solutions are \(x \equiv 9 \pmod{12}\): the modulus is the lowest common multiple \(12\), not the product \(24\). (b) Both conditions fix \(x \bmod 2\) (the shared factor \(\gcd(4, 6) = 2\)): \(x \equiv a \pmod 2\) and \(x \equiv b \pmod 2\). Solvable exactly when \(a\) and \(b\) have the same parity, and then there is one solution mod \(12\).
  4. One left over when counted in \(2, 3, 4, 5, 6\) means \(x - 1\) is divisible by all of them, so by \(\operatorname{lcm} = 60\): \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{60}\). Combine with \(x \equiv 0 \pmod 7\) (coprime to \(60\)). Try \(1, 61, 121, 181, 241, 301\): \(301 = 7 \cdot 43\). Smallest \(301\); in general \(x \equiv 301 \pmod{420}\).

Stretch. \(x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{105}\) holds exactly when \(x^2 \equiv 1\) mod \(3\), mod \(5\) and mod \(7\) at once. Each gives two choices, \(x \equiv \pm 1\), and by the theorem every one of the \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8\) combinations is a different residue mod \(105\). The eight are \(1, 29, 34, 41, 64, 71, 76, 104\), in four \(\pm\) pairs (\(104 = -1\), \(76 = -29\), \(71 = -34\), \(64 = -41\)). For instance \(29 \equiv (-1, -1, 1)\) mod \((3, 5, 7)\), and \(29^2 = 841 = 8 \cdot 105 + 1\). A composite modulus has more square roots of \(1\) than a prime, which is one way to detect that a number is composite without factoring it.
