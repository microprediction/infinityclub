The cubic formula, and the numbers it forced into existence

Solve \(x^3 = 15x + 4\) by Cardano's method and the answer, 4, arrives wrapped in square roots of negative numbers. This is why complex numbers were invented.

Name ____________________Date ____________

Tonight's idea

The quadratic formula has a cousin for cubics. Take a cubic with no \(x^2\) term, written \(x^3 = px + q\). The trick is to look for the answer as a sum, \(x = u + v\). Expand the cube and regroup:

\[(u + v)^3 = 3uv(u + v) + (u^3 + v^3).\]

This matches \(x^3 = px + q\) if \(3uv = p\) and \(u^3 + v^3 = q\). So \(u^3\) and \(v^3\) are two numbers with sum \(q\) and product \((p/3)^3\). Two numbers with a known sum and product are the roots of a quadratic (grade 10): \(t^2 - qt + (p/3)^3 = 0\). Solve it, take cube roots, and add.

Cardano's formula. \(x = \sqrt[3]{\tfrac{q}{2} + \sqrt{D}} + \sqrt[3]{\tfrac{q}{2} - \sqrt{D}}\), where \(D = \left(\tfrac{q}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\tfrac{p}{3}\right)^3\).

Gerolamo Cardano published the method in sixteenth-century Italy. It has a strange flaw. When \(D\) is negative the formula asks for the square root of a negative number, and that is exactly when the cubic has three different real roots.

Rafael Bombelli, later that century, calculated with those square roots anyway, by the ordinary rules. They cancelled and a real root came out. Quadratics never forced anyone to accept \(\sqrt{-1}\). The cubic did.

Watch one: \(x^3 = 15x + 4\)

Here \(\tfrac{p}{3} = 5\) and \(\tfrac{q}{2} = 2\), so \(D = 2^2 - 5^3 = -121\) and \(\sqrt{D} = 11i\), where \(i^2 = -1\). The formula gives

\[x = \sqrt[3]{2 + 11i} + \sqrt[3]{2 - 11i}.\]

Now cube \(2 + i\):

\[(2 + i)^3 = 8 + 12i + 6i^2 + i^3 = 8 + 12i - 6 - i = 2 + 11i.\]

So \(2 + i\) is a cube root of \(2 + 11i\), and in the same way \(2 - i\) is a cube root of \(2 - 11i\). Then \(x = (2 + i) + (2 - i) = 4\). The imaginary parts cancel. Check: \(4^3 = 64 = 15 \times 4 + 4\).

Your turn

  1. Use Cardano's formula to solve \(x^3 = 6x + 9\). Here \(D\) is positive and both cube roots are whole numbers. Check your answer in the equation.
  2. Write \(x^3 + 3x = 4\) as \(x^3 = px + q\) and apply the formula. Then show that \(\left(\tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2}\right)^3 = 2 + \sqrt5\), and use it to simplify your answer to a whole number.
  3. Show that \((3 + i)^3 = 18 + 26i\). Use this to solve \(x^3 = 30x + 36\) by the formula.
  4. The formula needs a cubic with no squared term. Substitute \(y = x + 2\) into \(y^3 - 6y^2 - 3y + 18 = 0\) and show that it becomes \(x^3 = 15x + 4\). You know \(x = 4\) is one root. Divide by \(x - 4\) to find the other two, and so give all three values of \(y\).

Stretch: find the cube root yourself

Solve \(x^3 = 6x + 4\) by the formula. You will need a cube root of \(2 + 2i\): try \(a + bi\) with \(a\) and \(b\) each 1 or \(-1\). Then find the other two roots of the cubic.

Answers

  1. \(\tfrac{p}{3} = 2\), \(\tfrac{q}{2} = \tfrac92\), so \(D = \tfrac{81}{4} - 8 = \tfrac{49}{4}\) and \(\sqrt{D} = \tfrac72\). Then \(x = \sqrt[3]{8} + \sqrt[3]{1} = 2 + 1 = 3\). Check: \(27 = 18 + 9\).
  2. \(x^3 = -3x + 4\), so \(\tfrac{p}{3} = -1\), \(\tfrac{q}{2} = 2\), \(D = 4 + 1 = 5\), and \(x = \sqrt[3]{2 + \sqrt5} + \sqrt[3]{2 - \sqrt5}\). Cubing, \(\left(\tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2}\right)^3 = \tfrac{1 + 3\sqrt5 + 15 + 5\sqrt5}{8} = 2 + \sqrt5\), and in the same way \(\left(\tfrac{1 - \sqrt5}{2}\right)^3 = 2 - \sqrt5\). So \(x = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} + \tfrac{1 - \sqrt5}{2} = 1\). Check: \(1 + 3 = 4\).
  3. \((3 + i)^3 = 27 + 27i + 9i^2 + i^3 = 27 + 27i - 9 - i = 18 + 26i\). For the cubic, \(\tfrac{p}{3} = 10\), \(\tfrac{q}{2} = 18\), \(D = 324 - 1000 = -676\), \(\sqrt{D} = 26i\). So \(x = \sqrt[3]{18 + 26i} + \sqrt[3]{18 - 26i} = (3 + i) + (3 - i) = 6\). Check: \(216 = 180 + 36\).
  4. \((x+2)^3 - 6(x+2)^2 - 3(x+2) + 18 = x^3 - 15x - 4\): the \(x^2\) terms are \(6x^2 - 6x^2 = 0\), the \(x\) terms \(12x - 24x - 3x = -15x\), the constants \(8 - 24 - 6 + 18 = -4\). Then \(x^3 - 15x - 4 = (x - 4)(x^2 + 4x + 1)\), so \(x = 4\) or \(x = -2 \pm \sqrt3\). Adding 2: \(y = 6\), \(\sqrt3\) or \(-\sqrt3\).

Stretch. \(\tfrac{p}{3} = 2\), \(\tfrac{q}{2} = 2\), \(D = 4 - 8 = -4\), \(\sqrt{D} = 2i\). Since \((-1 + i)^2 = -2i\), \((-1 + i)^3 = -2i(-1 + i) = 2 + 2i\). So \(x = (-1 + i) + (-1 - i) = -2\). Check: \(-8 = -12 + 4\). Then \(x^3 - 6x - 4 = (x + 2)(x^2 - 2x - 2)\), giving \(x = 1 \pm \sqrt3\). Again three real roots, reached through \(i\).
