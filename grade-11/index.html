∞ Grade 11

For curious 16 and 17 year olds. The grade 11 course with the reasons left in, ideas from infinite descent to max flow that the course never reaches, and where the math shows up in the world. Three tracks: Sharpen (school math, and why it works), Around the corner (new ideas), Connections (where it shows up). One sheet a night, ten to fifteen minutes, and a starred problem if you want more.

1Pick a sheetStart anywhere, or follow the first thirty nights. Sharpen sheets make school math make sense. Around the corner sheets are new ideas.
2Print itEach sheet prints on three pages: two for you, and the answers on their own page at the end. Work on paper; the thinking goes better.

Sharpen the grade 11 course, with the why

Radians, sine waves, the addition formulas, the sine and cosine rules, inverse functions, trigonometric equations, log scales, polynomial graphs, piecewise functions, resolving vectors, three unknowns, complex arithmetic, recursive sequences, random variables, the computed line of best fit and the margin of error.

Sharpen 1

Why radians

Measure an angle by the arc it cuts off and arc length is just radius times angle. Degrees are a habit; radians are what the circle itself uses.
Sharpen 2

Reading a sine wave

In y = a sin(b(x − c)) + d, each letter does one job: height, speed, slide, lift. Read all four off a graph, and write the equation of a wave you can see.
Sharpen 3

sin(A + B), from a picture

It is not sin A + sin B. One diagram of two stacked right triangles gives the true formula, and the double-angle formulas drop out.
Sharpen 4

Triangles without a right angle

The sine rule and the cosine rule solve any triangle. The cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction term, and you can see where the term comes from.
Sharpen 5

Undoing a function that is not one-to-one

sin 30° and sin 150° are both a half, so "the angle whose sine is a half" needs a choice. Restrict the domain, and see what the calculator chose for you.
Sharpen 6

All the solutions

sin x = 0.5 has infinitely many answers. Find the two in one turn from the unit circle, then add whole turns, and never lose a solution again.
Sharpen 7

Straight lines hiding in curved data

Plot the logarithm instead and exponential growth becomes a line; plot both logarithms and a power law does. The slope tells you the rate or the power.
Sharpen 8

Sketching a polynomial at sight

The leading term decides the ends, the roots decide the crossings, and a repeated root touches without crossing.
Sharpen 9

Functions in pieces

A tax schedule, a phone plan and |x| are all functions with different rules on different intervals. Graph them, compose them, and find where they join up.
Sharpen 10

Splitting an arrow in two

Any force is the sum of a horizontal part and a vertical part. Resolve, add the parts, and recombine: that is how every bridge and aeroplane is analysed.
Sharpen 11

Three equations, three unknowns

Eliminate one unknown at a time until the system is a staircase, then climb back up. The method scales to a million unknowns.
Sharpen 12

Dividing by a complex number

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate. Add, multiply and divide a + bi, and solve the quadratics that used to have no answer.
Sharpen 13

Sequences that feed on themselves

Each term is made from the one before. Some settle down, some run away, some cycle, and you can often predict the limit before computing a single term.
Sharpen 14

The average outcome and how far it strays

Expected value and variance of a random number, computed from a table. Why a fair bet can still be a bad idea.
Sharpen 15

The line of best fit, computed

Not by eye. The least-squares line passes through the point of means, and its slope is one formula. What r² tells you, and what it hides.
Sharpen 16

Plus or minus three points

A poll of 1,000 people reports 52% ± 3%. Where the 3 comes from, why it barely depends on the size of the country, and what it does not cover.

Around the corner new ideas

Infinite descent, the rearrangement inequality, modular division, decimal periods, the cubic formula, rational roots, linear recurrences, sums of powers, counting twice, necklaces, linearity of expectation, Buffon's needle, error-correcting bounds, counting trees, max flow, mixed strategies, the power of a point, complex geometry, the isoperimetric problem, Newton's method, Simpson's rule, the overhang puzzle, finite fields and fractal dimension.

Corner 1

Proof by infinite descent

If a solution exists, a smaller one does too, and whole numbers cannot shrink for ever. Fermat's favourite weapon, used on √2 and on a² + b² = 3c².
Corner 2

Big with big, small with small

To make a sum of products as large as possible, pair the largest with the largest. The rearrangement inequality, and three famous inequalities that fall out of it.
Corner 3

Dividing in clock arithmetic

You can divide by 3 on a 7-hour clock but not by 2 on a 6-hour one. When an inverse exists, how to find it, and why primes make everything work.
Corner 4

Why 1/7 repeats after six digits and 1/13 after six too

The length of a repeating decimal is the first power of 10 that leaves remainder 1. It always divides p − 1, and nobody can predict when it equals p − 1.
Corner 5

The cubic formula, and the numbers it forced into existence

Solve x³ = 15x + 4 by Cardano's method and the answer, 4, arrives wrapped in square roots of negative numbers. This is why complex numbers were invented.
Corner 6

Hunting for fraction roots

If a polynomial with whole-number coefficients has a root p/q, then p divides the last coefficient and q the first. A finite list to test, and a quick proof that √2 + √3 is irrational.
Corner 7

Guess rⁿ

To solve aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 6aₙ₋₂, try a power. A quadratic appears, its roots give the answer, and Fibonacci gets a formula.
Corner 8

Summing squares and cubes without guessing

Find a function whose differences are n², and the sum telescopes. A calculus of differences that works exactly like the one with derivatives.
Corner 9

Count it twice

Count the same thing in two ways and you have proved an identity. Committees with a chair, the hockey stick in Pascal's triangle, and Vandermonde's sum.
Corner 10

How many different necklaces?

Six beads, two colours, and rotations do not count as different. Average the number of colourings each rotation leaves alone, and the answer is exact.
Corner 11

Expectation adds, even when nothing is independent

The expected number of people who get their own hat back is exactly 1, for any number of people. One line of reasoning replaces pages of counting.
Corner 12

Dropping needles to find π

Drop a needle on a lined floor. If it is as long as the gap between lines, the chance it crosses one is 2/π, and a noodle of any shape shows why.
Corner 13

How many errors can a code fix?

Codewords must be far apart so that a damaged one is still closest to the truth. Counting how many fit gives a hard limit, and a few codes meet it exactly.
Corner 14

Counting trees

There are 16 ways to join four labelled towns into a tree, and nⁿ⁻² ways for n. A code that turns every tree into a list of numbers proves it.
Corner 15

The bottleneck theorem

The most traffic a network can carry equals the capacity of its narrowest cut. Push flow along paths until no path is left, and the cut reveals itself.
Corner 16

Where should the penalty taker aim?

If you always shoot left the keeper always dives left. The best plan is a fixed set of odds, found where two lines cross.
Corner 17

The power of a point

Draw any line through a point to cut a circle twice, and multiply the two distances. The product never changes. Similar triangles are why.
Corner 18

Geometry by multiplication

Multiplying by a complex number rotates and scales. Use it to rotate about any point, and to prove that equilateral triangles built on a triangle's sides have centres forming another.
Corner 19

Why bubbles are round

Of all shapes with the same perimeter, the circle encloses the most. Compare regular polygons, then follow an argument that any dent or lopsidedness can be improved.
Corner 20

Newton's method

Slide down the tangent to the axis, and repeat. Each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits, until a bad start sends it somewhere wild.
Corner 21

Areas from a table of values

Join the points with straight lines and you get the trapezium rule. Join them with parabolas and the error shrinks sixteen times faster.
Corner 22

How far can a stack of books lean?

One book can overhang half its length. Two can reach three quarters. With enough books the stack reaches as far as you like, because the harmonic series diverges.
Corner 23

A number system with five numbers

On a 5-hour clock you can add, subtract, multiply and divide. Lines and parabolas still make sense there, and a line still meets a parabola at most twice.
Corner 24

A shape of dimension 1.26

Double a line and you get 2 copies, a square 4, a cube 8. Triple the Koch curve and you get 4. Solve 3ᵈ = 4 and dimension stops being a whole number.

Connections tides · surveying · chemistry · chips · insurance · graphics · rainbows · power laws

The same math, found in the wild.

Connections 1

Predicting the tide

High water to low water and back in about twelve and a half hours. Fit a sine wave to a tide table and say when a boat can cross the bar.
Connections 2

Mapping a country with one measured line

Measure one baseline and two angles and the sine rule gives every other distance. Repeat, and triangles cover a continent.
Connections 3

What pH measures

Each step on the pH scale is ten times the acidity. A logarithm turns a range of a hundred trillion into the numbers 0 to 14.
Connections 4

Fifty years of doubling

Plot transistor counts on ordinary axes and you see nothing; on a log axis you see a straight line. Read the doubling time off the slope.
Connections 5

Why insurance works

You pay a little more than your expected loss and are glad to. Pooling many independent risks shrinks the uncertainty, and the square root explains by how much.
Connections 6

How a screen turns a cube

A 3D model is a list of points. Multiply by a rotation matrix, drop one coordinate, and you have the picture. Every game does this millions of times a second.
Connections 7

Why the rainbow is at 42 degrees

Light bends entering a raindrop, reflects, and bends again. Follow many rays and they pile up at one angle.
Connections 8

The few that are huge

City sizes, word frequencies and earthquake energies follow power laws: a straight line on log-log axes. Why the average of such data can mislead badly.
